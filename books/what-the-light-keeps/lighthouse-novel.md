# What the Light Keeps

## Chapter 1: Two and the Long Quiet

The boat that brought him out was called the *Catriona*, and her skipper did not speak to him once in nine miles.

Tobias Fenn sat on a fish box in the lee of the wheelhouse with his kitbag between his knees. He watched Eilean Fada sink behind them: the low green shoulder of it, the white crofts strung along the single road, the church with no steeple. Ahead, Sgeir Dubh came up out of the grey water like a knuckle, and the tower stood on it pale and narrow. It was the second week of October 1963. The wind was west-south-west, force four, backing. He noted it without meaning to, as he noted everything, the way another man might hum.

The skipper was a broad man of thirty-five or so, though the weather had worked on him until he might have passed for fifty. He had a face like a split stone and a cap pulled so low that only the jaw showed. Donald Iain MacAulay, the Board's letter had said: *will convey you and stores by arrangement*. At the harbour he had taken Tobias's kitbag and swung it aboard without a word, and Tobias had been grateful. He had come a long way from anywhere that required talking.

The girl at the bow talked enough for both of them. She was twenty or so, in a man's oilskin with the sleeves rolled, dark hair escaping a headscarf. She worked the lines as if she had been born with them in her hands. She told him the names of the rocks they passed, the Bodach and the Cailleach and the Teeth. She told him that the fishing had been poor since the summer, that the herring had gone off somewhere and nobody knew where, and that her name was Nessa.

"You'll have heard about Mr Crane," she said.

"I heard he was ill."

"He kept her thirty-five years. He's in the cottage at the end of the road now, past the church. His daughter looks after him." She coiled a rope and looked out at the tower. "He used to wave. Every time we came past at night. He'd put a hand lamp in the gallery window and swing it. Twice."

From the wheelhouse her father said something in Gaelic, short and flat. She stopped talking.

They came in under the rock on the rising tide. Sgeir Dubh had no harbour, only a landing stage cut into the eastern face, iron rungs set in the stone, and a derrick on the ledge above for the stores. A man was waiting there in Board uniform with his own bag already packed. This was Gillies, the relief keeper who had held the station alone for five weeks since Crane was carried off it. He caught the line and made fast. He looked at Tobias the way men look at the one who will take the watch from them, with a little pity and a great deal of relief.

The stores came up on the derrick: paraffin in drums, flour, tinned milk, potatoes, a crate of mantles packed in cotton wool, the mail. Donald Iain worked the boat's end of it in silence. When the last net of drums went up he straightened and looked at the tower for the first time, a long look, and then he turned his head and spat into the sea.

"Da," Nessa said.

He said nothing. He went back to the wheel.

Gillies went down the rungs and the *Catriona* stood off, and Tobias watched her go with his hand on the cold iron of the derrick. The girl raised an arm from the bow. He did not raise his own. He had not decided yet whether he would.

---

The station was Victorian and had been built to last. Its stone came from the island quarry and its iron from Glasgow, and the brass had been polished so long that the edges of things were soft. The tower was ninety-one feet, with a hundred and twelve steps and a window at every turn. At its foot stood the keepers' dwelling, built for three: a kitchen with a coal range, a store, an oil room, a bunk room with three iron beds of which two were now stripped to the springs. The Board had withdrawn the assistant keepers in the spring. There was a report under way, Gillies had said, a survey. *Economies.* He had said it the way men said *the weather.*

Gillies had left the logbook open on the kitchen table. The last entry was his, in a neat, unhappy hand: *Wind WSW 4. Vis good. Lit 17.42. All well.* Tobias sat down, uncapped his pen, and wrote beneath it: *Fenn, T., Principal Keeper, assumed charge 14.10 hrs.* He blotted it. The silence of the place came up around him like water filling a hold.

He climbed the tower at five.

The lamp room was a glass drum on top of the world. In the middle of it, taller than he was, stood the lens, a beehive of prisms in brass frames. The light caught in it and broke into a thousand clean edges, so that even unlit it seemed to hold a little of the sky. It sat on its mercury bath and turned on almost nothing; a child could have pushed it round with one finger. Below it, in an iron case, the clockwork waited. The weight hung in its tube down the centre of the tower, and the governor was a pair of brass flyballs that would spin and steady when the thing was running.

The burner was a Hood vapour burner, the same pattern he had been trained on at Granton. He laid out his tools on the cloth the way his instructor had taught him: the methylated spirits, the pricker, the spare mantle in its box, the matches, the rag. He pumped the paraffin tank to pressure and watched the needle climb. He filled the spirit cup and lit it, and the blue flame ran round the vaporiser tube. While it heated he wound the clockwork, forty turns of the crank. The weight rose in the dark of the tower with a sound like a well-rope. At 17.39 he opened the valve. The vapour hissed up into the mantle and caught, and the mantle went from grey ash to white. It brightened until he could not look at it, and the lens took that white and made it something else.

He set the lens turning. The panels came round. Each pair of bullseyes threw a beam across the sea, and there was a long dark between them. Two flashes, then the dark, and the cycle again every fifteen seconds. *Fl(2) W 15s*, as the Admiralty List had it. He stood with his watch in his hand and timed six revolutions until he was satisfied. Then he went out on the gallery and stood in the wind and watched the beams go out over the water toward Eilean Fada, toward nothing, toward America.

He did not sleep that night. He had not expected to.

---

He had not slept, properly, since 1944. People who did not know assumed he meant he slept badly. He meant that he lay down and closed his eyes and the hours passed. Sometimes there came a thin grey state like the surface of a held breath. Sometimes, near dawn, there was an hour of something. But there was no going down, never the deep, never the black water of real sleep. He no longer remembered what it was like. He remembered only that he had once had it and that it had been taken from him on a night in February in the Barents Sea, and that he deserved its loss.

On Sgeir Dubh, sleeplessness was at least useful. Rock keepers kept watches, four hours on and four off through the night, and a man alone was meant to snatch what rest he could between rounds. Tobias did not need to snatch. He sat in the lamp room through the dark with a mug of tea going cold on the sill. Every hour he wound the clockwork. He watched the pressure gauge and pumped when it dropped. He trimmed and cleaned. At dawn he put the light out at the time given in the book, drew the linen curtains round the lens to keep the sun off the prisms, and went down to log the weather.

*Wind W 5. Swell moderate from W. Vis 12 miles. Bar. 29.84 falling. Ext. 07.21. All well.*

Then the day's work: brass, glass, paint. He cleaned the lens with spirit and chamois, prism by prism, standing on the step-ladder inside the cage of light. He polished the burner. He swept the hundred and twelve steps. He took the hand pump to the rain tanks. He scraped rust from the gallery rail and painted it with red lead, and the wind took the smell of it out to sea. He ate standing up at the range, sausages from a tin, bread he had baked badly. In the afternoons he lay on the bunk with his eyes closed and let the grey shallows have him for an hour or two. Then it was dusk again, and the spirit cup, and the long blue flame.

It was a kind of peace. He would not have called it more than that. The rock was a mile round at low water and less at high, all black gneiss and weed and the white crust of the birds. Nobody came. When the wind blew he could not hear his own thoughts, and he was grateful for that too.

---

Nessa MacAulay came out every second Thursday with the mail and the perishables, in a clinker-built boat with an outboard that coughed. She came alone. He suspected her father did not know.

The first time, he stood at the top of the landing stage and took the boxes from her and said thank you. He would have let that be all, but she tied off and came up the rungs after him with the ease of a cat.

"I've never been up," she said. "Not inside. Mr Crane never let anyone."

"I'm sure he had his reasons."

"He said it was Board regulations." She looked at him sideways. "Is it?"

It was, more or less. He thought of saying so. Instead he put the kettle on, because she was standing in his kitchen and it would have been strange not to.

She talked as she had on the boat. She told him about the island, the forty-one souls left on it and how many had gone to Glasgow since the war, to Canada, to the whalers. She told him about the herring, how the drifters from Stornoway and the east coast had stripped the grounds bare in the fifties. Now there was only her father's boat and two others working out of Eilean Fada, and the other two were talking about selling. She asked where he was from, and he said Leith. She asked if he had been at sea, and he said the navy. She asked what he had done in the navy, and he said signals, and she waited for more, and when it did not come she let it go.

He found he did not mind her. That was what unsettled him.

She came again the very next Thursday, a week before she was due, with a sack of potatoes he had not ordered. That time she asked him about the light. He showed her the lamp room, which he should not have done, and she stood in the lens with her mouth open while the afternoon sun broke over her in pieces. She asked how it turned and he showed her the clockwork and the weight. She asked how often it needed winding, and how long the mantle lasted, and what happened if it broke.

"You fit another," he said. "There's spares."

"And while you're fitting it?"

"It's dark."

She put her hand flat on the glass of the lens, very gently, as if it were the flank of a sleeping animal. "How long?"

"Ten minutes. Less, if you're quick." He took her hand off the glass. "You'll leave marks."

On the way down she stopped on the forty-fifth step and looked out of the window. The sea below was the colour of slate and perfectly ordinary.

"My brother Calum drowned two winters ago," she said, without looking round. "Out there, past the Teeth. They never found him." She said it the way you set down something heavy that you will have to pick up again. "My father thinks it was the light. That it was wrong that night, or out. It wasn't. Mr Crane swore it wasn't. But Da doesn't listen to Mr Crane. He doesn't listen to anyone."

Tobias did not know what to say. He said, "I'm sorry."

"Aye," she said. "Everyone is."

---

The mantle failed on the night of the twenty-ninth of October, at 02.47.

He would remember the time exactly because he had just looked at his watch. The wind was north-west, force five, a clean cold wind with stars in it, and the swell was running long and easy from the north-west under it. He had wound the clockwork at 02.30 and pumped the tank. He was sitting on the stool with his back to the glass and the tea on the sill, and the beams were going round over his head, two and then the dark, two and then the dark. The rhythm had got into him over the weeks so that he no longer heard it, the way a man stops hearing his own heart.

There was a small sound like a struck match. The light went out.

He was on his feet before he understood. The mantle had split, and a flake of it, white ash, drifted down past the burner in the faint red glow of the cooling vaporiser. Then that glow died too, and there was nothing but the lens turning on in the starlight, empty, carrying darkness round and round.

He did it the way he had been trained. He shut the valve. He took the spare mantle from its box and burned off the coating with a match, his hands steady, the old discipline holding. He fitted it to the burner. The vaporiser had cooled too far to vaporise; he filled the spirit cup, lit it, and waited for the blue flame to bring the tube back up to heat. The waiting was the worst of it. Three minutes at the least, four to be safe. Open the valve early and the paraffin came through as liquid and flared, and you could crack the new mantle and have it all to do again.

He stood with his watch in his hand and waited, and while he waited the sea changed.

He did not notice it at first. Then he did. The sound of the swell against the rock had altered. All night it had come from the north-west, the same side as the wind, a long slow breathing on the western face. Now it was coming from the east. He went out onto the gallery with the watch still in his fist. The wind was in his face from the north-west, steady, cold, unchanged. Below him, the swell was rolling in against it from the south-east, from the direction of the land, heaping up against the wind. It made no sense. It was water running uphill. The waves stood up tall and dark and laced with white where the wind tore at their crests, and they kept coming in out of the wrong quarter of the night.

And on the water, in the starlight, there were rings.

He saw them first as pale marks, like foam. They were too regular for foam. They lay on the black sea all round the rock, a hundred yards out, two hundred, as far as he could see. There were dozens of them, each perhaps ten feet across, each a perfect circle, and each was made of fish. They were herring, he would learn at first light, when the gulls came down. The herring Donald Iain could not find were floating belly-up and silver, nose to tail, in rings as neat as if they had been laid out by hand. The rings rose and fell on the wrong swell and did not break.

He went back inside. The spirit flame was still burning. Two minutes and forty seconds. Not yet.

Then he heard the voices.

They came from below, up the well of the tower, up the hundred and twelve steps. At first he thought it was the wind in the windows. It was not the wind. It was men, talking low, the way men talk on a watch at night so as not to wake the ones sleeping. He could not make out words. He could make out tone: easy, ordinary, a little tired. Somewhere beneath them, he was sure of it, someone was laughing.

Then a single voice came up out of the rest, clear as if it stood on the step below the lamp room door.

"Bunts," it said. "Bunts. What's the word?"

He knew the voice. It belonged to Harry Lisle, leading seaman, from Gateshead, who had owed him four shillings and a packet of Woodbines on the night HMS *Corrigan* burned. *Bunts* was what they called signalmen. *What's the word* was what Harry said every time he passed the flag locker, every watch, every day, for eleven months, because it had made Tobias laugh once and Harry was the kind of man who kept a joke going for ever.

Tobias had last seen him in the water, his face lit orange.

"What's the word, Bunts?"

Tobias did not answer. His hands had started to shake, for the first time that night. He put the watch down on the sill. Three minutes forty. He reached for the valve.

"Bunts. They're waiting on you."

He opened the valve. The vapour hissed up into the new mantle. For a moment nothing happened, and the voice below him drew breath to speak again. Then the mantle caught and went white, and the light came back into the lens. The beams went out over the sea, two and then the dark.

The voice stopped mid-breath. All of them stopped.

He stood very still in the white light and listened, and heard only the wind and, after a while, the swell. It was coming from the north-west again, long and easy, the way it should. He looked at the watch. It read 02.58. Eleven minutes.

He wrote it in the log, because that was what you did. *02.47 mantle failed. 02.58 relit. Eleven minutes dark.* He did not write anything else. His pen hovered over the page for a long time before he capped it.

At 03.40, by the tide table pinned to the kitchen wall, the water at the landing stage should have been well down the ebb, near half-tide. He went down with a torch to look, because he could not stop himself. The water was at the fourth rung from the top: high water, springs, as high as it had been at 01.50. The weed hung lifted and floating, and the sea was full and black and still against the stone. It stayed there for twenty minutes, while he watched. Then it began, quietly, to go out again.

He wrote that down too. *03.40 HW at landing. Second HW. Not per tables.* He looked at the words in the lamplight and thought that a man who wrote such a thing was a man who should be relieved of his post, and that perhaps he should be. Perhaps that was what this was. Nineteen years of no sleep, and at last the mind had begun to fray at the edges, like the mantle, all at once.

At dawn the gulls came down onto the rings of fish and screamed and fought and ate. By eight o'clock there was nothing left on the water but a sheen of oil.

---

He went to Abel Crane two days later, on the Thursday, because he could not think of anything else to do.

He told Nessa he needed to see a doctor in Eilean Fada about his eyes. She looked at him, and he could see her not believing it, but she took him across. The station would be unlit and unwatched for the hours of daylight, which was permitted, just. She would bring him back before three. She did not talk on the crossing. Twice she started to, and stopped.

Crane's cottage was the last on the road, a white box with a tin roof weighted against the wind by a lattice of ropes and stones. The daughter, Ishbel, was a tired woman of forty with her father's long jaw. She let him in without a question. Perhaps she was used to Board men. Perhaps she had stopped asking questions a long time ago.

The old man was in the box bed by the fire. He had been big once; Tobias could see it in the frame of him. Now the skin was loose on the bones, yellowish, and the breath went in and out of him with a wet catch at the end. But the eyes were sharp, pale blue and watchful, and they found Tobias in the doorway and held him.

"You're Fenn," Crane said.

"Yes."

"Sit down. Ishbel, leave us."

She left. The fire ticked. Through the small window Tobias could see the sea, and at the edge of it, very small, the tower.

He had rehearsed what he would say. He could not say any of it. In the end he said only, "The mantle went. On Tuesday night. Eleven minutes."

Crane closed his eyes.

"I know," he said. "I saw." He moved one hand on the blanket, toward the window. "I watch her every night. Ishbel thinks I'm sleeping." He opened his eyes again. "Eleven minutes. That's a long while."

"It took that long to bring the vaporiser back."

"Aye. It does." Crane was silent. "And did you see anything, Mr Fenn, in your eleven minutes?"

So Tobias told him. He told him about the swell against the wind and the rings of fish and the tide coming twice. He did not tell him about the voices. He could not, even here. He told it flatly, the way he would have written it in a log. When he was done, Crane looked at him for a long time.

"There's no harm done," the old man said at last. "You relit her. That's all that matters."

"What is it?"

"It's the sea, Mr Fenn. Strange things happen on the sea. You were a navy man; you'll know that."

"The tide came in twice."

"The tide does what the tide does." Crane looked toward the window. "A surge, maybe. A wind further out. The water heaps up."

"Against the wind."

"It's a strange coast."

"There were voices," Tobias said, and heard his own voice crack on it, and hated it. "On the stairs. Men's voices."

Crane's hand closed on the blanket. For a moment something moved in his face, and it looked a great deal like fear. Then it was gone.

"You'll have been tired," he said. "Men hear things on a rock. I heard my mother, my first winter. Calling me in for my tea." He tried to smile. It was terrible to watch. "Keep her lit, Mr Fenn. Keep the time. That's all the job is. Two and the long quiet. Don't let her falter. That's all."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I've got."

Tobias stood up. He had come all this way, he had left the light, and the old man was lying to him. He knew it the way a signalman knows a hand on the lamp that is sending one thing and meaning another: by the hesitations, by what came too smoothly and what did not come at all. He knew the shape of a man holding a thing back.

He was at the door when Crane said, "Wait."

The old man was struggling up on one elbow. He pointed with his chin toward the press beside the bed. "Bottom shelf. Behind the blankets. Take them."

They were logbooks, seven of them, in black oilcloth covers. They were not the station's official logs, which went to Edinburgh, but the small kind a keeper might buy for himself in Stornoway. The oldest were swollen with damp. They were tied together with tarred twine.

"Ishbel's never seen them," Crane said. "Nobody has. They're for the keeper. They were always meant for the keeper." He lay back. His breath rattled. "Read them on the rock. Not here. And Mr Fenn..."

Tobias waited.

"Don't come and ask me about them." The pale eyes closed. "I'm tired. I'm very tired."

---

He read them that night in the lamp room, between windings, with the beams going round over his head.

At first they seemed to be nothing. Most of the entries were weather and routine, the same as his own: wind, swell, visibility, times lit and extinguished. But in the margins, in pencil, there was a second log running alongside the first. It was written in an abbreviated hand he could not at first make out, a mixture of English and Gaelic and something that might have been private shorthand. Some of it was only numbers.

*2/15. Held.*

*2/15. Held. Restless at 0300. Kept time.*

*Wick fault 0140, 40 sec. She turned over. Swell from E. Held after.*

He went back through the pages with the torch. Again and again the same notations. *2/15* was the light's character, two flashes every fifteen seconds. *Held* appeared nearly every night. *She* appeared often. Never a name, only *she*.

There was a word that recurred, underlined: *tàladh*.

He did not have the Gaelic. But there was a dictionary in the station library, left by some long-ago keeper, between the Admiralty Lists and a broken-backed *Treasure Island*. He went down and found it, and came back up, and looked.

*Tàladh*: a lullaby. A song sung to bring a child to sleep.

The oldest of the books was not Crane's at all. It had belonged to a keeper called Hector Macrae, and it began in 1863, the year the light was first exhibited. Macrae's hand was careful and old-fashioned, and he did not use code. Perhaps in his time there had been no one to hide it from.

*December 19th, 1863*, Macrae had written. *I have now been six weeks on Sgeir Dubh, and the light six weeks lit, and I am persuaded of what the island men told me, though I did not credit it when Mr Stevenson's surveyors were here and the men would not land on the rock, nor say why. There is a thing beneath the water off this rock which was awake before the light was put here. It has been sleeping since. I have tried the light at other characters, by stopping the machine and turning it by hand, and on the second night of trying it began to stir. The swell came contrary, and there were fish dead upon the water in circles, as I am told there were in the bad years before. I returned the light to its proper character and it quieted. Two flashes and the long dark. I have come to think of it as a song. I do not know how the engineers chose it. I do not think they knew what they chose.*

And further on, in a shakier hand, the ink browner:

*It is very large. I do not think it is evil, any more than the sea is evil, or hunger. But it is very large, and it is very hungry, and while it sleeps it does not eat. The keeper must keep the time. That is the whole of the duty. Whoever reads this after me, keep the time.*

Tobias sat with the book open on his knees and the beams going round over his head. His hands were cold.

He went back through Crane's books. Here and there, among the *held* and the *2/15*, there were other entries. One said only: *She knows the keeper's name, if he lets her.* Another: *Do not answer them on the stair.*

In the fourth book, in October of 1947, there were three pages cut out cleanly with a razor, close to the binding. Before them, the last entry read *Mary's anniversary. Calm.* After them, a week later, the hand changed. It was smaller, crabbed, pressed so hard the pencil had torn the paper. Crane had written nothing in the margins for a month but *held, held, held*, as if he were saying it to himself.

Tobias did not know who Mary was. He wrote the dates down on the back of an envelope and put it in his breast pocket. He did not know yet why.

At dawn he extinguished the light and drew the curtains round the lens, and he did not go down to the bunk room afterward. He sat in the lamp room with the books on his knees and watched the sea go grey and then silver and then ordinary blue. He thought about Harry Lisle, and four shillings, and *what's the word*, and the thing under the water listening for a name.

---

Margaret Hale arrived on the eleventh of November on the Board's tender, the *Pharos*, out of Oban. It was the first time the *Pharos* had come since he took the post. She brought nineteen crates of equipment and a letter from the Superintendent informing the principal keeper that Miss M. Hale, Assistant Engineer, was to be afforded every facility.

She was perhaps thirty-five, small, sharp-faced, in a navy duffel coat and slacks. She came up the iron rungs carrying her own case and refused his hand at the top. She had a Glasgow voice planed flat by education. She looked at everything on the station with the same quick, pricing attention: the derrick, the dwelling, the tower, him.

"You're Fenn," she said. "Good. I'll need the spare bunk room for the instruments, and power for the generator. It'll go in the oil store if there's space. I'll need the lamp room for the test fittings for three nights, starting Thursday. I'll be as little trouble as I can, and I'll be gone in a fortnight."

He helped the tender's crew bring the crates up on the derrick. In the largest, packed in straw and canvas, was the new lamp. It was an electric unit of a type he had heard of but never seen, a fat glass bulb in a steel housing on a turntable no bigger than a dinner plate. A small motor would drive it, and an automatic changer would swing a new bulb into place if the first failed. It would have no clockwork and need no pumping, no mantles and no spirit cup. It would need no keeper.

"Single white flash every ten seconds," she said, watching him look at it. "Flash, ten seconds' dark, flash. Standard character for the new automatic stations. We're bringing the whole western seaboard onto a common system. It's clearer to shipping, easier to maintain. In five years half the rock lights in Scotland will be run like this."

"And the old character?"

"Retired." She said it without emphasis, as one says a date. "There's a notice to mariners going out in the new year. Changeover's provisionally scheduled for the end of February."

He thought of Hector Macrae, stopping his machine and turning the lens by hand, and on the second night of trying, the swell coming contrary.

"You can't change the character," he said.

She looked at him. "I'm sorry?"

He did not know how to say it. He said it anyway, badly, at the kitchen table that night while the rain came in off the Atlantic. He told her about the eleven minutes, the swell against the wind, the rings of herring, the double tide. He said nothing of the logbooks. He said nothing of the voices. He kept his hands flat on the table so she would not see them move.

She listened with her head a little on one side. When he was done she was silent for a while. Then she asked how long he had been on the rock, and whether he had been alone the whole time, and how he was sleeping.

"I don't," he said, before he could stop himself.

She nodded slowly, as if a reading had confirmed a hypothesis.

"Mr Fenn," she said, and her voice was not unkind. "I've surveyed eleven stations this year. At four of them the keepers told me something like what you've just told me. At Muckle Flugga it was a woman on the gallery. At Bell Rock it was footsteps. These are lonely places. The sea does extraordinary things, and the mind does more extraordinary ones." She stood and took her plate to the sink. "A contrary swell is unusual but not impossible: a distant storm, a reflected wave off the shelf. Fish kills happen, deoxygenation, a plankton bloom. And tides..." She shrugged. "Tide tables are predictions. Seiches. Surges. I'll have the tide gauge running from tomorrow. If there's anything there, it'll show."

He went up to the light and did not argue. He did not have the words. He had not expected to need them.

---

She was efficient. He could not fault her. She set up her float-gauge in a stilling well on the landing stage, a drum of graph paper turning under a pen that traced the height of the water hour by hour. She bolted a seismograph to the bedrock in the oil store, a squat brass and steel thing with a smoked-paper drum and a stylus fine as a hair, to measure, she said, any vibration in the structure of the rock that might affect the new optic's alignment. She ran cables up the tower. She worked in the cold for fourteen hours a day with her sleeves rolled, ate what he put in front of her without complaint, and said little that was not about the work.

Once, passing her on the stair with a can of paraffin, he caught himself about to ask what she made of the glass, which had been falling all afternoon, as he might have asked a shipmate. He went on up without asking. He did not care to know what that meant.

The test fittings began on the Thursday, in a gale that kept every boat on Eilean Fada at its moorings. For two nights she did no more than measure: she sat on the lamp room floor with a spirit level and a notebook, sighted along the focal plane, and fitted the steel housing to a bracket she had brought out from Oban, while the old lens went round above her head. On the third night, the sixteenth, she asked for a live trial.

"Ninety seconds," she said. "I need to see the beam on the new character from the gallery before I can sign off the alignment. Your burner shut, the new lamp on, then yours back. That's all."

He told her no. She showed him the Superintendent's letter again, not unkindly, with her finger under *every facility*. He thought of Macrae turning the lens by hand. He thought that ninety seconds was not eleven minutes, and was not two nights, and that the Board paid him to keep a light and not a superstition.

At 00.55 he shut the valve. The mantle dimmed and died, and her bulb came up harsh and blue-white in its housing, and swung, and flashed once, and there was a long dark, and it flashed again. One, and the dark. One, and the dark. It was a clean light and a strong one, and it was wrong in his bones the way a misplayed note is wrong. He stood on the gallery and watched the water and saw nothing he could have put in a log. The wind held. The swell held. At 00.56 and a half she switched off, and he had the spirit cup already burning, and he brought the old light back in a little under four minutes more. The beams went out over the sea, two and then the dark, and he logged it: *00.55 extinguished for Board trial, new character. 01.00 relit.* Five minutes, not ninety seconds. He had known it would be. He had let her have it anyway.

At three in the morning she came up to the lamp room. He was winding the clockwork. She stood in the doorway with a strip of graph paper in one hand and her duffel coat over her pyjamas and said nothing until he had finished.

"Your gauge," he said, when she still did not speak.

She held out the paper. He took it. The tide line ran across it in a long gentle curve, low water, rising to high, falling away, as it should. Then at 01.10 the line turned and rose again, a second high water an hour and a half after the first, as high as the first. It held there for twenty minutes, and then fell.

"The float stuck," he said. He did not know why he said it, except that she had been kind.

"It didn't stick. I've had it apart." She took the paper back and looked at it as if it might have rearranged itself. "Ten minutes after the trial. Fifteen at the most." She said it as if she were reading it off the paper. "And there's this."

The second strip was from the seismograph. On the smoked paper, the stylus had scratched a fine white line, almost straight. But it was not straight. All along it, regular as stitching, were tiny tremors, a flutter of vibration that grew, steadily, across a span of a few seconds. Then, at intervals, it was cut almost flat. It grew again and was cut flat again. There were two flattenings close together, and then a long stretch in which the trembling built, and then two more.

He counted along the line with his fingernail. Two cuts, then a long space. Two cuts. Fifteen seconds, by her scale, from pair to pair.

Then, at the place she had marked in pencil *00.55*, the stitching changed. The trembling ran on and grew, and there were no pairs of cuts, only a faint single nick every ten seconds that did not quiet it at all. It climbed across the paper for the length of the trial and for the long minutes after, while he had stood waiting on the spirit cup, until the line was a ragged white band. Then the pairs came back, two and the long space, and the band narrowed and narrowed, and was stitching again.

"It's the lens," Margaret Hale said. Her voice was very level, the way his own went when he was frightened. "It has to be. The mechanism. Some vibration through the tower when the bullseyes come round. I've never seen anything like it, but it has to be that."

"The lens floats on mercury," he said. "It doesn't vibrate. You know that."

"Then it's the clockwork."

"The clockwork runs constant. It doesn't go in pairs. And it was turning the whole time your lamp was on."

She was silent. Overhead the beams went round. Two, and then the long dark.

"The tremors are from below," she said at last. "The signal's coming up through the bedrock, not down the tower. The source is under the rock. Directly under. And it's quiet when the light flashes, Mr Fenn. Every time. It builds in the dark, and when the beam goes over, it stops." She looked at the paper in his hand. "When it's your beam."

She folded the papers very carefully and put them in the pocket of her coat. Then she went down the stairs, and he heard her door close, and he did not see her until noon.

When she came to the kitchen at noon she said only, "I'll need to run the readings again. The equipment may be faulty." She did not look at him. She did not mention it again that day, or the next, and she did not ask for another trial.

---

Nessa should have come out on the fourteenth, but the gale had held the island boats at their moorings, and she did not wait for the twenty-eighth. She came on the twenty-first, a week out of her turn, and he met her on the landing stage, and she stood in her boat looking up at him without passing up the boxes.

"There's a woman," she said. "From the Board. The whole island knows."

"She's surveying the station."

"For the machine. Aye. Everyone knows that too." She threw him a line and he made it fast. She did not come up. She stayed in the boat with her hands on the gunwale, rising and falling on the swell, and her face was pale under the headscarf.

"The night your light went out," she said.

He went still.

"I saw it from the shore. I was down at the slip. I go down at night sometimes. I don't sleep much either." She almost smiled. "Everyone on Eilean Fada was in their beds, but I saw it. One minute it was there, two and the dark, and then it was only dark. I thought, he'll have it back in a minute. And then I heard..."

She stopped. The swell lifted the boat and set it down.

"The tide came in when it shouldn't have," she said. "Up over the slip, right up to my boots. And there were rings on the water, all white. And then there was singing."

"Nessa."

"Calum used to sing. When we were small. Our mother died when I was six, and he'd sing to me at night when I couldn't sleep, the old songs, the ones she sang." Her voice was thin now and very steady. "He was singing, Mr Fenn. Out on the water. Out past the slip where I couldn't see. He was singing the one about the seal-wife. I'd know his voice anywhere. I'd know it in my grave." She looked up at the tower, and then at him. "And then your light came back, and he stopped."

Tobias stood on the iron landing with the line in his hands and the wind at his back. He could not think of a single thing to say.

"I haven't told Da," Nessa said. "I haven't told anyone." She reached up, finally, and passed him the first box. Her hands were shaking. "I thought you should know. Since it was your light."

He took the box. Her fingers brushed his, and they were very cold.

"It sounded happy," she said. "That's the thing. He sounded happy."

She went back across the water to Eilean Fada with the outboard coughing. Tobias watched until she was a speck. Then he carried the boxes up to the kitchen one by one and set them on the table, and he did not open them. At dusk he climbed the tower and lit the spirit cup, and when the blue flame ran round the vaporiser he stood and watched it for a long time. Then he opened the valve, and the light came, and the lens began to turn. He stood with his watch in his hand and counted, though there was no need: two, and the long quiet. Two, and the long quiet.

Somewhere below him, in the oil store, the stylus scratched its fine white line across the smoked paper. It shivered in the dark and went still at every flash. Under the rock, under the black water, something very large turned over in its sleep and was quieted, and quieted, and quieted again.

He did not go down to the bunk that night, or the next. He sat on the stool with his back to the glass. He did not take his eyes off the burner, and he did not let himself listen to the stairs.

## Chapter 2: The Long Quiet

By December he had moved the chair.

It was the deal kitchen chair with the split seat, the one Gillies had left by the range with a cushion tied to it. Tobias carried it up the hundred and twelve steps one afternoon and set it in the lamp room. It went between the pedestal and the door to the gallery, facing the burner, close enough that he could put out a hand and touch the brass of the vaporiser without standing. He untied the cushion and threw it down the stair. A cushion was for a man who meant to sit a long time and be comfortable doing it.

On the lid of the spare-parts chest he laid out what he would need. There were four mantles in their tissue, the spirit cup already charged, a box of Swan Vestas, the long-nosed pliers, the pricker for the nipple, and a cloth folded in quarters. He set them in the order he would want them, like instruments on a tray. In the afternoons, while the light stood unlit, he drilled. He shut the burner, counted, stripped the dead mantle, fitted the new one, lit the cup, waited for the vaporiser to come to heat, opened the valve, and brought the light back. He did it with his watch propped on the chest. The first day it took him four minutes ten. By the end of the week he had it at two minutes fifty. He wrote the figures on the back of a tide table and pinned it to the wall where he could see it.

Two minutes fifty was still twelve pairs of flashes missed. He had worked it out. Twelve times two, and the long quiet between, and whatever was under the rock turning in its bed.

He did not go down to the dwelling at night. He had stopped doing that before Margaret left, and now he had stopped pretending he might. He ate in the lamp room, bread and cheese, tinned pilchards, tea from the flask. He pissed in a bucket and carried it down at dawn. He wound the clockwork on the hour, forty turns of the crank, and listened to the weight settle in its tube down the core of the tower. He watched the flyball governor spin up and steady. Between windings he watched the mantle. At night the mantle was the whole world: a small, fierce, white sock of light with the lens turning around it, so that the beams went out over the water and he sat in the still centre where there was no flash at all, only the glare.

He did not sleep. He did not try. The tiredness had passed through some wall in him in the first week of December. Now it was a kind of clarity, cold and thin, as if the air in his head had been pumped out. Sometimes the edges of things moved. The brass rail of the gallery crawled faintly if he looked at it too long. Once a gull went past the glass and he saw it twice, the second a half second behind the first, like a film run badly through the gate.

He kept the log. He was particular about the log.

*Dec 3. Wind WSW 6. Sea rough. Vis good. Light exhibited 15.51. All in order.*

*Dec 4. Wind W 7–8. Sea very rough. Light exhibited 15.50. All in order.*

He did not write that from about midnight on the fourth there had been a sound at the bottom of the stair, in the long quiet between flashes. It came through the thirteen seconds of dark that followed each pair of beams and stopped when the beams came round. It was not a voice. It was the sound of a crowd before it becomes a voice, the shifting of men in a mess deck before the tannoy speaks.

On the sixth it was a voice.

"Bunts."

It came up the stair like a draught. It was Harry's voice, the flat Gateshead *u*, *Boonts*. The flashes came round and it stopped. Thirteen seconds later, in the dark, it came again.

"Bunts. What's the word?"

He sat in the chair with his hands between his knees and looked at the mantle. *Do not answer them on the stair.* He had copied the line into the back of his own notebook in his own hand, as if that might make it his.

The beams came round. The dark came after.

"We're waiting on you, Bunts."

At four in the morning there was another voice, older, Scots. He thought it was Petty Officer Rennie, who had died in the boiler room and never come up. It said, "Fenn." Only that, the way a PO says a name at muster. *Fenn.* As if checking he was still on the books.

He did not answer. He wound the clockwork at five. The weight went up. The voices did not stop for the winding. They stopped at first light, when he shut down the burner and the lens slowed under his hand and the sea outside was only the sea, grey and heaving, with the gannets working off the Teeth.

---

Margaret Hale had gone on the twenty-fifth of November. The *Pharos* came for her and twelve of her nineteen crates. She left the bracket in the lamp room, the cables up the tower and the seismograph bolted to the floor of the oil store, with its drum turning slowly under a cover she had made from a biscuit tin. She asked him to change the paper on Mondays and post it to her in Edinburgh. He said he would.

On the landing, waiting for the boat to come in under the derrick, she had stood with her case at her feet and her hands in the pockets of her duffel coat. "I've written it as an instrument fault," she said.

"Aye."

"The stilling well was badly sited. The float could have stuck on the up-run and then freed itself. Wave action against the cable." She was not looking at him. "The seismograph is a borrowed unit. It's not been calibrated since August."

"You told me where it came from," he said. "Under the rock."

"I told you what the trace showed." She bent and picked up the case, though the boat was still a hundred yards off. "I'll be back in January with the production lamp. We'll run it in daylight. Long runs, on the proper turntable. If there's a fault in the character, that'll find it."

"And if there's no fault in it."

She gave him a look then. It was quick and sharp, and very tired at the bottom of it, as if she too had been lying awake. "Then the lamp works, Mr Fenn," she said, "and the Board will have its automatic light, and you'll be found a nice shore station with a garden." She went down the rungs without his hand.

He changed the paper on Mondays. He did not post it to her. The traces were in a drawer in the spare bunk room. Every night they showed the same thing: a low growl in the rock, cut flat every fifteen seconds and building a little in the quiet, then cut flat again. It was like the breathing of something under a blanket. He laid the strips side by side on the bunk once, by day, and saw that the growl was a little higher in December than in November. It was a little rougher, as a sleeper's breathing roughens towards waking.

---

Nessa came on the twelfth with the mail and a box of groceries from the shop at Fada. She brought the mantles in their carton from Oban, which came out on the steamer to the island and were sent on. She came in the clinker boat, alone, with the outboard coughing.

The wind had been dropping all morning. By the time she had unloaded it was rising again from the south-west, and the swell was getting up over the landing so that the boat lifted and fell four feet against the rungs. He stood at the top and watched her look at the sea and then at the sky.

"I'll not get back in that," she said.

He knew she was right. He knew too that he should tell her she must go now, at once, before it got worse. He also knew that if she went now and the outboard failed she would be on the Cailleach in twenty minutes.

"You'll need to haul her up," he said.

They took the boat up on the derrick together. She was a light boat, but even so it was hard work in the wind, and when it was done they stood in the lee of the oil store breathing, and she laughed at him. He did not know what was funny.

"You've a face like a ghost," she said. "Are you eating?"

"I'm eating."

"You're not sleeping."

"I'm on watch."

"Mr Crane slept," she said. "I asked him once. He said a keeper sleeps in his chair with one ear open, like a dog."

"Mr Crane had assistants, for most of it."

She looked at him. Then she went into the dwelling and set about the range as if it were her own house, and he went up the tower.

She came up at lighting-up time. He heard her on the stair and went cold for a moment, until he heard that the steps were real ones, booted and a little out of breath at the hundredth step. She stood in the doorway of the lamp room and looked at the chair and the bucket and the instruments laid out on the chest.

"Can I watch," she said, "or is it against regulations?"

"It's against regulations."

She came in anyway and stood by the pedestal while he lit the cup and the vaporiser came to heat. The burner caught with its small hollow roar. The mantle went from red to orange to white. He let in the clutch and the lens began to turn, slow, then up to speed, the governor spinning. Out across the water the two beams went round, and the long quiet after.

"Two," Nessa said softly. "And the long quiet."

"Who told you that?"

"You did. The first day." She was watching the mantle. "Show me what you do. If it fails."

He showed her. He did not know afterwards why he did it. Perhaps it was that she asked plainly and he was too tired to find a reason not to. Perhaps it was that it had been seven weeks since anyone had sat with him in the night. Perhaps, and this was the thought that came to him at three in the morning, it was that he wanted someone else to know how. He wanted it to be possible for the light to be kept if he went down. He showed her the mantles and how to lift the dead one off without touching the hot brass. He showed her the spirit cup, and how long to wait, and the valve. He showed her the clockwork too, the forty turns and the governor, and what happened if the weight reached the bottom of its tube: the lens slowed and stopped, and the light did not go out but stood fixed, one white glare in a single direction. That was a different character, as bad as dark. Worse, Crane's books said. He showed her the station tool chest by the door, and the old spares in the bottom of it, in case the ones on the tray were ever dropped or lost. He showed her the tide table on the wall with his drill times on the back.

"Two minutes fifty," she said. "That's fast."

"It's not fast enough."

She turned the paper over and looked at the tide figures, and then at him, and did not ask what he meant.

---

She stayed two nights. The wind went to a full gale on the first and the seas came over the landing and broke against the base of the tower so that he felt them in the soles of his feet. She slept in the spare bunk room among Margaret's instruments. At least she said she slept. Twice in the night he heard her on the stair and the second time she came up and sat on the floor with her back against the wall and her knees up, and they did not talk. He wound at two. She watched his hands on the crank.

On the second night, a little after half past twelve, he slept.

He did not know it was coming. He was in the chair with his eyes on the mantle and then there was a hand on his shoulder. It was a cold hand, through his jersey, and the cold went into the bone.

*Hold it, Fenn. Not yet.*

There was ice on the shutter handle of the lamp and his fingers were stuck to it. The convoy was dark ahead, and the ship ahead was dark, and the sea was black and full of noise. Somewhere out in it was the thing he had seen, the line of white in the black. *Not yet.*

He woke and the lamp room was dark.

It was not wholly dark. The vaporiser glowed dull red. But the mantle had split down one side and fallen in on itself, a grey ruin, and there was no light, and the lens was turning around nothing. He was on his feet before he knew he was awake. His hands went to the chest: pliers, dead mantle off, new mantle out of its tissue. They were shaking. He had to hold the mantle against his chest a moment to keep from tearing it. The cup, the match. The match would not strike. The second one did.

Under him the stair had filled. It was not the shifting of men now. It was voices, many of them, all at once, and above them, nearer, someone singing.

He did not hear the words. He heard the gallery door bang open and the wind come in, and he did not look round. The cup burned blue. He counted. He opened the valve too early and the burner coughed and spat and he shut it and counted again. Under him on the stair Harry Lisle said, quite close, almost kindly, "Bunts. Toby. Leave it."

The mantle caught. Red, orange, white. The beams went out. He stood with his hands on the rail of the pedestal and his head down and the stair went silent at once, as if a door had shut.

He looked at his watch. Seven minutes to one. Three minutes forty, from when he had woken. He had not kept the watch on it but he knew. How long the mantle had lain dead before that, while he slept in the chair with a cold hand on his shoulder, he did not know. He would never know.

When he turned round Nessa was in the gallery doorway with the wind tearing her hair. She was soaked with spray. Her face was not frightened. It was open, as if it had been unwrapped, and in the white glare of the beam swinging past behind her she looked younger than she was. She looked like a girl.

"He was singing," she said. "Tobias. He was right there. At the bottom of the rock, he was singing to me. He knew I was here."

"Come in and shut that door."

"He *knew* I was here."

He went past her and shut the door himself, and she let him, and stood dripping on the floor of the lamp room while he logged it.

*Dec 13. 00.49. Mantle failure. Relit 00.53. Cause: fault in mantle.*

He did not know that it had failed at 00.49. He wrote it because four minutes was a figure a keeper could put his name to. He did not write that the keeper had been asleep. He did not write that he had heard a man say *Toby*, which nobody on the *Corrigan* had ever called him. Nobody anywhere had called him that except his sister when they were small, and she was in Australia now and wrote at Christmas.

---

Afterwards she would not go below. She sat on the floor with a blanket round her and talked, in a low steady voice, as if she were afraid that if she stopped she would forget. Calum had sung the song about the seal-wife. It was the same song as on the night in October, but clearer, all the words. He had sung the verse their mother used to leave out because it was sad, the one where the seal-wife's children stand on the shore. She had not heard that verse since she was six.

"He was happy," she said. "He sounded happy. Wherever he is."

"You don't know where he is."

"I know he's in the sea." She looked up at him. "And the sea's not cruel to him. You can hear it. If it were cruel he'd not sing like that."

He wanted to tell her about Hector Macrae's book. *Very large. Very hungry. While it sleeps it does not eat.* He wanted to tell her that the voice on the stair had called him by a name it had no business knowing. He did not tell her. He told himself it was because she would not believe him. That was not it. It was that the books were the keeper's, and the keeping was his, and if he gave any of it away he did not know what he would have left.

"What did you hear?" she said.

"The wind."

She was quiet for a long while after that. Then she said, "You're a liar, Tobias Fenn," but gently. She put her head back against the wall and closed her eyes. For a time he thought she might be sleeping. He sat in the chair and watched the new mantle and felt, with something like grief, how much he wanted to close his own eyes, and how impossible it was.

In the morning the sea had gone down. He helped her get the boat off and watched her go, the outboard coughing, until she was a speck against the low brown line of Fada.

---

On the Thursday before Christmas he went over to see Crane.

He went by day, on Nessa's boat, and was back before dusk. The light was not lit by day. It was permitted. It had always been permitted. Still, all the way across he sat in the stern with his back to the island and his face to the tower. It looked very small from the water, a white peg on a black rock, and he could not shake the thought that while he was not in it, it was not being kept.

Crane was worse. Ishbel met them at the door with a towel over her arm and said he had been wandering. He had been talking to people who were not in the room, and he did not always know her. She looked at Tobias with her father's long jaw set hard. "If you upset him," she said, "you'll go."

Crane was propped in the box bed with the curtain half drawn. His face had fallen in. His hands lay on the blanket like two pieces of driftwood. But his eyes, when they found Tobias, were clear enough.

"You let it go dark," he said. "Last week."

"The mantle split."

"Six minutes. Near enough seven."

"Four," Tobias said. "It's in the log."

Crane looked at him a long moment. "Aye," he said. "It would be." He shut his eyes. "I saw it. From here. I sit up for it. Ishbel thinks I'm daft." He breathed for a while. "She'll have turned over."

"There were voices on the stair," Tobias said. "Before. With the light lit. In the quiet."

Crane's eyes opened. "Did you answer?"

"No."

"Good man."

"They called me by name. One of them called me by a name nobody uses."

"Aye," Crane said. He was looking at the wall above Tobias's head. "Aye, she does that. She listens. She's very patient. She has all the time there is."

"Who's Mary?"

The old man did not move. But something went out of his face, a kind of light, and his mouth worked. Ishbel, in the doorway, said sharply, "That was my mother."

Tobias turned. She was standing with the towel in both hands.

"She was lost from the island boat in 'thirty-eight," Ishbel said. "Coming back from the Lochmaddy sale. In a squall. They never found her." She came and stood by the bed, between Tobias and her father. "You'll go now, Mr Fenn."

At the door he looked back. Crane had turned his face to the wall.

---

Nessa walked him down to the slip. It was already getting dark; it got dark at half past three now. Across the water the tower was a faint white mark. He kept looking at his watch.

On the road past the church a man was standing at the gate of the last croft but two, smoking. It was Donald Iain. He had a hand-written card on the gate. It said, in capitals, *CATRIONA. 34 FT. KELVIN ENGINE. FOR SALE. ENQ. WITHIN.*

Nessa stopped.

"When did you put that up?"

Donald Iain looked at her. Then he looked past her at Tobias, and his eyes went flat.

"There's a man from Mallaig coming on the Tuesday boat," he said. His voice was deep and quiet and very hoarse, as if it were not much used. "For the drift nets and the engine. The hull he'll take or leave."

"And where will we go?"

"Glasgow. Your aunt Peigi has room."

"I'll not go to Glasgow."

"You'll go where I go." He threw down the cigarette and put his heel on it. "There's nothing in the water now. Nothing. Four crans last week from the Barra grounds. Four." He looked at Tobias. "Your light can shine on an empty sea, keeper. Like it did for my boy."

"Don't," Nessa said.

"He went past the Teeth with your light at his back." Donald Iain was still looking at Tobias, not at her. "And Crane swore to me on his Bible it was lit. Lit and turning. Maybe it was. Maybe it was. And still the sea took him." He turned and went in and shut the door.

Nessa stood a long time looking at the card on the gate. Then she pulled it off and tore it in half and in half again, and put the pieces in her pocket.

"He'll only write another," she said.

"Aye."

"He won't say Calum's name. He's not said it since the funeral with no coffin. He calls him *my boy*. *Your brother*. *Him*." She started down the road again, fast. "I hear him singing and I can't tell my own father."

On the crossing she did not speak at all. When they reached the rock, she held the boat against the rungs while he climbed. He looked down at her from the top and she looked up at him, and her face in the dusk was set and private and he could not read it.

---

He lit up at three fifty-one. He did not sleep.

The voices came at midnight.

They came every night now, in the quiet between flashes, and the light did not stop them. It only cut them off every fifteen seconds, the way it cut the growl on Margaret's seismograph. They spoke in short broken sentences, a word or two before each pair of beams came round. It was like listening to a wireless in a storm. Harry. Rennie. A boy called Pinder from the after gun, whom he had hardly known. They said the things men say on watch. *Cold one. Any word. Kye's up.* And they said his name.

*Fenn.*

*Bunts.*

*Toby.*

*Tobias.*

It was the last that frightened him. *Tobias* was what was on his papers. It was what the woman at the Board office in Edinburgh had called him, and what Nessa called him, and he understood after a week of it that the thing at the bottom of the stair was learning him. It was not Harry Lisle. Harry was nineteen years drowned under the ice of the Barents Sea. It was something that had taken Harry's voice the way you might pick up a shell on a beach. Now it was holding the shell to Tobias's ear to see what sound he made.

*She knows the keeper's name, if he lets her.*

He did not let her. He sat in the chair and did not answer.

But he remembered.

He did not want to and he could not stop it. The voices brought it up the stair with them, in pieces, the way the sea brings up wrack. There was the cold of the bridge wing, a cold that came through the duffel and the jerseys and the long johns and sat in the marrow. There was the shaded Aldis in his hands, the shutter handle furred with ice. The convoy was blacked out in its columns. The *Corrigan* was on the starboard wing. Out on the beam, a mile off, was the dim shape of the commodore's ship he was meant to be speaking to. And low in the water, catching some light from somewhere, was the line. White on black. Moving.

He had seen it. He knew he had seen it. He had seen it and his hand had been on the shutter, and then something, someone, *not yet*, and then—

The beams came round. He was in the lamp room on Sgeir Dubh. The mantle was white and steady. His hands were gripping the edge of the chair so hard that the split in the seat had opened and pinched the skin of his palm.

He had always known it was his fault. That was the only thing he had ever been sure of. The *not yet* was his own voice. It was his own fear, his own slowness, his own frozen hand, and the men in the water had paid for it. Harry Lisle with his face lit orange had paid for it. He did not sleep because Harry did not sleep. That was all. That was fair. It was the only fair thing in the world.

---

At Hogmanay Nessa came out with a half-bottle of Grouse in the pocket of her oilskin and a black bun her aunt had sent from Stornoway. She stayed only the afternoon. They sat in the kitchen and drank a dram each out of the station's tin mugs, and she told him the man from Mallaig had come and gone. He had offered two hundred and eighty pounds for the gear and engine. Her father had said he would think on it.

"He wants five hundred," she said. "He'll not get it. Nobody wants herring boats. Nobody wants herring. There's two drifters laid up at Castlebay and the owners can't give them away."

"And then Glasgow."

"And then Glasgow." She turned the mug in her hands. "Do you know what he said to me last night? I'd been out to the slip, and he was waiting up. He said the sea's gone sour. He said it's never been the same since he was a boy. The fish move wrong, they go deep when they should rise, the tides come strange. He said his father said the same before him, that there was a sourness in the water off Sgeir Dubh since before the war. Since before the last war." She looked at Tobias. "He said, 'Something's eating them, Nessa.'"

He said nothing.

"I said, 'Maybe it's just tired of being fished.' And he looked at me as if I'd hit him." She drank. "It's not a bad thing, Tobias, the sea waking. Don't look at me like that. All my life the sea's been a thing that takes and takes and gives nothing back. And now it's giving him back. A wee bit. A verse of a song." Her voice had gone thick. "How is that bad?"

He thought of Hector Macrae's hand in the oldest book, plain and slanting, without code. *While it sleeps it does not eat.*

"You don't know what's singing," he said.

"I know my brother's voice."

"So do I. I mean, I know—" He stopped. "I knew a man's voice. On the ship. I know how he spoke. And I've heard it on the stair."

She went very still.

"And it's not him," Tobias said. "It's got his voice, but it's not him. It calls me things he'd never have called me."

"You said it was the wind."

"I lied."

She put the mug down. She was looking at him with an expression he had not seen on her before, something careful and measuring.

"What does it say," she said, "your man on the stair?"

"It doesn't matter."

"It matters to me."

"It's the keeper's business," he said, and heard how it sounded, Crane's words in his mouth.

She sat a moment longer. Then she pulled on her oilskin and said, "Happy New Year, Tobias," and went down to the boat. He did not go out to help her with the derrick. He watched from the kitchen window as she managed it on her own, and was ashamed, and did not go.

*Jan 2. Wind NW 4. Sea moderate. Vis good. Light exhibited 15.58. 22.17 mantle failure. Relit 22.20. Cause: fault in mantle.*

*Jan 9. Wind W 5–6. Sea rough. Vis moderate, showers. Light exhibited 16.06. 01.32 mantle failure. Relit 01.35. Cause: fault in mantle.*

He had never in his time at Granton seen mantles fail like this. He wrote to the Board stores at Oban and said the last two cartons were defective and would they send from a different batch. He kept the dead mantles in a tin and looked at them by daylight, but there was nothing to see. They were ash, grey and crumbled. They told him nothing.

He did not sleep through either failure. He was in the chair, awake. He saw the mantle split. The first time he had the new one on and the cup lit inside forty seconds, and the burner back in two minutes thirty-five, the best he had ever done. Still it was too long. Under him the stair was full, and Harry said *Tobias* in a voice like a man calling across a dark field. The second time, at half past one on a black squally night, he heard the singing. It was far off. It was not coming from the stair but from outside, carried on the wind through the gallery door, which he had not opened. It was a young man's voice, light and true, singing in Gaelic, and the only word he caught was *Nessa*.

When the light came back the singing did not stop at once. It went on a second or two after the beams went out, and broke off in the middle of a word.

Afterwards he went out on the gallery. The sea round the rock was strange, a long glassy heave from the east against a westerly wind, and on the face of it, faint in the swinging beam, were the rings. One ring, out towards Fada, wider than any he had seen before, pale with turned-up bellies. He watched it a long time. In the morning the gulls had it.

Margaret came back on the thirteenth of January with the production lamp in a crate the size of a coffin, and four Board artificers who stayed two days and drank all his tea. They mounted the lamp on the bracket in the lamp room. It was a squat drum of grey steel with a lens no bigger than a dinner plate, on a turntable driven by a small electric motor in a sealed housing. The bulb changer was a revolving cage that held six lamps. When one filament failed, a relay dropped the next into place, with a click like a rifle bolt. Cables ran down the tower to the generator in the oil store and to a bank of batteries. A clock, its face sealed under glass, would switch the whole thing on at dusk and off at dawn without a hand touching it.

"It'll run six months without a visit," Margaret said. "Twelve, with the new bulbs."

She looked thinner than in November. She had a cold and her nose was red, and she walked round the lamp room with her notebook. When she saw the chair and the bucket and the instruments laid out on the chest she stopped, but she said nothing.

They ran it in daylight, as she had said. The old lens was stopped and the burner cold, and the new lamp turned on its bracket beside it, throwing its single white flash every ten seconds into the grey afternoon. Margaret sat on an upturned box with a stopwatch and a clipboard and timed it.

The first afternoon it ran clean. Ten seconds, ten seconds, ten seconds. She wrote the figures in columns and showed him and he nodded.

The second afternoon, at ten past three, the flash drifted.

He saw it before she did, because he was not looking at the lamp but at the sea. Margaret was looking at her stopwatch. "Nine point eight," she said. "Nine point seven." Then, more slowly, "Nine point five."

The motor was rated at a constant speed. He had read the specifications; she had made him read them. It was a synchronous motor locked to the frequency of the generator, and it could no more change its speed than a clock could choose its hours. He watched the turntable. It was slowing and quickening. It was not steady about it. It went in a pattern, a hesitation and a quickening, a hesitation and a quickening.

"Twelve point one," Margaret said. "Two point nine. Twelve point—"

She stopped.

The flash had become two. It was not two beams, because the new lens had only one panel. It was one beam that came round, stuttered, and came round again almost at once, as if the turntable had flinched and caught itself. Then there was a long pause. Then the two again.

Margaret sat on the box with the stopwatch running in her hand and did not click it.

"Fifteen," Tobias said. "That'll be fifteen."

She went down to the oil store without a word. He heard her on the stair, fast, and then the generator note changed, and the new lamp stopped. When she came back up she had the frequency meter from the generator panel in her hand, and she held it out to him. The needle sat on fifty cycles, steady as a stone.

"I didn't touch anything," he said.

"I know you didn't." Her voice was very calm. "I was watching you."

That night, with the old light lit and turning over their heads, she sat in the lamp room with him for the first time. She had brought down a blanket from the bunk room and sat on the floor by the wall where Nessa had sat, and she had her notebook open on her knees, but she did not write in it.

"I told Edinburgh it was a fault in the generator," she said eventually.

"Was it?"

"No."

The beams went round. The long quiet. At the bottom of the stair someone said, very softly, *Bunts*, and he saw her head come up.

"You heard that," he said.

She did not answer. She was staring at the doorway to the stair. Her face in the glare of the mantle had gone the colour of paper.

"Don't answer it," he said. "Whatever it says."

The beams came round. The dark. Thirteen seconds.

*Margaret.*

It was a woman's voice, a Glasgow voice, older than hers and rougher. Margaret made a small sound in her throat. She put her hand over her mouth.

The beams came round, and the voice stopped, and did not come again that night. But she did not go down to her bunk. She sat against the wall till dawn with her hand over her mouth, and in the grey of the morning, when he was shutting down the burner, she said, "My mother. She died in 'fifty-one. In the Southern General. I was in Edinburgh doing my finals, and they telegrammed, and I didn't go because of the exams."

He wound the cloth over his hand and began to wipe down the brass.

"I know how that sounds," she said.

"No," he said. "It sounds like the sea's been listening."

On the third afternoon of the trials the bulb changer ran through all six lamps in eleven minutes. Click, click, click. There was a pause between each, as if the cage were testing them one after another and finding each one wanting. When she took them out of the cage the filaments were whole. She held one up to the window. The glass was clear, the coil was bright, there was nothing wrong with it. But on the inside of the envelope, inside the sealed vacuum where nothing could get in, there was a faint white bloom, like salt left by a drying wave.

She put the bulbs back in their box and wrote the box number in her notebook and did not test them.

On the fourth afternoon the turntable ran backwards for ninety seconds and then forwards again.

On the fifth afternoon she did not run the lamp at all. She sat at the kitchen table in the dwelling with a pile of telegram forms and a pencil, and wrote and tore up and wrote again. When he came down to light up she was gone. He found her on the landing, standing at the top of the rungs looking at the stilling well she had built into the rock in November. The float was riding high. It was half past three on a falling tide.

"It's eight inches up on the tables," she said without turning. "Since noon. There's no wind to account for it."

"Aye."

"It's not a fault, is it."

"No."

She turned round. Her eyes were red, and not from her cold.

"The Board wrote," she said. "On the boat with the artificers. They asked if I wanted a delay. With the trials and the generator report. They'd stand it over to the autumn if I said so."

"And?"

"I've telegrammed to say we're on schedule. Twenty-ninth of February. The notice to mariners has gone out already. It went out in the second week of January. The new character. Every ship's master between Barra Head and Cape Wrath has it on his chart table." She laughed, a short hard sound. "Do you know how many women are assistant engineers with the Northern Lighthouse Board? One. I'm the first. There's a man at Granton who calls me *the Girl*. In meetings. To my face. If I go to Edinburgh and tell them that their lamp changes its own character, that it learns the old rhythm, that the bulbs fill up with sea salt inside a vacuum—" She stopped. "They'll not stand it over to the autumn. They'll stand *me* over. They'll put a man on it. A man from Granton who'll write that the girl got hysterical on a lonely rock and the keeper filled her head with stories. And they'll put the new lamp in anyway."

"So you'll put it in yourself."

"So I'll put it in myself and make it work." Her voice cracked. "I'll *make* it work, Mr Fenn. There has to be a reason. There's a reason for everything. There's a frequency, a resonance in the rock, some feedback I'm not seeing. I'll find it. I'll shield the motor and I'll isolate the bracket and I'll—"

"There's a reason," he said. "It's not one you can shield."

She looked at him a long time. "Show me," she said. "Whatever you've got. The old keeper's books. Gillies said there were books. Show me."

He thought of Hector Macrae's plain slanting hand. He thought of the razored pages. He thought of her face against the lamp room wall with her hand over her mouth.

"There's nothing to show you," he said.

She went past him up to the dwelling and he heard the door shut. He went up the tower and lit the light. He sat in the chair and listened to the stair, and he did not sleep, and at the bottom of the stair a voice that had learned his name said it over and over, gently, in the dark between the beams.

*Jan 16. Wind SW 5. Sea moderate. Vis good. Light exhibited 16.14. 23.08 mantle failure. Relit 23.11. Cause: fault in mantle.*

*Jan 20. Wind W 6–7. Sea rough. Vis poor in sleet. Light exhibited 16.20. 02.44 mantle failure. Relit 02.47. Cause: fault in mantle.*

Margaret slept through the first. Through the second she sat against the lamp room wall in her blanket, and watched his hands on the pliers and the match, and did not ask him anything. In the morning he wrote to Oban again and underlined *a different batch* twice.

The mantles from the new batch came on the twenty-third, with Nessa and the mail.

He had not seen her since Hogmanay. She had come on the ninth with the mail and stayed in the boat and handed it up to him, and he had not asked her to come up and she had not asked to. Now she climbed the rungs and stood on the landing in her oilskin and said, "You're thinner. You look like Mr Crane did at the end."

"He's not at the end."

"He is now." She held out a letter. It was in Ishbel's hand, short and stiff. *My father is asking for you. He will not say why. He has not long. I. Crane.*

He went over the next morning.

The cottage was dark and smelt of paraffin and sickness and peat. Ishbel let him in and went out to the byre without a word, and he heard her through the wall, talking low to the cow, as if to give them the house. Crane lay in the box bed with the curtains open. He was so small now that the blanket hardly lifted over him. His breath came slow and wet. When Tobias sat on the stool by the bed the old man's eyes moved and found him.

"Four times," Crane said. "Since Christmas. I've counted."

"The mantles are failing."

"Mantles don't fail like that." The eyes stayed on him. "I had mantles fail like that. Once. Before the war." A long wet breath. "Ishbel had a pin in her sewing box."

Tobias said nothing.

"Somebody's been at them," Crane said. "Somebody wants to hear."

"There's nobody on the rock but me and the Board woman."

"And who brings the mail?"

The question sat there between them. After a while Tobias said, "Tell me about Mary."

Crane closed his eyes.

He thought the old man had gone to sleep. Or that he would turn his face to the wall again, as before. But after a long time Crane said, "She was a MacInnes from Fada. Bonnier than Ishbel, and sharper. She sang. Not well. She sang all the time, in the kitchen, at the washing. I was always telling her to whisht." His mouth moved. "She went over to Lochmaddy for the sale in the autumn of 'thirty-eight, on the island boat, with two calves and Morrison's wife. And a squall came off Uist. The boat came home. Mary didn't."

"I'm sorry."

"I didn't hear her sing for nine years." The eyes opened. They were wet. "I had the books. Macrae's book. I knew. I'd always known what the light was. I'd sat in that chair twenty years and kept the time and never once wanted to know what was on the stair. And then it was 'forty-seven, and the anniversary, and I thought. I only thought. Five minutes. What's five minutes. The sea's been asleep a hundred years. Five minutes won't wake it. It'll only turn over. I'll hear her. Once. And I'll light it again."

The cow shifted in the byre. Ishbel's voice went on low through the wall.

"I shut the burner at ten to one," Crane said. "And I went out on the gallery. And I waited. And she sang." His voice broke. "She sang 'Fear a' Bhàta.' Under the rock, right under me. And I couldn't light it. I couldn't go back in. I stood on the gallery and listened to her and I thought, one more verse. One more. I stood there forty-one minutes."

The fire ticked in the grate.

"The *Kestrel* was coming home from the Barra grounds," Crane said. "She'd been out four days. Murdo MacAulay was skipper. Donald Iain's big brother. And Calum Morrison and his boy Kenny. Ruairidh Macleod. Seonaidh MacInnes, that was Mary's cousin. And Peter Gillies's father, Angus. Six. They'd have been steering for the light to clear the Teeth. And there was no light." He breathed. "I heard them. When she stopped singing. I heard them come up past the rock. The sea stood up out of the east. Out of a flat calm. A wall of it. I saw their masthead light go up and over. Then I went in and lit the burner. And she went down quiet again. Satisfied."

He was crying now, without sound, the tears running sideways into the pillow.

"The Board wrote it as weather," he said. "There was an inquiry and I said the light was lit. The assistant was asleep below. I said it was lit and turning and nobody could prove different, and the island believed me. Mostly. Murdo's mother never did. She'd come and stand at the slip every year on the anniversary and look at the tower. Till she died." He swallowed. "I cut the pages out. I'd written it down. I couldn't burn the book. I cut the pages and burned them."

Tobias sat on the stool and looked at the fire.

"And Calum MacAulay," he said.

Crane's face twisted.

"December of 'sixty-one," Tobias said. "Your book. *Wick fault 0140, 40 sec. She turned over. Swell from E.* Nessa said he went past the Teeth. Two winters ago. December. That's the night."

"I was seventy-eight," Crane said. "They'd cut the station to one assistant the spring before, to save the Board a wage, and the one they left me was away on his shore leave. I was keeping it on my own. I was sitting in the chair. I was so tired. I'd been tired for twenty years." His hand moved on the blanket, feebly, towards Tobias. "Forty seconds. I woke and the wick had gone and I had it back in forty seconds. It was nothing. Forty seconds. But she'd never been quite settled, not since 'forty-seven. She was always just under, always just turning. Forty seconds was enough."

"Donald Iain asked you. And you swore."

"On my Bible. I swore." The hand found Tobias's sleeve and held it. It was very cold. "I couldn't tell him. I'd have to tell him the rest. Murdo. I couldn't."

"He thinks the light killed his boy."

"It did," Crane said. "I did. Twice over. And now you're sitting in my chair, son, and somebody's been at your mantles, and you've not slept since Christmas. And you'll do what I did. You'll keep it all inside you, because it's yours. You'll think nobody else can carry it. And one night you'll be so tired—"

"I'll not sleep."

"You'll sleep," Crane said. "Everybody sleeps."

His grip went slack. His eyes closed. For a moment Tobias thought he had died there with his hand on his sleeve, but the wet breath came, and went, and came.

"Don't tell Ishbel," Crane whispered. "Please. She thinks I was a good keeper."

He crossed back alone. Nessa had brought him over and she waited at the slip, but he told her he would take the boat himself. She looked at his face and gave him the boat without arguing and stood on the slip with her arms round herself while he started the outboard on the fourth pull.

He was back by half past two. He went straight to the lamp room. He took the carton of new mantles from the chest, the ones she had carried out the day before, and opened it on the floor by the window.

He held each one up to the light, one at a time.

The fourth had it. He would never have seen it in lamplight. In the low clean winter sun through the storm glass it was there: a hole in the weave, no wider than a pin, near the top where the mantle would take the most heat. And another below it. And another, a neat line of them. Somebody had taken the needle in steady fingers and placed each one where it would open the fabric after an hour, or two, or three.

He went through the rest of the carton. Seven out of twelve.

He sat on the floor with the mantles in his lap. Then he went down to the spare bunk room, where Margaret was working at her bench with her soldering iron, and asked her whether she had a lens.

She gave him a jeweller's loupe from her toolbox without a question. He took it back up the tower. In the tin where he had kept the dead mantles, among the ash, there was a scrap of the January failures that had not quite burned, a crumb of the top of a mantle. Through the loupe he could see the hole in it, round and clean.

Nessa came on the Thursday as she always did, with the mail and a box of messages from the shop. He met her at the top of the rungs. He did not reach for the box.

"You'll not need to come out again," he said.

She stopped on the third rung with the box in her arms and the sea sucking at the boat below her.

"The Board tender'll bring the mail from now," he said. "I've written to Oban. Mrs MacAulay at the shop can put it on with the oil."

"Tobias."

"I've had the mantles under a glass."

She stood very still on the rung. The wind pulled at her hair.

"Seven of twelve," he said. "You did them well. You did them where the heat goes."

She did not say she hadn't. He had been afraid she would. He had been more afraid that she would and he would believe her.

"I only did them so it would fail for a wee while," she said. "A minute. Two. You'd have it back in three. I'd timed you. I knew you'd have it back." Her voice was quite steady. "I heard him, Tobias. The last time. He said my name. He knew it was me. He said, *Nessa, come down to the water. It's warm.*" Her face broke then, just for a moment, and mended. "He's never once since he drowned been warm."

"It's not Calum."

"You don't know that."

"I know what's in the water," he said. "I know what it does when it's let wake. You don't."

"Then tell me." She came up another rung. "Tell me what you know. Tell me what's in those books of Mr Crane's. Tell me what your man on the stair says to you. Tell me what was wrong with my brother's boat the night he went over, because there was nothing wrong with the weather and nothing wrong with the boat and my father's never said one word about it in two years. Tell me *anything*."

He thought of the old man in the box bed with the tears running into the pillow. *Murdo MacAulay was skipper.* He thought of Donald Iain at the gate with the card in capitals. *Your light can shine on an empty sea.* He thought of how it would be to tell her. Her uncle and her brother both. *The light was out.* He thought of her face, which was the face of someone who had already lost everything the sea could take, and the sea was still not done.

He thought that if he told her, she would go to her father. And Donald Iain would come out through any weather there was with a crowbar and smash the lens. Or he would go to Glasgow and never come back. And the whole island would know, and Ishbel would know, and then the light would not be the keeper's any more. It would be everyone's.

"It's the keeper's business," he said.

She stood on the rung looking up at him. Then she set the box of groceries on the top rung by his boots, very carefully, and went back down into the boat.

"You'll not keep it alone," she called up to him over the noise of the outboard. "He didn't. Mr Crane. He never kept anything. You'll see."

He watched her go.

Crane died on the eleventh of February, in the night, with the light turning on the window.

Ishbel sent word on the tender. Tobias crossed by day for the funeral and stood at the back of the church with no steeple, in his good serge suit that was loose on him now. Nobody spoke to him. Ishbel sat in the front pew with her long jaw set. When the minister spoke of thirty-five years of faithful service on Sgeir Dubh, of a keeper who had never once let the light fail in all his years, she put her hand over her eyes.

Donald Iain was there in a dark coat, standing at the side, apart. He did not look at the coffin. Nessa stood at his shoulder. She did not look at Tobias at all.

Afterwards, on the road, Tobias walked behind the small crowd going down to the burial ground by the shore. He saw that the names on the war memorial by the gate were not only war names. Somebody had added a brass plate at the bottom, newer, gone green. *Lost from the fishing boat Kestrel, 14th October 1947.* Six names. *Murdo MacAulay. Calum Morrison. Kenneth Morrison. Ruairidh Macleod. John MacInnes. Angus Gillies.* Under that there was room for more. Under that someone had scratched with a knife, small, *Calum MacAulay 1941–1961*, and somebody else had tried to scrub it out and not managed.

He stood at the gate with the crowd going on without him and read the two MacAulays, the one in brass and the one cut with a knife, one above the other.

The light had killed them both. Not the sea, not the weather the Board had written down, not bad luck past the Teeth. The light, going dark under Crane's hand, once because he wanted to hear his wife sing and once because he was an old man asleep in a chair. Donald Iain had lost his brother to it when he was a man in his middle thirties, with a boy of six at home, and he had lost that boy to it at fifty, and between the two he had stood at a gate and been sworn to on a Bible. He blamed the lighthouse. The whole island thought it a grudge, a bitter man's grudge, the kind that grows on a croft like rust. It was not a grudge. He had been right. He had been right for sixteen years and had never once known why, and the only man who could have told him was going into the ground in a box at the bottom of the road, with the minister saying he had never let the light fail.

And now Tobias knew why, and Donald Iain did not.

He thought of the island's silences. Of Donald Iain not saying his son's name. Of a woman standing every year at the slip. Of all the things nobody said on Eilean Fada, and whose names filled the gaps.

He did not tell anyone. He crossed back to the rock before the gathering in the hall and lit the light at five past five, and sat in the chair, and listened to the stair.

The voices did not wait for the dark now.

They came in the flashes too, faintly, under the roar of the burner. At three in the morning, with the mantle white and the lens turning, Harry Lisle said at the top of the stair, just outside the lamp room door, quite clearly, "Tobias. You held it."

He did not turn round.

"You held it, Toby. You'd your hand on the shutter. Why'd you hold it?"

*Not yet.* The cold through the duffel. The hand on his shoulder. Whose hand?

"We were waiting on you," Harry said. "All of us. We're waiting on you still."

He gripped the seat of the chair. He fixed his eyes on the mantle so hard that the white of it burned a green afterimage into everything. He did not answer. He did not answer. Below him, fifty feet down, the weight was dropping slowly in its tube, and he would have to wind it at four, and he did not know if his arms would do it.

He wound it at four.

Margaret finished the installation on the twenty-fifth.

She had shielded the motor housing in a copper box and earthed it to the bedrock. She had mounted the bracket on rubber blocks. She had fitted a new governor to the turntable, and a crystal clock that would not drift a second in a year. Every afternoon she ran the lamp, and every afternoon it ran clean. Ten seconds, ten seconds, ten seconds. She wrote the figures in her columns and did not show him.

"It's in daylight," he said once, on the stair.

"I know it's in daylight," she said, without stopping.

The plan was written out on Board paper and pinned to the wall of the kitchen. At sunset on the twenty-ninth the old light would be exhibited for the last time. At midnight the burner would be shut off and the new lamp switched on. There would be no gap: the electric light would be burning before the paraffin was out. Two Board officers would come on the *Pharos* to witness it and sign the certificate. Tobias would remain at the station for a period of one month to observe and report. After that he would be posted elsewhere, a shore station, with a garden.

He read it twice and went up the tower.

That night Margaret came up at two in the morning, which she had not done since January. She stood in the lamp room doorway in her duffel coat with the hood up.

"You'll be there," she said. "At midnight. When we switch over."

"I'll be here."

"If it goes wrong," she said, "you'll have the burner back."

"Two minutes fifty."

"That's not what I asked."

He looked at her. She was so small in the coat. Her face was all angles.

"I'll have it back," he said.

She nodded and went away down the stair. He heard her stop at about the eightieth step, at a window, and stand there for a while. Then she went on down.

The weather turned on the twenty-seventh.

It came out of the Atlantic, a deep low the shipping forecast gave as falling fast, going to severe gale nine, storm ten later. By the afternoon of the twenty-eighth the sea was coming green over the landing. The *Pharos* telegrammed from Oban by the radio that she would sail at first light and stand off the rock until it was safe to land the officers, if it was ever safe. Margaret went down to the oil store every hour to check the batteries and the generator.

On the twenty-eighth, in the last of the light, Tobias stood on the gallery in his oilskins with the wind trying to tear him off the rail, and looked across at Fada.

There was a light at the island slip. A small one, a hand lamp. It was moving, along the slip and back. Somebody was working there in the dark and the rain, with the sea breaking white on the rocks below.

As he watched, the hand lamp lifted, and swung once, twice, the way Crane used to swing his lamp at passing boats from the gallery window.

Two. And the long quiet.

Then it went out.

On the slip at Fada, in the rain, Nessa MacAulay put the hand lamp down on the stone and pulled the canvas back over the clinker boat.

She had filled the tank and two spare cans. She had stripped the outboard and cleaned the plugs and put it back together on the kitchen table while her father slept. Or while he lay in his bed pretending to sleep, as he had every night since Calum, staring at the ceiling. She had bought a new shear pin at the shop and a new starter cord. In the forward locker, wrapped in oilcloth, were a torch with fresh batteries in it, and the big adjustable spanner with the taped handle that she had taken from the bottom of the station's tool chest the day he showed her the lamp room, where he had never once looked for it since. Under them, folded small, was Calum's gansey. It was the navy one their mother had knitted him in the Fada pattern the winter before she died, with the cable and the ladder and the tree of life up the front, and it had lain at the bottom of the kist in his room for two years, where her father had put it and never lifted the lid. She had lifted it. She had packed no matches. Matches were for lighting.

She had the plan by heart. The date was on the notice to mariners pinned in the post office window since January, for anyone who cared to read it: *On and after 0001 hours on 1st March 1964 the character of Sgeir Dubh light will be altered.* The rest she had from Mrs MacAulay at the shop, who had the Board woman's telegrams through her hands before they went to Oban and could keep nothing to herself. The *Pharos* standing off with two officers aboard. A certificate to be signed. The burner shut and the electric lamp switched on at midnight on the twenty-ninth. There would be a minute, two minutes maybe, of business: men in the lamp room, the Board woman with her clipboard, everybody looking at the new lamp. Nobody would be looking at the cables running down the tower to the batteries. Nobody would be looking at the old burner gone cold.

Ten minutes, he had told her in October. Less.

She did not want ten minutes. She did not want three. She wanted as long as it took. She wanted to stand on the black rock in the dark with no light at all, and hear Calum sing every verse there was. She wanted to sing back to him, and ask him, at last, what had happened in the water past the Teeth. She wanted to ask why he had gone out that night when the forecast was fair. She wanted to ask whether it had hurt, and whether he was cold. She wanted to tell him their father had put the *Catriona* up for sale, and would not say his name, and was going to take her to Glasgow, where there was no sea at all.

The rain came across the slip in sheets. Behind her, up the road, there was a light in her father's window. Across the water, through the murk, the tower went on with its slow white work.

Two. And the long quiet.

She stood and counted it, the way she had been counting it since she was a girl. Then, softly, under the noise of the wind, she began to sing the seal-wife song. She sang it with the verse their mother left out, so that if anyone was listening in the water, they would know her.

## Chapter 3: Two and the Long Quiet

The *Pharos* came out of the murk at first light on the twenty-ninth and could do nothing. Tobias watched her from the gallery with the glass. She lay a mile off in the lee of the rock, rolling her rails under, her funnel smoke blown flat. Twice she put her nose towards the landing and twice she thought better of it. The sea at the foot of the rungs was not waves any more. It was a single grey movement that came up the east face, hung there, and fell back in sheets. The derrick arm shook in its housing like a tooth.

Margaret came up at eight with two mugs and stood beside him in her duffel coat, the hood laced tight so only her nose and spectacles showed.

"They'll not land," she said.

"No."

"Then they'll witness from the bridge. The certificate allows it. Visual observation of the character from a vessel at sea." She had the paper by heart. "You show the old light from sunset. At midnight you shut the burner down, and I throw the switch in the cabinet below, and the new lamp takes over. One white every ten seconds. They stand off and watch the one become the other, and they signal it, and the inspector signs. They only need to see it change."

He drank. The tea was too sweet. She had begun making it the way he made it, and he could not remember when.

"They'll see something," he said.

She looked at him then, quickly, and away. Behind them, on its bracket at the seaward side of the great lens, the electric lamp sat waiting under its canvas hood: a squat drum of new glass, with the crystal clock sealed in its base and a grey cable running from it down the tower stair to the cabinet beside the generator. Below them the oil store roof streamed. Somewhere under it the generator was chuntering to itself and the seismograph pen was writing on its drum. She had checked them every hour since Thursday. He had heard her on the stair at two and three and four, up and down, like a keeper herself.

"I've been through the circuit four times," she said. "I'm not a fool, Mr Fenn."

"I never thought you were."

"There's nothing in the drum that salt can reach. The governor on the turntable is new. I fitted it myself and I've had it apart twice since. The crystal clock is sealed. The bulbs are new, from a different supplier, and I've had them under the glass." She stopped. "If it does whatever it does, you'll have the burner back."

"I'll have it back."

"That's all I need." She went down. He listened to her boots go round and round until the wind took the sound.

He wound at nine, forty turns, and felt it in his shoulders by the thirtieth. His arms had grown thin. The crank was the same crank and he was not the same man who had taken it in October. On the thirty-eighth turn the handle caught and went on. It was the smallest stiffness, a grain of something. He crouched with his torch and looked into the housing of the new governor Margaret had fitted. The flyballs spun in their bright circle. On the brass collar where they rode there was a dusting of white, fine as flour.

He wiped it with his thumb and tasted it. Salt.

There was no window in the governor housing and no way for spray to reach it. He wiped it clean with the cloth from the tray and oiled the collar. He wrote nothing in the log, because he did not know what words to use, and because the log was the Board's. When the *Pharos* could land, the inspector would carry it off, and the Board would read it with the new character pinned in the front.

At five he went down to the kitchen for the first time in daylight in a week and boiled the kettle and stood with his hands round it. On the wall by the range was the Board's paper with its timetable. *Sunset 29th February: last exhibition of the existing light. 2400 hrs: existing light extinguished; electric light exhibited, character Fl W 10s. Changeover to be witnessed by NLV Pharos.* Under it Margaret had written the sunset time in her small square hand. Under that, in pencil, someone had drawn a pair of short strokes and a long line. It was the old character, two and the long quiet. It might have been Margaret, or Nessa before Christmas. It might have been himself, and he did not remember.

---

He lit at 17.34. It was the last time he would light it the old way, and he did it slowly so that his hands would remember. He warmed the vaporiser with the spirit cup, opened the valve and waited for the roar. The mantle took and went from orange to white. He let the clockwork go and the great lens began its turn on the mercury, the bull's-eyes sweeping out over the water. Two, and then the long quiet. He wrote the time in the book, and the wind, which was SW 10. He looked at what he had written and then wrote *Last exhibition* in the margin, the way a man might write *Amen*.

The *Pharos* answered with a lamp. He read it without thinking: *OBSERVED.* His wrist went to the shelf where the station Aldis sat in its box, and he took it out and gave her *R* for received. He had not held a signal lamp since 1945. The shutter clacked under his fingers, too loud and too familiar, and he put it away and stood with his hand flat on the box until it stopped shaking.

The voices began at seven. They had come with the flashes for ten days now, so there was no point in waiting for the quiet. There was the crowd-murmur at the foot of the stair, a pub's worth of men in another room. Above it was Rennie, saying *Fenn* in his older voice, Scots and dry, the way he had said it across the mess when there was a cup to be had. Pinder was laughing at something. Then Harry was at the top of the stair, outside the lamp room door, so close Tobias could have opened it and touched him.

"You held it, Bunts. You'd your hand right on the shutter."

He did not answer. That was the rule, and it was the oldest rule on the rock, older than the Board. He sat in the split-seat chair with his back to the door and watched the beams go out.

"We're waiting on you."

At ten the tower began to move. He felt it in the chair first, a slow give, the way a tall tree moves in its roots. The glazing bars ticked. The lens rode its mercury like a cup on a tray and the flashes went out true. He watched the paraffin gauge, and the clockwork weight's mark on the tube, and the sea, which he could not see, only the white of it when the beams crossed. Two hours to midnight. He found he was counting them in fifteens.

At half past ten there were boots on the stair.

He knew the voices. He did not know these. They came round and round, heavy, uneven, stopping at the turns. Margaret's tread was quick and light and these were not hers. Harry had gone quiet. The murmur below had stopped, the way a room goes quiet when someone it knows walks in.

The boots stopped outside the door. He heard breathing, a woman's, hard and ragged, and the drip of water on iron. He had kept the rule for ten nights. He had kept it against Harry. He found he could not keep it against that breathing.

"Who's there?" he said.

The door opened. Nessa stood there soaked to the skin, her oilskin torn at the shoulder and her hair stuck flat to her skull. Her face was white with cold except for a graze along the cheekbone that was beginning to bleed again in the warmth. She had a canvas bag over one shoulder.

"Don't," she said. "Don't say it."

He had stood up without knowing he had. "You came across in that."

"I came round by the north of the Teeth. There's a lee if you know where to find it." She was shaking so hard the words came out broken. "I nearly did what Calum did. Twice. The second time I heard him tell me to bear away, and I bore away, and here I am." She laughed, and it was not a laugh. "So you see."

"I'll get you a blanket."

"You'll not leave this room. You never leave this room." She came in and shut the door behind her and leaned on it. The beam went over, two, and the long quiet. Her eyes went with it. He saw them go, and he saw them come back, not to him but to the pressure tank under the burner, to the brass air screw on its top and the brake lever on the clockwork frame. Through the autumn she had asked him about all of it, what that was for, and that, and how long the mantle burned if the pump was let go. He had told her. He had liked telling her. At Hogmanay, in the kitchen, she had asked what Crane's books said, and he had given her no answer. Then at the end of January there had been the business of the mantles, and he had sent her down the stair and told her not to come back to the rock.

"Margaret's below," he said.

"I know where Margaret is. I watched her lamp go in at the generator door from the rocks." She slid the bag off her shoulder and set it on the floor and knelt by it, and he let her, because his legs had gone to wood. She took out a torch. She took out her father's big adjustable spanner, the one from the *Catriona*'s engine box, with the jaws already set. Last she took out a jersey, a fisherman's gansey in navy wool gone brown at the cuffs, and held it against her chest.

"His," she said. "Mam knitted the pattern on it. They'd know him by it, if he came ashore. That's what it's for." She looked up at him. "At midnight it goes, Tobias. Your two and the long quiet. A machine starts winking at the water every ten seconds and it'll never stop and it'll never miss, and he'll never come up again. This is the last night there's a hand on it. So it has to be tonight."

"You don't know what's down there."

"I know what's down there. He is."

"Crane's books—"

"You'd not show me Crane's books." Her voice cracked and held. "You'd not tell me anything. You sat up here with it like a hen on a stone and you shut the door on me. So I learned it myself. I learned your pump and your screw and your brake. I learned you." The beam went over, and his eyes went with it. He could not help it. Ten nights he had watched every flash go out. "There," she said quietly. "Every fifteen seconds. You look up."

He looked back down and she was already at the tank.

He saw it the way he had seen things on the bridge of the *Corrigan*, whole and too late. She had the spanner on the air screw and she spun it, and the tank sighed like a man set down after a long carry. He took two steps. She turned with the spanner up between them, not to strike him, only to be there, and with her other hand she threw the brake lever on the clockwork frame. The lens checked on its mercury. The bull's-eyes slowed and stopped, staring at the north wall.

The roar of the burner thinned. The mantle went from white to yellow to orange, a coal in a grate, and then to a dull red web, and then it was a ghost of itself, and then it was nothing. The lamp room went black except for the torch in the bag on the floor, lying on its side and lighting her boots.

"22.41," he said, out of habit, to nobody.

"I'm sorry," Nessa said. "I'm not. I'm sorry."

He had the spirit cup in his hand. He did not remember lifting it. Pump the tank back up, sixty strokes. Warm the vaporiser, three minutes, four in this cold. Open the valve. Five minutes, and the sea would have had five minutes, and he knew what it had done with eleven. He knelt by the pump.

Below them, at the foot of the tower, a switch went over with a clack he felt through the floor. The electric lamp lit.

It was very white, whiter than the mantle had ever been, and it came out of its drum and went across the water once, a single clean flash. Margaret had not waited for midnight. Margaret had heard the burner die and done what she had said she would do. He waited, kneeling, with the count running in him. Ten seconds, the new character. At eight there was a sound from the bracket, a small dry sound like a man grinding his teeth. At ten there was nothing. The drum stayed dark. In the base of it something went on ticking, slower and slower, and then stopped.

Under the rock the sea drew in its breath.

---

He did not pump the tank. He would think about that afterwards, for a long time, in daylight. He knelt with his hand on the pump handle, and outside the glazing the wind went on screaming over the rock, and under the wind the sea went quiet. It did not go calm. It went quiet, the way a great dog goes quiet before it gets up. The tower stopped moving. The drip on the iron stair stopped.

Then the singing began, somewhere very far down, a long note without breath in it, and the stair filled with the sound of men going down.

Nessa had the gansey and the torch and she was out of the door before he stood. He went after her. He had not been below the kitchen after dark in ten nights, and his feet did not know the stair, and he went down it like an old man with one hand on the rail. On the turn by the oil store door Margaret was standing with a storm lantern, her hood thrown back and her spectacles misted, holding the seismograph drum against her coat like a child.

"It flashed once," she said. "Once. Then the clock stopped. I opened the base." She held out her hand in the lantern light, and in the creases of her palm there was salt, white and fine as flour. "It's sealed, Mr Fenn. It's sealed and it's full of salt."

"I know."

"The pen's gone off the drum. Right off. I've never—" She stopped and looked at him. "The tide's come in," she said. "There isn't a tide. It's three hours past high water and it's come in."

He took the lantern from her, and she came with him.

The landing door was open and Nessa had gone through it. The sea stood level with the top of the landing, and it lay as flat as poured lead, though the wind still tore over it and flung the rain sideways into their faces. No wave broke. Where the rungs went down there were no rungs, only the black glass of the water and a light in it, a faint green burning that came up from underneath, as if the rock stood over a lit room.

And on the tideline they were walking.

They came up out of the flat water onto the weed and stood there with the water running off them and did not shiver. There were six in the long oilskins and thigh boots of seventeen years gone, standing close together the way a boat's crew stands on a quay, and the foremost had a face Tobias had seen in a photograph pinned in the back of Crane's fourth logbook. *Kestrel*, Crane had written under it, and nothing else. There were men in navy duffels and seaboat lifejackets, the old cork kind, a dozen of them, more, and he knew the way they stood before he knew their faces. Rennie, with his hands in his pockets. Pinder, still laughing at something. And a little apart from them all, at the lip of the landing, a boy of twenty in a gansey with the cuffs gone brown, his hair dark with water, looking up at Nessa as if she were late.

"Calum," she said.

"You took your time," the boy said. It was a boy's voice, rough with cold and teasing. "I said bear away. I didn't say go round by Barra."

She went down on her knees on the wet stone in front of him and held out the jersey, and he took it, and Tobias saw his hands. They were whole and they were wet, and the water did not stop running off them.

Someone was at his own shoulder. He knew before he turned.

"Bunts," said Harry.

He was nineteen, as he had been, in his duffel with the toggles done up wrong, and he was smiling. Behind him the *Corrigan*'s men stood waiting on the weed.

"You held it," Harry said. "You'd your hand right on the shutter."

"I know," Tobias said. He had broken the rule once tonight. He broke it again. "I know I did. I saw the track. I had the lamp on the *Saltaire* and I had the letters in my hand, and I held it. Seventy seconds. I counted them. And she went up and the oil went out on the water and you went into it in the boat for the men off her, and you didn't come back."

"And why'd you hold it?"

He opened his mouth and nothing came.

"Go on, Bunts," Harry said, gently. "You were there. Why'd you hold it?"

It came up out of him the way the men had come up out of the water. The bridge of the *Corrigan* at three in the morning, sleet in the voice-pipes, the convoy darkened all round them like cattle in a field. The feather of the periscope, the track running white towards the tanker's quarter. The Aldis cold and heavy, his thumb on the shutter, the first flash of the *A* already in his wrist. And a hand on his. A hand on his wrist, not hard, very steady, and the First Lieutenant's voice at his ear, flat and clear. *Hold it, Fenn. No lights. Commodore's orders. You'll show the whole convoy to every boat in the pack. Hold it.* And he had held it, with that hand on his, and counted, and at seventy the order had come off his wrist, *Make it, make it now,* and he had made it, and the sea ahead of them had turned to day.

"Jimmy," he said. "Jimmy had my wrist."

"Jimmy had your wrist," Harry agreed. "And the Old Man had Jimmy's, near enough, and the Commodore had his. You weren't on your own on that bridge, Bunts. Nobody keeps a watch on his own. You've been carrying a lamp nobody ever let you work." He tilted his head, the way he used to when there was a card game going and Tobias was being slow. "Twenty year. You want to put it down now."

"I don't know how."

"Same as you picked it up. With somebody's hand on yours." Harry looked past him, up the rock, at the black tower. "You'll want to be quick about it, mind. It's waking. It's not us, really. You know that. It's only got us on."

Down on the landing Nessa was talking to her brother, fast and low, in Gaelic and in English all mixed, and he was answering. Margaret stood by the door with her salt hand pressed flat against her coat and her mouth open, and she was writing nothing down.

Then there was a light on the water that was not green.

It was a masthead light, low and yellow, coming in from the north round the Teeth across the flat lead of the sea, and under it the shape of a herring boat with her engine hammering. The *Catriona* came alongside where the rungs should have been as if she were coming alongside the quay at home, and she did not rise or fall by an inch. Donald Iain MacAulay stepped off her onto the landing with a rope in his hand and no oilskin, and his face in the lantern light was the face of a man who has run the whole way.

"Nessa," he said. "You took the spanner out the box—"

Then he saw who she was kneeling to.

Tobias had never heard him say his son's name. In five months on the rock and the island he had heard Donald Iain say *my boy*, twice, at the gate, with his back half turned, and never once the name. He watched the old skipper's mouth try the shape of it and fail. The six in the long oilskins turned their heads. Donald Iain looked at them, and Tobias saw him know them, as he had known them on the quay, the year they did not come home.

"That's the *Kestrel*," Donald Iain said. "That's Angus Gillies." His voice went. He looked at the foremost of them, the face from the photograph, and it came back to him thin as a boy's. "That's Murdo. That's my brother Murdo." He turned to Tobias, wild. "The light was out that night. Forty-seven. Everybody said. The light was out and it took them, and it was out the night it took my boy—"

"It was Crane," Tobias said. "In forty-seven. He put it out himself, to hear his wife. It's in his books. I've read it. It wasn't the light. It was a man at the light, on his own, with nobody to stop him." He swallowed. He could have stopped there. He did not. "The night Calum went, it went dark too. Forty seconds. A wick fault, while an old man slept in the chair with nobody to wake him. He wrote it in the margin, small, where the Board wouldn't look, and I found it. Forty seconds." He made himself hold Donald Iain's eye. "The sea doesn't need long to take a boy. It only needs a man left on his own."

Donald Iain stood with the rope slack in his hand. Then he let it fall and went down on the stone beside his daughter, slowly, the way a man goes down who has not knelt since he was married.

"*A bhalaich*," he said. *My boy.* And then, in English, as though it had to be said in the plainest words there were: "I sent you out. I said the weather would hold. It was me said it, not the light. I've been telling the whole island it was the light for two years so I'd not have to say it was me."

"I know, Dad," said Calum. "I was there."

"I'm sorry."

"I know that, too."

Donald Iain put out his hand to his son's face and stopped a breath short of it. He did not touch him. Tobias saw him decide not to, and saw what it cost.

Tobias was counting. He had not meant to. The count had come on in him at 22.41, and it had been running ever since, fifteen and fifteen and fifteen, as steady as the old lens. He let it run. He let the father say his piece and the sister say hers. The boy on the landing put the gansey over his head and pulled it down, and it was dry for a moment, the wool fluffing up at the cuffs, and then the water came up through it from inside.

"Tobias," said Calum.

It was Calum's voice. It was not Calum who said it. It came out of the boy's mouth a little too slowly and a little too deep, the way a man repeats a word in a language he is learning, and behind it, far down, the long note of the singing rose and found the shape of his name too. *Tobias.* Rennie said it on the weed. Murdo said it. The flat sea said it, all round the rock, under the wind.

Nessa had gone still. She was looking at her brother's face, and Tobias watched the certainty go out of hers the way the mantle had gone out, white to orange to red.

"That's not him," she said. "That's not him now."

"No," Tobias said. "That's long enough."

She got up. She did not need help. She stood a moment longer looking at the boy in the gansey, and said something to him in Gaelic too low to hear, and he smiled. It was his own smile. It had been his own all along, and that was the worst of it. Then she turned her back on him and came up the landing.

"How long to light it?" she said.

"Five minutes for the burner."

"And the clock?"

"Salt in the governor. It won't drive. It'll have to go round by hand." He looked at the three of them, the fisherman getting up off his knees, the engineer with her lantern, the girl with the blood running down her cheek again. Every night for ten nights he had told himself that he would do it alone, because that was the penance, and the penance was the point. A hand on his wrist. *Nobody keeps a watch on his own.* "I can't keep it on my own," he said. "Not till dawn. I'll be out by a second inside the hour, and a second's enough. I need you. All of you."

"Then show us," said Margaret.

---

They went up the stair together in the dark, with the sea saying his name in the walls.

He pumped the tank to pressure while Nessa held the torch. Sixty strokes. He lit the spirit cup and set it under the vaporiser, and while it warmed he showed Donald Iain the brass handles on the lens carriage and how the lens floated, how one push would bring the first bull's-eye through and then the second, and how the push must stop clean, and how you waited. Donald Iain put his big hands where he was shown and said nothing, and his hands were steadier than Tobias's.

"Two," Tobias said, "and the long quiet. Fifteen seconds from the start of one pair to the start of the next. It has to be exact. Not near. Exact."

Margaret took off her glove with her teeth and brought out of her coat a stopwatch on a lanyard, nickel-plated and scratched, the one she had used on her tide gauges every hour since Thursday. She held it up to the torch. Her hand was shaking. She pressed the crown, and the hand began to sweep round the dial, and her own hand steadied.

"I'll keep the time," she said. "I'll call it. I've been timing things that couldn't happen for a fortnight. I may as well time something that has to."

"I'll count," said Nessa. "Out loud. So you've something to hear when she calls."

The vaporiser ticked as it warmed. Outside, the sea had begun to lift at last. He heard it hit the east face and climb, and this time it did not fall back. He opened the valve. The mantle took, orange, and he stood with his hand over it as if he could warm it with his palm, and it went white.

"Now," said Margaret.

He put his hands on the carriage beside Donald Iain's. They pushed. The great lens moved on its mercury and the first bull's-eye went out over the water, and the second, and they stopped it clean.

"One," said Nessa. "Two. Three."

Fifteen. Two, and the long quiet. Margaret's voice came steady out of the dark by the glazing: *Now.* They pushed. Nessa counted. The beams went out, and down on the landing the green light under the water shook, as if something had turned over in its sleep.

By midnight his arms were gone. He kept his hands on the carriage anyway, because that was his place, and he felt the handle begin to go wrong under them, a breath early. Before he could check it there was a hand on his wrist. It was not hard. It was very steady.

"Let me," Nessa said. "You've shown me. I learned it. Let me."

He thought of Jimmy's hand on his twenty years ago in the sleet, holding him back, and of how he had carried that hand every night since as if it were his own. This one was not holding him back. He took his hands off the lens and let her have it.

"Now," said Margaret. They pushed, the girl and her father, and the beams went out true.

He sat on the floor with his back against the lens pedestal, under the bracket of the dead electric lamp, and counted with Nessa under his breath. At one he took the watch from Margaret, so that she could sit down and drink the tea he made her, too sweet, the way she made his. At two Donald Iain stood back with his face grey and Tobias took his place. At three Margaret took the watch back from him without a word and he did not argue. At some time after four the wind dropped a point, and he realised the walls had stopped saying his name. Under the beams the sea was only the sea, white and broken and loud, beating on the east face as a sea should in a westerly gale, and below the noise there was nothing at all.

At first light the *Pharos* was still there, a mile off in the lee, rolling her rails under. Her lamp began to wink.

*OBSERVED OLD CHARACTER THROUGHOUT,* he read. *NEW NOT EXHIBITED. REPORT.*

He went to the shelf and took the Aldis out of its box. His hand was not shaking. He looked at Margaret.

"What do I tell them?"

She took off her spectacles and wiped them on her scarf, and put them back on, and he watched her decide.

"Tell them the electric lamp failed in service owing to sea ingress at the flasher unit," she said. "Tell them the station kept the light by hand from 22.43 until sunrise and no exhibition was lost. Tell them the engineer will report in writing."

He made it, letter by letter, with the shutter clacking under his fingers, and the *Pharos* answered *R*.

---

She wrote it at the kitchen table on the second of March, when the ship had finally landed the inspector and taken him off again, and Tobias sat across from her and watched her do it. She wrote that the trial lamp had failed after one exhibition owing to the ingress of salt into a sealed unit, by a route she had not been able to determine. She wrote that the exposure of Sgeir Dubh made unattended operation unsafe at the present state of the art, and that she recommended the station remain manned until a lamp could be devised to withstand it, which in her professional estimation would not be soon. Under *Tide gauge, 29th February* she wrote *Normal for springs.* Under *Seismograph* she wrote *Instrument fault; pen displaced from drum; record discarded.*

Then she took the drum out of its box, with the pen's last line running off the edge of the paper like a man walking off a cliff, and she put it in the range and shut the door on it.

"That's the first thing I've ever written that wasn't true," she said.

"Is it?"

"No." She screwed the cap back on her pen. "The last one wasn't true either. The one that said none of this could happen. This one is only a different kind of lie." She looked at him. "They'll want a second man. An assistant. You can't sit up every night for ever. I've seen what it does to the reading of a man's hand."

"There's nobody on the Board's list who'd come."

"There's a form," said Margaret, "for an occasional assistant keeper, locally engaged. Nobody has used it since the war. I know where it is."

---

In April the herring came back into the Minch. Not as they had been, the old men said, but enough. Donald Iain did not sell the *Catriona*. He came out to the rock on the first calm Saturday with a box of fish and a sack of coal and Nessa in the bow, and he stood on the landing a long time looking at the weed where his son had stood. Then he helped carry the coal up, and drank his tea in the kitchen, and talked. He talked about Calum the whole length of a mug, the time he had rowed to Barra for a dance and the time he had put the boat on the Teeth at fourteen and lied about it, and Nessa sat across from him with her hands round her own mug and laughed until she cried, and he let her. Tobias went up to the lamp room and left them to it.

Abel Crane had been in the ground on Eilean Fada since February. In the middle of March Tobias went across on the *Catriona* and walked up to the burial ground alone, and stood at the grave with the wind at his back and the light on the horizon behind him, and told him everything. He said it aloud, plainly, the way a man makes a report, the eleven minutes and the salt and the hand on his wrist and the six on the weed. Nobody answered. He had not expected anybody to. He thought he knew what the old man would have said, if he had been made to say anything: that he had been on his own, and that was the whole of it, on his own with her voice. When he got back to the rock he took down the fourth logbook, and on the inside of its cover, under the photograph, he wrote the names of the *Kestrel*'s six, as Donald Iain had given them to him, so that someone had.

The form came through in May. Margaret posted it from Edinburgh with a note in her small square hand: *Occasional Assistant Keeper (Local), N. MacAulay. Don't let the Board see her on the stair in trousers. M.H.* Under that, in pencil, she had drawn two short strokes and a long line.

Now the light went in at sunrise and the house went quiet. Nessa wound the clock at nine, forty turns, and he lay on his bunk in the bunk room with the curtain pulled half across and listened to the crank go round. On the thirty-eighth turn there was no catch. She cleaned the governor collar every morning with a cloth, and she had found no salt since March.

He meant to listen to the end of it. He meant to hear the brake go off and the weight settle and her boots go down the stair, round and round. He heard the thirty-ninth turn, and the fortieth. The sun lay across the blanket and warmed his hands where they lay outside it, and below the rock the sea moved against the landing, slow and heavy and asleep.

He did not hear her boots on the stair. He was sleeping.
