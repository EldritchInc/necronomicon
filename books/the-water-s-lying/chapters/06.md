# Chapter 6: Heater Test

The chime went at 01:20. Piq was already awake to hear it.

She had spent two hours lying on top of the bunk in C-41, unstrapped, feeling the ring hold her down. At 0.38 g the weight never let go. It lay along her hips and the backs of her knees, and it sat behind her sternum like a hand that had forgotten to lift. When she swung her legs off the bunk her heels met the deck harder than she'd judged, and the ache climbed her shins into both knees. Six months, the Vesta clinicians had said. With exercise, with supplements, with sense.

She dressed in the dark. She put on the grey coverall with the IHB patch, because an auditor caught wandering at night ought to look like an auditor. The receiver went into her sleeve pocket, its shell printed with a cartoon moon and the words SLEEP BETTER. The stylus case went into her thigh pocket. The daybook stayed on the shelf with Teodor's pencil in its spine.

The ring corridor was in night cycle, lit by amber strips at ankle height. She walked a little to spinward the way the crew did, and twice corrected the Coriolis late. Spoke three's elevator stood open on standby. She set her feet in the floor loops and pressed HUB.

The weight drained out of her as the car climbed. At sixty metres her stomach began its old suspicion. By ninety she was barely touching the deck, and her shoulders came down from somewhere near her ears. The car shuddered through the spin-transfer collar, and the floor stopped being a floor and became a direction she happened to be facing.

The doors opened. Piq let go and went.

This was the part she couldn't hide, and at night she had stopped trying. One push on the frame carried her eleven metres down the axial corridor without touching a wall. She caught a rung with two fingers, swung, and cleared the junction where the spoke-head conduits crossed. The hub smelled of cold air and scrubber graphite, and under that it smelled of water. Eight thousand tonnes of it, or whatever was really in the tanks behind the walls.

Bus B began where the corridor necked down past the shelter hatches. The light changed there, very slightly, from Bus A's cool white to the yellower cast of a panel set somebody had bought cheap. She had learned to feel the seam the way you feel a change in water temperature. Twice now, at two in the morning, the yellow side had dimmed and recovered. It was the first thing in this ship she had been sure meant something.

She passed the shelter's antisunward hatch, sealed and green-lit, and then the comms office, which had a little round window in its hatch. Inside, a single screen scrolled columns of numbers. In front of it, strapped loosely to a chair, a cup of tea tethered to his wrist by a lanyard, sat Osei Brandt.

He looked up before she could drift past. The hatch slid open.

"Auditor Ansari-Holm." He said it the way some people said *weather*. "Is this an audit?"

"Insomnia," she said. It came out easily. "The ring sits on my chest. I come up here to float."

"Ah. Ceres." He nodded as though she had named a chronic condition with a known protocol. "Come in, if you're floating. Don't touch the second screen. It has opinions."

She hooked a toe under the hatch rail and hung in the doorway. She wanted the corridor, the console bay, the dark. But leaving straight away would be noticed. Sitting with Osei for three minutes would only be forgotten.

"You're on late watch."

"I'm always on late watch in conjunction season. Nobody else will take it, because nothing happens." He turned the screen toward her with real pride. "Except this happens. Relay Trailing-Four is holding three hundred and eleven packets for onward forwarding. Do you know how it orders them?"

"Priority?"

"*Size.*" He let it sit, as if it were a scandal. "Smallest first. A forty-kilobyte lunch roster from Deimos goes ahead of anything with an attachment. If you ever need something to arrive, auditor, write it short. Brevity is the only priority store-and-forward respects."

"I'll remember."

"Nobody remembers." He scrolled with one finger. "Queue depth on Four is up nine percent on the week. Dwell's up too. The headers are stripped, so I can't tell you who's waiting, only how many and how fat. It's like counting a crowd by the weight of the floor." He tapped another column without much interest. "Long-range set's been picking something up again. Steady line, well outboard. Too warm for a rock, no transponder. The classifier has three bins: natural body, registered vessel, vessel in distress. It isn't any of them, so it goes in the fourth bin."

"What's the fourth bin?"

"Debris." He sipped from the tethered cup. "Debris is a very accommodating category."

*Too warm for a rock.* Warm meant power, and power meant somebody. The thought surfaced and went. A warm thing a long way off had no bearing on what was happening forty metres down this corridor in the next quarter-hour.

"And Bus B sighed at me again on Tuesday," Osei went on. "Two in the morning, a little brownout, every light on the yellow side. That's my fourth ticket. Engineering thinks I'm describing a feeling."

"Maybe you are."

"I'm describing a voltage." He looked faintly hurt. "Feelings don't come with a timestamp."

"Mine do," Piq said, and pushed off before he could ask what she meant.

---

Past comms the corridor narrowed again and turned antisunward toward the far cap, where the Umbrella's control bay sat behind its own hatch. She had stood at that hatch four nights ago with a pick in her fingers and a rehearsing pensioner coming round the bend. Tonight she didn't plan to get that far. She had picked a recess two bays short, behind the junction box for the cryoplant feed. It was dark, with a sightline to the bay, and it smelled of nothing.

The cryoplant line ran along the corridor ceiling, a fat insulated pipe as thick as her thigh, and it was white.

She stopped herself with one hand on a rung and looked up. In daylight shift that pipe was matte grey and dry. Tonight frost had feathered along its underside in delicate combs where the lagging thinned at the brackets. Her breath fogged near it. She put a fingertip to the rime and felt the cold sting through the skin, a cold that came from something on the other side working very hard.

Cryoplants ran harder when there was heat to pull. A coil sitting cold and idle needed only enough cooling to hold it there. A coil carrying current, and taking ripple, needed more.

She followed the line hand over hand along the ceiling rungs, the frost a pale road ahead of her. It turned once at a bracket and dropped down a bulkhead. It went through a penetration into the control bay wall beside a hatch that was not locked. The hatch stood open a hand's width, and light came through the gap.

Piq checked her chrono. 01:57.

She went in without making a sound, because the hub let you do that if you had been born to it.

The bay was smaller than she had pictured from the floor plans: a crescent of consoles, a viewport onto nothing, and a lot of pipework sweating and crackling with frost. Hana Szelig hung at the centre console with one foot hooked under the rail and her hair tied up badly in a pencil and a twist. She wore a thermal jacket over sleep clothes. On the main display a single bar climbed in slow green increments, with a label Piq could read from the hatch: FIELD 0.38 / NOMINAL.

Thirty-eight percent. The ring's gravity, it occurred to her stupidly, and then the thought was gone.

In her sleeve pocket the receiver clicked.

It was the sound she had heard four times now: a dry, rising chatter like a fingernail run fast down a comb. Then the short haptic buzz against her forearm that meant *capture complete*. The bay lights dimmed a few percent and came back.

Hana turned. She saw Piq. Her face did nothing at all for one full second.

"Get out," Hana said.

"No."

"This bay is closed outside day shift."

"I'm an IHB auditor. Nothing on this ship is closed to me." She came off the hatch frame and stopped herself against a console, close enough to read the display properly, close enough that Hana's hand moved to cover a key. "What are you sending?"

"What?"

"The burst. It went out as I came in. It went out at 02:00 on the ninth and at 02:13 on the fourteenth. Who's receiving it?"

Hana stared at her. The fury on her face was real, but there was something else in it that Piq couldn't read yet. "You think I'm *transmitting*."

"I think somebody on this ship is putting out encrypted traffic at two in the morning, and I think you're the only person awake in the room it's coming from."

The receiver had slid half out of her sleeve pocket with the movement. Hana's eyes went to it. Piq felt the moment she was seen, and couldn't stop it.

"What," Hana said, "is that."

Piq had the lie ready, as she always did. "A white-noise unit. I don't sleep."

Hana reached out, quite slowly, and turned it face-up between two fingers. The cartoon moon smiled at them both. SLEEP BETTER.

"It's a broadband scanner," Hana said, "in a children's shell." She let go of it and it drifted. "If you're going to accuse me of espionage, the least you could do is not carry the equipment in a nightlight."

There was nothing to laugh at, and neither of them did.

---

Hana could have called security. Piq watched her think about it. She saw Hana's eyes go to the wall handset and then to the display, and she saw the arithmetic arrive. A security call meant a log entry, and a log entry meant someone asking why the coil was at thirty-eight percent at two in the morning. Piq knew that look. She had worn it in mirrors.

"Give me that," Hana said.

Piq didn't move.

"You think it's catching a transmitter. Fine. Put it on the console where we can both see it. Don't watch me. Watch the readout."

After a moment Piq caught the drifting receiver and pressed it to the console's grip pad. Its small screen showed the last capture as a waterfall of hot pixels, broad across the band and hard-edged in time.

Hana pulled up a panel Piq hadn't seen before: QUENCH PROTECTION / DIAGNOSTIC. There was a row of channel indicators and a single key under a hinged guard labelled HTR TEST.

"Superconducting coil," Hana said. Her voice was flat, the voice of someone lecturing a person she disliked. "If any piece of the winding goes normal, it starts dumping stored energy as heat right there, in a spot the size of your thumb, and it burns through. So we watch the voltage across every segment. If one starts to rise, we fire heaters along the whole winding so it all goes normal at once and the energy spreads out instead of punching a hole. That's quench protection. You lectured me on it, as I recall, the day you were going to write it up."

"I remember."

"The heaters are useless if they've failed. So the system test-fires them. A short current pulse through every heater circuit, a few hundred amps for a few milliseconds, to prove they're alive." She flipped up the guard. "A few hundred amps switched in a few milliseconds through metres of cable makes a very good antenna. You've been listening to my heaters clear their throat."

She pressed the key.

The receiver chattered on the console, the same fingernail down the same comb, and buzzed. A fresh stripe of hot pixels scrolled up its screen. The bay lights dipped by a hair.

Piq did not believe it. Believing things on first demonstration was how officers ended up in Ceres's memorial niches.

"Again," she said. "When I say."

Hana took her hand off the key and folded her arms. Piq watched the second hand of her own chrono. She waited a count of seven, which nobody would guess, and then a count of eleven.

"Now."

Hana pressed. The receiver chattered.

"Now."

Chatter.

Piq let the silence run for nineteen seconds, watching Hana's hands the whole time. They stayed folded. The receiver stayed silent.

"Now."

Chatter. Buzz. Another hot stripe.

There is a particular feeling when a structure you have built in your head loses a load-bearing member. It isn't a collapse. It's a sway. Piq felt it go through her now, and in microgravity there wasn't even a floor to blame.

"That's a pulse you fired by hand," she said. She could hear how steady her voice was, and how much that cost. "The bursts I recorded weren't regular. My decoder flagged them as high-entropy. They didn't come at fixed intervals. Interval and shape both varied. That's what made them look like traffic."

"Yes," Hana said. "Because they *aren't* regular when the system fires them itself." She opened another panel, and her hands were faster now, the hands of someone showing her work despite herself. "Automatic test pulses aren't on a clock. They're gated by a ripple discriminator. The heater test only fires when the coil current is quiet enough that the test won't be mistaken for a real quench signature. And the coil current isn't quiet. Not out here."

On the display she dragged up two traces and laid one over the other. The top one was labelled SUNWARD PLASMA MONITOR — DENSITY / VELOCITY. It was a ragged, fine-grained line that rose and fell without pattern. The lower trace was a column of tick marks: HTR TEST — AUTO, with timestamps.

"The field pushes on the solar wind," Hana said, "and the solar wind pushes back. Every gust of plasma against the field induces a little ripple in the coil current. When the wind is gusty, the discriminator waits. When it calms, the test fires. The pulse shape changes too, because the ripple is riding on the current when the heaters switch." She tapped the top trace. "That's your cipher. The Sun breathing on my magnet."

Piq looked at the overlay. Every tick sat in a trough. The spacing between the ticks followed the spacing between the troughs, which was no spacing at all. It was weather.

"Show me the ninth," she said. "02:00."

Hana's mouth tightened. She scrolled back. The log was local, kept on a drive that was not, Piq noted, the ship's maintenance server.

*09 — 01:59:40 RAMP START. 02:00:02 HTR TEST AUTO. 02:00:02 BUS B DRAW 344 kW (PEAK).*

Piq took the stylus case out of her thigh pocket and thumbed it open. Her own entries sat in her own cramped shorthand: *09 / 02:00 burst + hub lights dim (Bus B side).* She had written it standing in the shelter corridor with a hen loose somewhere overhead, and she had underlined *lights*.

Three hundred and forty-four kilowatts. She ran it without wanting to. Bus B fed the comms office, the shelter auxiliaries, the yellow-lit half of the hub corridor and, apparently, this bay. A cheap panel set, a ramp supply pulling a third of a megawatt to push current into the winding, and a cable run that sagged under it for a second and a half. Lights dip. Osei files a ticket about a feeling.

"The fourteenth," she said. "02:13."

Hana scrolled. *14 — 02:13:07 QUENCH DET — SEG 11 — TRANSIENT (FALSE) — HTR FIRE ABORTED — TEST PULSE.* Under it, in Hana's own hand-typed note: *wind spike, 640 km/s, nearly lost it, went to bed 04:10.*

Piq remembered the next morning: the 08:00 coil-status briefing in the hub wardroom, with Hana behind the colour-tabbed binder. She had yawned so hard on the word *margins* that her jaw cracked, and she had glared at Piq as if Piq had caused it. Piq had written in the daybook: *Szelig — tired. Why?* She'd assumed a lover, or a conscience.

She did the last piece of arithmetic because it was the one that should have stopped her a week ago. A mole talking to anyone off the ship needed to reach a relay, and in conjunction season that meant a Mars-trailing station tens of millions of kilometres out. That took a dish, gain and pointing. It took a beam narrow enough that you'd never catch its sidelobes with a novelty scanner in a cabin on the ring. Her receiver had picked up every burst at roughly the same faint strength from C-41 and loud in the hub, and loudest now, here, a metre from the heater bus. Inverse square from a source on the axis. Not an antenna aimed at anyone. A radiator, spraying in every direction, aimed at no one.

There was no mole. There had never been a mole. There was a physicist who wasn't sleeping, and a magnet, and a wind.

Piq closed the stylus case. She noticed that her hand was not quite steady, and she put it flat on the console until it was.

---

"Who authorised this?" she said.

Hana laughed. It was an unpleasant sound and she seemed to know it. "Nobody. That's rather the point."

"An energised prototype on a crewed ship. Unlogged, at night, with the ship asleep. Past the safety review, past the Board, past—"

"Past you," Hana said. "Yes. Specifically past you. You and people like you."

"Then *why*?"

Hana held her gaze for a long moment. Then something in her shoulders gave. She didn't soften. She was simply too tired to hold the posture. She hooked her foot deeper under the rail and spoke to the display, not to Piq.

"Day six after Earth flyby. Ferro's office on the ring, the one with the real chair. He'd had my test authorisation pulled up. Nine months, the Board's estimate, for a live flight-test approval on a crewed ship. He said Tharsis didn't have nine months. He said the Directorate would review the second-coil money at the Mars flyby. If he didn't have a full-field flight log to show them, a real one, a certifiable one, the money went, and then the company went, and then the Umbrella went into a warehouse on Deimos for the next twenty years." She said the next part as though reading it off a page. "He asked for twelve nights of ramps under forty percent, and he said he'd handle the logs. He wants a readiness certificate by day one hundred and twenty. Before solar maximum peaks."

"And you said yes."

"He started asking on the long loop," Hana said. "I started saying yes then." She turned her head and looked at Piq directly for the first time since the key. "He keeps asking how soon. Never how safe. How soon. I assumed that was just what management sounds like."

Piq filed it the way she filed everything, in the cold drawer behind her eyes. *How soon. Day 120. Before maximum. Handle the logs.* Something about the schedule nagged at her, something she'd seen on the board in the ring commissary and hadn't finished thinking about, and it wouldn't come.

"I know what you're going to write," Hana said. "Unauthorised energisation. Unlogged test. Quench-protection system operated outside certification. I can probably give you the article numbers. And then the Board seals the bay, and Ferro says he never asked, which he'll say very well, and I go home on the next taxi, and the only working active shield anyone has ever flown goes cold on a ship that's about to fly through the worst sky in eleven years." She stopped. "So write it. You came up here for something to write."

The stylus case was still in Piq's hand. She opened it.

The finding form was already in there. It was always in there. IHB Form 7, Nonconformance — Critical. Her real credential would sign it with a real key, and the signature would travel out through Osei's beloved queue to the Board and be forwarded, smallest first, ahead of the lunch rosters. Her thumb rested on the field marked DESCRIPTION.

She thought about it properly, because thinking about it properly was the only thing she trusted.

Disposition A. *Observe.* Sabine's words had come through with their headers stripped, as stark as a tide table. Observe what? An energised coil was exactly what Ceres wanted to know about. A live test meant the Umbrella was further along than anyone in the Compact had modelled. But the moment Form 7 went out, the coil went under Board seal, and Piq stopped observing anything. She would be reading about it secondhand in someone else's inquiry.

And the auditor who wrote that finding would become the most visible woman on the ship. Ferro would want to know how she'd been in a locked bay at two in the morning. He would want to know what was in her sleeve. Ferro, who handled logs, would start looking at hers.

Those were the reasons. They were good reasons. She could have defended them to Sabine at the hearing table on Ceres.

There was another reason, and she noticed it and did not look at it straight. It was the way Hana's hands had moved over the console when she stopped being angry and started explaining. *That's your cipher. The Sun breathing on my magnet.* It was the yawn at the briefing, and the frost on the pipe, and twelve nights of this alone while the ship slept. Some part of Piq that she rarely consulted had registered all of it. It had decided, without asking her, that she didn't want to be the person who turned that green bar off.

She closed the form without saving. She closed the case and put it back in her thigh pocket, and the small sound of the clasp was very loud.

That was the moment, she understood later. Not the lock four nights ago, and not the lies. A Board auditor had watched a crewed ship run an uncertified prototype in the dark and had chosen to say nothing. It was the first thing she had done aboard the *Aubade* that was a crime under her cover as well as under her name.

"I'm not going to log it," she said.

Hana didn't relax. If anything she went stiller. "Why?"

"Because I can't defend it." The lie was smooth and well made, and it came out before she had decided to tell it. "I don't have a calibrated reference for your field strength, and I don't have chain of custody on that drive. A finding I can't defend is a finding that gets thrown out, and then you're cleared, and I'm the auditor who cried wolf. I don't write findings that fall over."

Hana looked at her for a long time. The frost on the pipework crackled faintly as the cryoplant cycled. On the display the green bar sat at thirty-eight and did not move.

"That's very procedural," Hana said.

"I'm a very procedural person."

"You're a person who breaks into locked bays at two in the morning." Hana reached out past her and plucked the receiver off the grip pad. The cartoon moon grinned up between her fingers. "With a broadband scanner, built to look like a toy, sitting in your sleeve. You recorded my heaters on the ninth and the fourteenth and you underlined the lights. And you came up here not to *audit* a transmitter but to *catch* one." She turned it over and read the back, where there was no serial number, only a smooth blank where one should have been. "Auditors don't hunt moles, Piq."

Piq said nothing. Nothing true came to her.

Hana held the receiver out. When Piq took it, Hana didn't let go at once. They hung there with it between them, both holding on, a metre from a magnet carrying enough stored energy to tear itself apart.

"I've just shown you mine," Hana said quietly. "On a log I could go to prison for. That's my crime." She let go. "What's yours?"
