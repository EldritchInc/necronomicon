# Chapter 5: Casting Notes

The notebook was heavier than it looked. It had been bound by hand in board covers and filled in fountain pen, and Ambrose Tull's pen pressed so hard that every page had a ridged back, as if he were writing for the blind. Piq carried it under her tray from the ring galley. Ring gravity pulled the tray one way and her tea the other, so she held the cup low and tilted it two degrees spinward, the way the crewman had shown her on day two. She spilled only a little.

The notebook went into the drawer under her bunk in C-41. She looked at the drawer for a moment. She had found the notebook face-down on a prop crate in the rehearsal room, where nobody had any business being unless they were looking for something. Returning it at the read-through would be easy. Explaining why she had read it would not be, unless she planned the explanation now. So she planned one. The notebook would have been left on a bench in the ring galley at lunch, where anybody might leave anything and anybody might pick it up. It would have fallen open, and she would be a little sheepish about it. She liked that version. Then she stopped thinking about it, which was how she knew it would work.

She had four hours before the read-through. She spent them on water.

---

Checklist item 58 was *Consumables reconciliation: potable, process, and shielding water inventory against logged draws.* It was a dull item, which made it useful. A dull item never needed a reason. The auditor's terminal in the purser's annex gave her read access to the quartermaster's digital log, and the log came up at once with clean columns.

Teodor Vasz had kept it for six years. On most days the entries were a single line: tank levels by section, draws for the farm decks, draws for hygiene, and the occasional transfer between hub and ring reservoirs, each with a work-order number. The handwriting tic that marked his tally inside her locker could not show up in a digital file, but people show up in digital files anyway. They are terse on bad days. They abbreviate the same word three different ways. They put a space before a colon because of a keyboard they learned on forty years ago.

In the early years Teodor did all of that. His entries said *Hub sec 3 — 97.1. ok.* and *Hub sec 3 – 97.0 (gauge sticky, rechecked)*. He wrote *xfer* in one place, *trans.* in another and *XFR* in a third. Sometimes the space before the colon was there and sometimes it wasn't.

From long-loop day 190 to his death on day 231, the entries were perfect. Every line ran to the same length. Every abbreviation was the house standard from the protocol's appendix. There were no rechecks and no sticky gauges. Each hub section read between 96 and 98 percent, as the shelter gauges had, and drifted down by a believable hundredth of a percent per week.

Piq opened the file properties. The auditor's view showed the history field, because the Board insisted on it: *Created: TVASZ. Last modified: OPS-ADMIN, LL day 233, 04:12. Comment: format migration.*

Two days after he died, somebody had migrated his format.

She thought about that for the length of a breath and then wrote it into her audit notes in exactly the words a Board auditor would use: *QM log, LL190–231: entries show post-hoc reformatting by OPS-ADMIN, LL233. Original entries not retained in view. Request originals.* She marked it amber, not red. A red mark would get a phone call. An amber mark would get a reply in a week, and by then she might know what she was asking for.

Purser Halloran watched her from the doorway of his inner office, binders stacked behind him with their strings tied in neat bows. His moustache was combed into two grey awnings.

"Teodor's things are still here," he said, though she hadn't asked. "He had a sister on Luna, Plato side. They should have gone down with the Earth taxi, by rights. But Operations impounded the lot pending the incident ruling, and the ruling isn't closed. So they ride to Mars with us, and we ship from there if Mr Ferro's office signs the release." He looked at the screen, at her, and at the screen again. "He kept paper. Section forty-four, records redundancy, recommends a paper daybook for any officer holding a consumables account. Recommends, not requires. Nobody does it. He did it."

"I'd like to see it," Piq said. "For reconciliation."

Halloran's expression held the conflict of a man who loved procedure and also, perhaps, had been fond of the dead. "It's personal effects. Impounded."

"It's a consumables account record under section forty-four," she said gently. "Kept in his capacity as quartermaster. An impound holds effects against release. It doesn't bar inspection by the Board. I'll sign for it."

That settled it. Halloran's face cleared, the face of a man handed a form he understood. He went to his shelves and came back with a string-tied binder labelled *CHAIN OF CUSTODY — TEMPORARY*. He opened it to a fresh page, unscrewed a pen, and watched her write her name, her credential number, the date, the item, and the purpose of the loan. Then he had her initial the margin. Then he initialled under her initials, blotted both with a square of paper he kept for the purpose, tied the strings and patted the binder.

The daybook came out of a crate behind the shelves. The crate had an Operations seal on its hasp, and Halloran broke it and logged that too. The book was a small, square paper log with a cloth spine gone soft, the kind sold in Lunar stationers to people who liked to feel their lives were being recorded by hand. It smelled of machine oil, and of something else she placed after a moment: the faint mineral tang of hub water, which got into everything stored near the tanks.

"Back by Mars," Halloran said.

"Long before," she said, and believed it.

---

She did not open the daybook in the annex. She took it to her cabin and set it on the shelf beside the dark sleep tracker that was not a sleep tracker. Then she went back to the terminal and did the second thing, because the clean log had given her an idea.

If someone had smoothed Teodor's entries, they had done it for a reader. The reader was a Board auditor, and that meant her. So the question was not what the log said but what it had to agree with. A water log had to agree with water draws. Most draws were small: farms, hygiene, the galley. The large ones were propellant.

The Aubade's attitude and trim thrusters were electrothermal. They took water, cracked and heated it on an arc, and threw it out of a nozzle. Each firing drew power from the hub bus in a hard, square-shouldered pulse that looked like nothing else on the telemetry. Her auditor access let her read every bus. It did not let her read the thruster control logs, which sat behind Operations with everything else. But a thruster could not fire without drawing current, and current could not be drawn without the bus recording it.

She pulled Bus C from Earth flyby forward. Bus C carried the thruster power conditioners, the docking collar heaters and the axial pumps. She set the filter for rise times under a hundred milliseconds and plateaus over two seconds.

The published propellant schedule for the fast leg was a two-page table in the protocol's appendix F, and she had read it on the taxi because she read everything. It listed four planned trim firings in the first week after Earth flyby, all short and all attitude, to hold the spin axis on the Sun as the ship's line to the Sun swung. Each was a few seconds on the small trim heads at a few kilowatts. A firing like that spent less water than she had spilled from her tea.

Bus C showed eleven firings in five days.

Four of them sat where the schedule said they should, in the planned windows, and they were short and clean. Seven did not. Two were at night, and five were scattered across ship's afternoons in a way that suggested someone was avoiding a pattern. Three were long, a full minute each, and they weren't on the trim heads. The draw climbed to the conditioners' plate rating, which appendix C gave as three megawatts. That was the most the main array could spare them while the radiators held.

She ran the numbers in her head because she did not trust the terminal to keep a record of what she asked it. Three megawatts at the conditioners, and call it forty percent of that reaching the jet, so one point two. Exhaust at six kilometres a second on a good day. Mass flow was twice the jet power over the square of the exhaust velocity: two point four million over thirty-six million, about sixty-seven grams a second. That made four kilograms a minute. Three long burns came to twelve kilograms, perhaps fifteen with the stragglers.

It wasn't much in a hub that held eight thousand tonnes. It was a couple of parts in a million, a rounding error, and Ferro had called it "housekeeping" through the duct. *Nobody files attitude trim.*

But three long burns were not attitude trim. Attitude trim was a tap, and these were shoves. A shove at the right moment, along the right line, did not turn a ship. It moved one.

She closed the bus view. She typed *Bus C: power events inconsistent with published App. F schedule (11 vs 4). Three events at conditioner rated draw, ~60 s. Request thruster control logs* into her notes, and marked it amber as well. Then she sat back in the chair, which in 0.38 g did not quite hold her and did not quite let her go. She looked at the two amber lines one above the other and felt the faint prickle at the back of her neck that she had felt on Kerberos at nineteen. It was the feeling she got when a lock's last wafer was nearly in line.

She told herself it was nothing yet. She had two anomalies, fifteen kilograms of water and no theory, and the Ledger trained you to distrust the theory that came first, because it was usually the one you had brought with you.

---

The ring galley's schedule board was a long flat panel above the serving line. It showed the ship's day, the hours to the next shelter drill, and the countdown that every passenger looked at at least once a meal: MARS FLYBY, with a ship-date and a time to the second, and below that the days, hours and minutes remaining.

Piq had read it on the morning of day two while she was spilling her tea, and she had kept it because she kept numbers the way some people kept stones from beaches. Flyby closest approach was ship-day 146, at 11:42:09.

At 18:30 on day five, the board said 11:42:49.

She stood in the queue for the vegetable stew with her tray tilted spinward and looked at it for a while. Ahead of her a woman in farm-deck green was explaining to a small boy that the window was not a window but a picture of outside, and that outside was not really that colour. Behind her someone was arguing about whether the play would have real costumes.

Forty seconds was not a difference anyone would notice. Nobody would miss the Mars flyby by forty seconds. The taxis would compensate. But the number on the board was not a timetable someone had chosen and typed in. The protocol's appendix A said so, proudly. The board took its feed straight from the navigation solution and refreshed at every fix, so that the passengers would always see the true figure. Nobody had to sign off on it, which meant nobody could hold it back. Someone who could reformat a dead man's log could not keep this from moving.

And it could only move for one reason. Closest approach came out of the ship's orbit, and the only way to change that number was to change the orbit, or to find out at a new fix that the old orbit had been wrong.

She did not know which. She did not know whether the shoves on Bus C had pushed the number or chased it. She knew only two things, and she set them side by side the way she had set the amber lines. The orbit was not the orbit it had been on day two, and the burns were not attitude trim.

"You're blocking the bread," said the man behind her.

"Sorry," she said, and took a roll, and did not think about it again until she was in the elevator.

---

The elevator was car three this time. She caught herself bracing at sixty-one metres all the same, where the brake interlock had failed in car two, and she felt her own stupidity at bracing and then relief when nothing happened. As the car rose, ring gravity bled out of her joints. The ache in her hips and knees, the low steady complaint of Ceres bones under nearly thirteen times the weight they were made for, loosened at forty metres, softened at twenty, and was gone by the hub. She came out of the car the way a fish comes out of a net into water.

The observation blister sat at the antisunward end of the hub, on Bus B. It was a hemisphere of laminated glass four metres across with a ring of foot loops around its rim and a stack of folded chairs no one would ever use, clipped to the wall for regulation. Beyond the glass the stars were steady. The hub did not spin, so there was no sweep and no wheeling. Earth was a bright blue-white point low to one side, and a smaller grey spark sat at its shoulder.

Ambrose was already there. He had looped one foot into a rim strap and was floating in his plum waistcoat with a sheaf of paper scripts fanned in one hand like a hand of cards. His lens hung on its chain and turned slowly in the air in front of him. He was giving notes to the glass.

"—and *pause*, and then the reveal, and *then*—Ah." He turned and saw her. "Ms Ansari-Holm. The face. Come in, come in. Mind the scripts, they're the only copies. Paper doesn't crash."

She pushed off the hatch rim with two fingers, drifted across, and caught a loop beside him without looking. She held out the notebook.

"You left this on the bench in the galley," she said. "At lunch. I'm afraid I read it." That was the plan: a small, specific lie wrapped around a small confession, offered before anyone could think to ask about the rehearsal room. "It fell open. I saw my name."

Ambrose took it. He did not ask which bench. He did not look embarrassed. He looked thrilled.

"Did you? Did you really? Which page?" He thumbed it open. His knuckles were swollen, the hands of a Lunar man who had worked with his hands in a sixth of a gee for decades. "Ah, yes. My suspects." He beamed at her. "Casting notes, dear. One casts the spy last, because the spy must be right. The spy *carries* the play. Everyone else is furniture with lines."

Piq felt the shape of the room shift. She knew it was a fair surprise, and it annoyed her that she hadn't seen it.

"Casting notes," she said.

"I watch for type. Mr Brandt, now." He tapped the first entry. "Far too obvious. A man who watches the hatch like that would be spotted in the first act. The rigger, wonderful presence, always there, but she won't speak to me. I've tried four times. She climbs away. One cannot cast a woman who climbs away." He sighed. "Ferro I considered for the villain, but he's management, and management never comes to rehearsal. And then—" He looked up at Piq with real affection. "Then I saw you at that door."

"The lab door."

"At one in the morning, in the dark, with a little case, absolutely *intent*. You thought no one was watching. That's the quality. It can't be taught." He shut the notebook. "And the towels. I nearly applauded. I followed you to the laundry to see if you'd break, and you came out with towels and a regulation. A *regulation*." He shook his head in wonder. "You are an auditor playing a spy who is playing an auditor. It's the whole play in one woman."

"Mr Tull, I am an auditor," Piq said, and it was true, and the truth sat oddly in her mouth.

"Of course you are, dear. That's why it works."

---

The cast drifted in over the next quarter hour. Halloran came, carrying his own binder. He was to play the Ambassador, a man of few lines, all of them about protocol, which he had already annotated. Dagny Wirth came as stage manager. She had a hub-chief's way of moving, all small corrections and no wasted thrust, and she took the scripts from Ambrose and redistributed them in order without being asked. Two postdocs from Hana's team came giggling. A farm-deck couple came, and a boy of about twelve who was to play a messenger and had been told he could carry a real envelope.

Osei Brandt came a little after the others, headset round his neck and cardigan buttoned wrong. He took the loop opposite Piq and fastened his foot in it as though it were a workstation.

"Telegraphist," Ambrose announced. "Small part. Pivotal. You decode the message in act two, Mr Brandt."

"I'd be decoding it from a store-and-forward relay," Osei said. "Realistically. There'd be a queue."

"There's no queue in my play."

"There's always a queue," Osei said mildly, and Piq marked the way he watched the hatch.

Hana Szelig arrived last. She did not drift in. She came through the hatch hand over hand on the rim rail like someone climbing a ladder she disliked, in the same dark jacket she had worn at the briefing, with a cardboard tube under one arm. She stopped when she saw the room was full.

"I was told," she said to Ambrose, in a voice built to carry to the back of a lecture hall, "that this was a hazard review for stage rigging."

"And so it is!" Ambrose spread his arms and turned a slow quarter-turn in the air. "Dr Szelig, you will play Professor Ilse Vantongeren, inventor of the Heliograph, a device that can turn the Sun itself against the inner worlds. You have four scenes, a cape, and a death."

"No."

"Your contempt reads to the back row. I watched you at lunch tell a man his soup was 'thermally naive.' I can't get that from an amateur."

"I'm not—"

"Item 233," Dagny said from the scripts, without looking up. "Passenger wellbeing. Senior science staff are encouraged to participate in one social activity per transit. You missed the quiz."

Hana looked at Dagny and then at the room. Last of all she looked at Piq, who was holding the rim loop with one foot and floating at a slight angle, entirely at ease, with a script in her hand that said *AUDITOR VESNA CRANE (SECRETLY AGENT NIGHTJAR)*.

Something happened in Hana's face that Piq did not know how to read. It wasn't quite amusement or surrender. Then she hooked a foot into the loop nearest the hatch, as far from Ambrose as the blister allowed, and held out her hand for a script.

---

*Dead Drop at Perihelion* was very bad. It was bad in a way Piq came, over the next hour, to admire, the way she admired well-made locks. Every scene was built to be reached through the wrong door. An Ambassador mistook a cipher for a dinner menu. A messenger carried a vital envelope to the wrong ship. The Heliograph's plans were hidden in a crate of eggs, and Ambrose noted that this would need "livestock liaison". The villain, Baron Stroud, a water magnate, was played by Ambrose himself, because, he explained, no one else could do the laugh.

"'Your water,'" Ambrose read, in the voice Piq had heard at a bulkhead at one in the morning, "'is as *hollow* as your heart, Ambassador.'"

"'I must consult the protocol,'" read Halloran, with feeling.

The postdocs snorted. The farm-deck woman laughed so hard she drifted out of her loop and Dagny reeled her back by the ankle. The boy read his messenger lines as if they were a threat.

Then they reached Crane.

Auditor Vesna Crane entered in act one, scene three, to inspect the Ambassador's embassy, and her first speech was a lie. She said she was there about the plumbing. Piq read it the way she thought a spy in a farce would read it, with a dry edge and a knowing glance at the audience, slightly too smooth.

"'I'm only here about the plumbing, Ambassador. Purely routine.'"

"Stop," said Ambrose.

She stopped.

"Again. Less."

She read it again with less.

"No, no." He drifted toward her with one hand out, as if the line were a bird he wanted to stop flying. "You're *lying*, dear. One can see it from Luna. You're telling us she's lying."

"She is lying."

"Of course she is. But you're too obviously lying." He put his face close to hers with his lens swinging. "Real liars don't sound like that. Real liars sound bored. They sound like a woman reading out the plumbing schedule. The best liar I ever knew was my second husband, and he lied exactly the way he ordered breakfast." He snapped his fingers. "Breakfast. Again."

Across the blister, Hana laughed.

It was short, surprised out of her, and quickly put away, but Piq heard it. Hana looked down at her script as if it had said something to her, and colour came up under the dark of her jaw.

Piq read the line again. She read it the way she had read *IHB-A-4471, active since 2191* to an immigration officer on Earth's elevator, the way she had told a rigger that old Lunar haulers had zero-g hubs, the way she had told Ambrose about a bench in the galley ten minutes ago. It was flat, a little tired, and faintly put-upon.

"'I'm only here about the plumbing, Ambassador. Purely routine.'"

The blister went quiet.

"*Yes*," said Ambrose softly. "There she is."

Nobody was laughing. Osei was looking at her with his head tilted, the way he looked at panel edges. Halloran had lowered his binder. Hana was not looking at her script any more. She was looking at Piq, and her expression had nothing to do with the play.

Piq made herself smile, the smile of a woman embarrassed to be good at something silly, and turned the page.

---

They broke at 20:45. Ambrose collected the scripts, because paper did not crash and also could not be trusted out of his sight, and gave notes to everyone. Halloran was to find a better protocol voice. The boy was to stop threatening people. Osei was to stop correcting the transmission scene. Piq was to be less brilliant at the start of act two "or they'll guess." He left in a slow tumble of plum velvet, still talking. The postdocs went with him. Dagny stowed the folded chairs that had never been unfolded, gave Piq a look Piq could not read, and went back to her hub.

Osei stayed a moment, looking out at the Earth-point.

"It's a decent signal, the Earth," he said, to no one in particular. "This close. Ten seconds out. Lovely clean link. Gets worse every day from here." He unhooked his foot. "Night watch. Ten o'clock."

"You're on tonight?"

"I'm always on." He said it without self-pity, as a fact about a queue. He pushed off for the hatch.

Hana was still there, by the hatch, holding her tube.

"You're very good at that," she said.

"Reading menus."

"Lying." She did not make it a joke. She pushed off the rim and drifted across the blister, not gracefully. She let her shoulder take the stop against a strut and winced, then hooked an elbow through the loop beside Piq. Up close she smelled of coolant and cold metal and something like oranges. "Is it the job? Auditors. Do you train it? People say yes to you in rooms and you need to know which yes is real."

"Something like that."

"Hm." Hana looked out at the stars. She was clearly working herself up to something, and Piq waited, because waiting got more from people than asking did. "Tab seven," Hana said finally. "Orange. Your note on the controls review. You asked for stray field at the shelter."

Piq had asked for it on the first day, in passing. It was item 140 on the checklist, *personnel field exposure*, and she had not expected an answer before the lab inspection.

"I did."

"I don't like answering questions in writing when people misunderstand pictures." Hana pulled the cap off the tube with her teeth and shook out a roll of heavy plotter paper. In the hub it didn't fall. It hung open between them in a long slow curl until Piq caught the far edge and held it flat against the curve of the glass.

The lines on it were beautiful. Piq hadn't expected that.

It was a cross-section through the hub, drawn to scale. The long cylinder of the hub ran up the page with its tanks shaded blue and the shelter a pale rectangle at its heart. The solar array at the sunward cap was a thick bar at the top, labelled with a small sun symbol. Around the waist of the hub, a dark ring was cut through in section: the Umbrella's coil, two black ovals at either side of the cylinder, like eyes. Inboard of them, tucked against the tank walls above and below the shelter, were four smaller ovals hatched the other way, each labelled *BUCK* in pencil. From the big ovals the field lines poured out in great nested loops, dense near the coil and wider and wider outward, curving up over the sunward cap and down under the antisunward cap and back into the coil from the other side. They had been plotted by a machine and labelled by hand in Hana's narrow upright figures: *10 T*, *1 T*, *0.1 T*, a bundle of lines marked *design rigidity cut-off*, and a broad zone outside the coil drawn in light hatching labelled *PROTECTED VOLUME (equatorial)*.

"Here." Hana's finger, nail bitten short, touched the pale rectangle of the shelter. "That's where people are. Which is the problem, because a ring coil is strongest dead in its own middle. Left alone, the main winding would put several tesla straight through the shelter. So we cheat." She tapped the hatched ovals. "Bucking windings, two pairs, run counter to the main coil and tuned so their field cancels its field over the shelter volume. It costs us a little outside, and it costs a lot of conductor. But inside the bore it's under a millitesla at the shelter. You could put a pacemaker in there and it wouldn't notice. That's your number." She tapped a small table in the corner, three significant figures, signed and dated. "And here—" her finger swept out across the hatched zone along the equator, "is where the field turns a proton round. That's the shield. Anything coming in sideways at storm energies gets bent back out. The tanks catch what gets through. Together, a sliver of what water alone would let in."

"It's lovely," Piq said, and meant it, and saw Hana hear that she meant it.

But she was looking at the top of the page.

At the sunward cap, above the array, the field lines from both sides of the coil swept up, bent in, and met. They did not cross, because field lines never did. They crowded. They ran close together along the axis and dived down toward the hub in a narrowing throat, a funnel with its mouth open to the Sun. The same thing happened at the bottom of the page, under the antisunward cap. The bucking windings flattened the field inside the bore and did nothing to the throats beyond it. Two throats, top and bottom. Two places where the shield's lines did not wall the hub off but pointed into it, straight down the axis of the ship, toward the pale rectangle where everyone would go when the sky went bad.

She did not need to know the plasma physics. She was Ceres-born, and every shed hand at Occator did a dosimetry course before the first shift topside. The first slide was always the same one: a single field line with a proton corkscrewing along it. The instructor's voice was always the same too, bored and certain: *They follow the lines. You want to know where your dose comes from, look where the lines go.* The lines here went straight in.

Hana was talking about the cryostat. Piq let her talk, held the edge of the paper, and kept her face exactly as it had been. That took no effort. It never did.

She did not say *what about the poles*. She did not say it because Sabine's letter said *assess capability* and a weakness was the most useful thing an assessment could find. She did not say it because something the Ledger had built in her said a fact kept was worth more than a fact given away. She did not say it because Hana might already know, and asking would show what Piq knew. She did not say it, and the not saying sat in her like a swallowed stone.

"You're quiet," Hana said.

"I'm reading." Piq let the paper roll back toward her. "Can I have a copy of this?"

Hana hesitated. Then, briskly: "It's in the binder. Green, p11. Low resolution." She rolled the plot and slid it back into its tube. "This one's mine." She capped it. Then, as if it cost her, and without looking at Piq: "The rivet trick. It works on the transfer elevator. I used it this morning."

"Eyes before head."

"Breathe out longer." The ghost of the laugh again, faster this time. Then she was pushing for the hatch, hand over hand, not gracefully, and gone down the antisunward corridor toward the dark end where her lab door was. Piq watched the corridor lights for a while after she had gone.

---

At 22:40 in C-41, with the ache back in her hips and the bunk's restraint strap unfastened because she could never sleep with it on, Piq opened Teodor Vasz's daybook.

His hand was small and cramped and leaned hard to the right. He used a fine pencil he must have sharpened with a knife, because the line varied. Most of the book was what the digital log should have been: tank levels by section, draws, rechecks, gauge complaints. He wrote *sticky again, bastard* and *xfer* and *trans.* He had a tic of drawing a small box around a number he had checked twice.

After day 150 of the long loop the boxes started to multiply.

By day 190 the tank levels in the paper log stopped. The digital log, the clean one, carried on happily with its 96s and 97s and 98s. The paper book had no level readings at all, just dates and short notes: *gauges as panel*, *gauges as panel*, *gauges as panel*, as if he had stopped believing the panel and had not yet decided what to believe instead. Here and there was a small stroke in the margin. She took out her own notes and laid them beside the book and compared the strokes to the dates of the scratched tally behind her locker hinge. Several matched. The last stroke was on day 231.

She found it on the page for day 214. Under *gauges as panel* there was a smudged arithmetic of figures she could not quite read, crossed out, and in the margin, in that cramped right-leaning pencil, boxed twice:

*D/H?*

She looked at it for a long time.

Dagny-Halloran? Two names, two people. It might be someone to ask, or someone to suspect. Dump-heater, a coil term she had heard at the controls review. Deck/hub, a quartermaster's shorthand for the two reservoirs he moved water between. Or the ratio, the oldest one, the number every Compact assay ticket printed in its top right corner beside the purity grade. She had seen it on ten thousand tickets in the Occator export sheds. Deuterium to hydrogen.

But why would a cycler's quartermaster question the D/H of his own water? It was Compact water, certified at the shed and certified again at the tank. Its isotope ratio was the one fact about it that never changed. Water could be lost, stolen, spilled, boiled off, burned. It didn't change what it was made of.

She wrote all four readings in her notes and ranked none of them. She didn't like that, so she closed the daybook and set it on the shelf beside the sleep tracker.

She had three bursts. The first came at 02:07 on day two, short at four hundred milliseconds and broadband, as if a door had been opened and shut. The second came at 02:00:07, longer at 1.7 seconds, with the lights sagging on Bus B. The third came at 02:13:41, 1.8 seconds, Bus B dipping three percent and recovering inside two seconds, Buses A and C clean. All three fell in the hour after midnight, all three were inside the ship, and the last two were on the same bus as the comms office, the blister, the lab and the console room. Osei Brandt was on watch from 22:00 to 04:00 on every one of those nights. *I'm always on.*

The lab was on Bus B too. She made herself look at that, because the Ledger also trained you to look at the thing you didn't want to. The morning after 02:13 Hana had come into the controls review yawning, a real jaw-cracking yawn that she had covered with the back of her wrist and not apologised for. Piq turned it over and set it down. Hana's team kept the cryostat on a watch rota. A postdoc had complained about it at lunch: four-hour checks, alarms that woke whoever had the pager, a helium boil-off curve that didn't care what time it was. A physicist yawning was not a signal. Half the science deck yawned. And the bursts were encrypted-looking, timed and patient, built to be missed. Hana Szelig, who corrected strangers' soup, did not strike her as a woman who did anything that could be missed.

Then there was Teodor, who had kept a paper book because he did not trust the screens. He had stopped writing down levels because he did not believe them, and he had gone out at night without a spotter to look at something he couldn't trust any panel to show him. Someone had migrated his format two days after his death, and someone had impounded his effects so they could not go home.

Whatever was talking from inside the ship spoke between two and a quarter past two. It spoke from Bus B.

Piq took the receiver from the shelf and checked its charge. She clipped it into her sleeve pocket. She set the cabin chime for 01:20, which gave her the elevator, the hub, and twenty minutes to find somewhere dark on Bus B with a sightline to the comms office hatch and the console room door.

She lay down on the bunk without the strap, with the ring's 0.38 g pressing her into the mattress like a hand. Above her, the forty-page protocol lay on its shelf, initials page signed. Somewhere outside the hull, the Sun was behind her and Mars was ahead. The board in the galley said 11:42:49, and it had said something different on day two, and it would go on saying whatever the orbit told it, whoever wished otherwise.

She did not sleep. She lay there and counted the seconds of the ring's slow turn, thirty-five and a bit to every revolution, and waited for 01:20.
