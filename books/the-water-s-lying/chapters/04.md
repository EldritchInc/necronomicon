# Chapter 4: Housekeeping

The bug was the size of a lentil and cost more than Piq's first year of salary. It was a disc of piezo film backed with gel, built to stick to the inside wall of a duct and listen to the whole ship through it. Ducts carried voices better than most people knew. A ship's ventilation was one long ear, and the Ledger had spent a great deal of money learning how to put something at the end of it.

She held it between thumb and forefinger at 10:40 on her fourth morning aboard, floating in the hub's axial corridor beside access panel H-14. She was thinking about Osei Brandt.

He had told her, kindly and at length, exactly how messages moved through the conjunction queue. He sat on the late watch most nights and didn't sleep much. He tapped the edge of every panel he touched as though counting packets. The comms station in the shelter ran off Bus B, and so did the lights that had sagged at 02:00:07. A comms officer was the person best placed to hide a burst, and the person best placed to explain one away. Anyone who could talk that warmly about stripped headers knew exactly what a header was worth with the postmark torn off.

That was one reason. The other was Ambrose Tull.

She had tried tailing him after breakfast, which had seemed a modest ambition for a trained officer against an eighty-one-year-old in a plum waistcoat.

At breakfast he had checked the room in the curved steel of the urn before he sat. He took the chair with its back to the bulkhead and wrote in pencil in a paper notebook, and paper was the one medium nobody aboard could read remotely. When he left, Piq left forty seconds later, with her tray, and followed him anticlockwise around the ring.

The ring did not want her following anyone. At 0.38 g every stride came down harder than her body expected and landed a fraction late, so that she walked like someone descending stairs that weren't there. Ahead of her Ambrose walked as if he had been born on the Moon and found everything since then a little rich. He stopped at a polished stretch of bulkhead outside the gym and studied his reflection, adjusting his collar with great care. Piq recognised it at once as a man checking behind him.

She turned aside into the nearest open hatch.

It was the laundry. Warm air, the smell of hot lint and citrus soap, and a young crewman with a stack of folded towels as tall as his chin, who looked at her with relief.

"Twelve for C-deck," he said, and put them in her arms. "You're late. Ofori's been asking."

"I'm the auditor," Piq said.

"Then you can audit them on the way." He went back to the folding table.

When she stepped out, Ambrose was no longer at the bulkhead. He was coming back toward her, very quickly for his age, and he stopped a metre away and looked at the towels, then at her face, then at the towels again.

"Ms Ansari-Holm," he said. "Auditing the linen?"

"Item one-seventy-one," Piq said. "Textile hygiene."

There was an item 171. It covered biofilm in humid storage. It said nothing about carrying anything.

Ambrose leaned close. He smelled of pencil shavings and an old-fashioned cologne. "Did you see anyone? Behind me. Just now."

"Only me."

"Hm," Ambrose said. He took out the notebook, wrote a line without letting her see it, and went on his way at his own speed. He did not look back, which was either innocence or very good training.

Piq delivered the towels to C-deck, where a woman called Ofori told her she was late.

In anyone else she would have called his behaviour tradecraft. In an eighty-one-year-old playwright she called it a possibility, and disliked herself for it.

He had also seen her at Hana's door last night, with her picks barely back in her sleeve, as he came round the curve declaiming somebody's death scene to the empty corridor. She didn't know how much he had seen. She knew how little she had found. The dark lab had been exactly that: three consoles locked to a biometric she didn't have, a cryostat readout reporting 4.2 kelvin, which was what a coil in cold soak was supposed to report, and frost furring the coolant lines, as it would on any night of the voyage. There was a field-line printout pinned to the wall with no date on it. There was no transmitter, no hidden antenna, nothing warm. If a transmitter existed anywhere in Hana's lab, it wasn't where a lockpick could reach it, and the burst had come from somewhere.

The lentil could settle one of those questions. H-14 was the junction where the shelter's return air met the main run toward the bridge, so anything said loudly at the comms panel would come down this branch. With a night of recording she would know whether Osei talked to anyone at two in the morning, or only to his queue.

She checked the corridor both ways. It was empty: flat light, the smell of water and cold stone, the fans' steady breath. The panel came off with a quarter-turn of four captive screws. Behind it the duct throat was square and grey. A damper sat halfway along on its little actuator, with a flow vane beside it like a weathercock. The air moved past her fingers at the speed of a sigh.

She peeled the gel backing, held the lentil up to the airflow, and let it go.

The air should have carried it a few centimetres and pressed it face-first against the duct wall. That was the physics in the manual. The manual had been written on Vesta, where the Board trained its auditors and through whose stores the Ledger quietly routed its kit, so that everything Piq carried came stamped as Board inspection equipment. Nobody on Vesta had been inside the Aubade, and its duct walls carried a film of condensate that had been building for nineteen years. The gel touched, failed to grip, and skated. The lentil tumbled off the wall in a slow wobble, rode the airstream two hand-spans downstream, and lodged neatly in the hinge of the flow vane like a seed caught in a tooth.

The vane stopped.

Piq watched it not move for one long second.

Somewhere above her a soft two-tone chime sounded, polite as a waiter. A panel light by the hatch turned from green to amber. A voice that had been recorded by someone who had clearly never been in a hurry said, "Ventilation junction H-fourteen. Flow sensor fault. Damper reverting to safe position." With a small, satisfied clunk, the damper swung fully open.

Air changed direction around her ankles. The duct seemed to breathe in. Down the branch from the bridge, carried with startling clarity by a pipe that had suddenly connected two rooms that were never meant to be connected, came a man's voice.

"—not asking you to sign anything irregular, Maelis. I'm asking you to sign a number."

The voice was warm and well modulated, the voice of someone accustomed to being agreed with in meeting rooms. Piq had never heard it before. She knew whose it was anyway, the way you know a ship by its silhouette.

"You're asking me to sign a trim budget that doesn't appear in the published schedule." Captain Odum sounded tired and very precise. "That makes it a number I have to explain."

"It's housekeeping."

"Then housekeeping can sign it."

Piq realised she had stopped breathing. She started again quietly and pressed her shoulder against the frame of the access hatch so she wouldn't drift. Her receiver was in her sleeve pocket. It was useless here, a broadband ear tuned for transmissions, not voices. The bug was in the duct, but it was jammed in a vane, and she had no way of knowing what it was hearing. She had her own memory. That was going to have to do.

"Maelis." The warm voice dropped half a register, and the duct carried that too. "We have been through this. The Board doesn't need to see attitude trim. Nobody files attitude trim. You'll have an auditor reading line items she doesn't understand and writing us up for a—"

"Three signatures." It came flat, from a woman who had said it before. "Mine, the chief engineer's and the navigator's, for any propellant allocation outside the published plan. Section thirty-one. I wrote it."

"I know you wrote it."

"Then you know I'm not going to be the first person to break it." A pause. "Get me two more signatures, Lucan, and I'll sign. Until then it's a number in a folder, and the folder stays in your office."

The warm voice laughed, and it was a nice laugh, which made it worse. "You'd rather wait for the paper than—"

The chime sounded again. The damper actuator whined, tried to close, found it could not trust its sensor, and opened again with a clank. The voices became the shapes of voices, then the fans, then nothing.

Piq stayed where she was, holding the hatch frame, while the amber light pulsed.

Trim budget. Propellant outside the published plan. Something that needed three signatures and was being offered with one. A director of operations who called it housekeeping, and a captain who would not keep the house.

It could be nothing. Ships trimmed attitude all the time. The Aubade held its spin axis sunward, and that took tiny corrections through the electrothermal thrusters, and every correction burned water. The water was booked, budgeted and published, because water was shielding and shielding was life. Nobody files attitude trim, Ferro had said. That was true. Nobody needed to hide it, either, and he was hiding it.

She pushed the thought into her pocket next to the receiver.

The bug was still wedged in the vane. She reached into the duct throat and felt for it with two fingers. Her hand was half inside when she heard a carabiner click behind her, back along the axial corridor, the confident snap of someone who handled clips the way other people handled pens.

Piq took her hand out of the duct. She had the lentil pinched between her fingers. She pressed it into the cuff of her sleeve and turned, already a little embarrassed, an auditor caught doing an auditor's thing.

It was the rigger, the one with the cropped sandy hair and black tape over her name tape. She came up the corridor hand over hand without hurrying, a toolbag strapped to her hip, and stopped a body length away with one boot hooked under a rail. Her pale grey eyes took in the open panel, the amber light, the four captive screws Piq had threaded onto her knuckle like rings, and Piq.

"H-fourteen," the rigger said.

"It started chiming," Piq said. "I was passing. I thought I'd look before I wrote it up. Item two-forty-four is ventilation fault response."

That was true. There was an item 244. It had nothing to do with whether auditors should open access panels with their own hands, which they shouldn't.

The rigger looked at the vane, now swinging freely in the restored airflow, and then at Piq's sleeve.

"Fault cleared," she said. "Must've been ice."

"Must have been."

The rigger said nothing else. She pulled a tablet from her bag, tapped the fault closed, and fitted the panel back over the throat with four quick turns of a driver. Piq handed her the screws one at a time from her knuckle, like a nurse passing instruments, and felt a fool. When the panel was on, the rigger looked at her for exactly as long as she had looked at her after the hen. Then she turned and went back the way she had come. She didn't push off. She climbed, as if speed were a conversation she didn't want to start.

Piq went to the nearest hygiene alcove, unrolled her sleeve, and looked at the lentil.

The gel was smeared with grey condensate. The piezo film had creased across the middle. When she held it to her receiver and pressed the test sequence, it gave her a single long rattle, the sound of a flow vane trying to turn against a stone. It had recorded three minutes and forty seconds of that and nothing else. The grinding had driven the film to full scale from the moment the vane jammed, so two people arguing a few metres up the duct had been, to the bug, a silence under a scream.

The bug had failed completely. It had also given her a trim budget, which was better. She didn't yet know why it was better, and that kept her quiet all through lunch.

Orange tab seven scheduled the controls and protection review for 14:00 in B-7, with attendance limited to the auditor, the lead physicist and one systems representative. Beneath that, in a typeface somebody had chosen on purpose: *Questions outside the scope of the binder will be noted and addressed in writing.*

The systems representative was the quiet older man, the power systems lead, who had a soft Tharsis accent and a habit of laying his hands flat on the table as though calming it. The shaved-head woman with the straw came too, though the tab said one. She sat in the corner and moved her lips with page numbers, and Hana didn't send her out.

"Quench protection," Piq said, and opened the binder at red fourteen.

"Quench detection," Hana said, "is page fourteen. Protection is pages twenty-two and twenty-three. You'll want both, so you'll want to keep two fingers in."

Piq kept two fingers in.

The design was good. Her first discomfort was how good it was. A superconducting coil carried its current with no resistance at all, so long as every centimetre of it stayed cold. If one centimetre warmed past its critical temperature, that centimetre became an ordinary resistor carrying an extraordinary current. It heated, the heat spread, and more of the coil went normal. The energy stored in the field, which was enormous and patient, would try to leave through that one hot spot. A quench was the magnet's version of a dam bursting at a crack. Protection meant detecting the crack fast and opening other gates before the water found it.

Voltage taps ran along the windings, page fourteen. If a section showed a resistive voltage above threshold for longer than the validation window, the system called a quench. On page twenty-two, it fired heaters along the whole winding so that the coil went normal everywhere at once and spread the heat. On page twenty-three, it switched the current out of the coil into a bank of dump resistors, which turned the field's energy into heat. That heat ran by bypass line to the radiators on the darkside hub cradle, green nine, and from there into the dark.

"Stored energy at full field," Piq said.

"One point nine gigajoules."

Piq did the arithmetic without meaning to. It was roughly half a tonne of chemical explosive, held in a ring of cold wire round the waist of the hub, a few metres from a shelter where two hundred and twenty people were supposed to go when the Sun turned against them.

"Dump time constant," she said.

"Thirty-eight seconds to one over e." Hana didn't look at the binder. "Peak terminal voltage under eight hundred volts. Dump resistor peak temperature under five hundred kelvin with margin. It's in the table."

"It's in the table as calculated."

The power lead's hands went a little flatter on the table.

"It's in the table," Hana said, "as calculated, as modelled, and as tested at full scale on the ground at Tharsis Station, eleven times, with Board witnesses. Black tab, item four. You'll find the witness signatures on the facing page, unless you've decided the Board's witnesses are also suspect."

"I've decided nothing," Piq said. "I'm asking what happens if it quenches during a storm."

"It doesn't quench during a storm."

"Everything quenches eventually."

"Not because of a storm. Particle heating on the windings at storm flux is two orders of magnitude below the margin." Hana's voice had gone quiet, which in her was worse than loud. "A quench would come from a mechanical event, a cryo failure or a control error. Those are pages twenty-six, twenty-seven and thirty respectively. And if it quenches during a storm, then the coil sheds its energy into the radiators in thirty-eight seconds and the ship loses the active shield and falls back on its water, the way it would if the Umbrella had never been built. Which is your preferred outcome anyway."

The woman with the straw stopped mouthing page numbers.

"My preferred outcome," Piq said evenly, "is not to find that a magnet holding half a tonne of explosive's worth of energy depends for its safety on detection thresholds nobody has validated in flight. A shield that can't fail safely is a bomb strapped to the shelter."

She heard herself say it and knew it was the right thing to say, because it was an auditor's sentence through and through. She also knew she would have said it even if she hadn't believed it, because the Ledger needed to know whether the thing could fail, and how. She couldn't separate the two, and she noticed that she couldn't, and she put the noticing away with everything else.

Hana looked at her for a long moment.

"You wrote that sentence before you came in," Hana said.

"I wrote it on Vesta." That was true as well. The Board's certification course had made her write one hazard statement a day for eight months, and this had been one of the better ones.

"I believe you." Hana turned to fourteen and pushed the binder across the table so the page faced Piq. "Detection self-test. Bottom of the page. The detection circuit injects a pulse across each tap pair to confirm the taps are alive. In a live magnet the self-test runs continuously, two seconds maximum per pulse, at a variable interval set by the field conditions. It's in your checklist as item one-nineteen, and you'll be witnessing it after the Mars flyby, along with everything else. When the coil is live. Which it isn't." She sat back. "Is there anything else outside the scope of the binder?"

Piq read the paragraph. *Self-test pulse: ≤2 s. Interval: adaptive.* It was a good design. A coil in cold soak wouldn't need it. She ticked item 119 as *noted; to witness*, and moved on to the cryo bypass, where the arguing was easier.

Twice more Hana answered a question by repeating it back with a page number attached, as though stating where something was written settled whether it was true. Once she was right in a way that made Piq feel clumsy, about the heater firing sequence, and Piq conceded it out loud, and Hana looked briefly startled. Once Piq was right, about a missing calibration date on the voltage tap amplifiers, and Hana wrote it on a slip of paper, flagged the page with a fresh orange tab and said, without looking up, that it would be addressed in writing.

"By when?" Piq said.

"By when it's addressed."

The power lead took his hands off the table, which seemed to be how he adjourned meetings. The woman with the straw left with him, glancing back once at Piq as if checking the auditor was still the same shape.

Hana closed the binder and squared it against the table edge. "I'm going up," she said, meaning the hub. "If you're going to the shelter, it's the same car. I'd rather you didn't take notes in it."

"I'll hold them in," Piq said.

---

The spin-transfer elevator was a padded cylinder with two fold-down seats, a strap for each, and a ring of handholds round the ceiling, which would become the floor if anyone lost track of things. A strip display by the door showed the car's distance from the spin axis in metres, and beside it the local acceleration in hundredths of a g, for anyone who liked to watch their weight leave them.

Piq liked watching it. At 120 metres the display read 0.38, and her knees and the small of her back read it too. The car started up the spoke with a soft hum. As it climbed, Coriolis took hold. The car was being dragged toward the axis but still carried the ring's sideways speed, so the wall leaned into her shoulder as though someone were sitting too close. Her weight began to drain away. At ninety metres she felt her hips unclench a little. At seventy she noticed her jaw.

At sixty-one metres the car stopped with a jolt that went up through the seat into her teeth.

The display held at 0.19. Half her weight was gone, which was still far too much.

"Car two," said the recorded voice, as unhurried as the one in the duct. "Brake interlock fault. Please remain seated. Maintenance has been notified."

Hana pressed the intercom stud. "Szelig in car two. How long?"

A live voice came through, a man chewing something. "Interlock's tripped on both sides. We'll try a jog to the upper landing, Doctor. Hold on to something."

The car lurched upward, stopped, lurched again. At each jog the wall shoved Piq sideways and released her, and the number on the display dropped as if someone were spending it: 0.14, 0.09, 0.06. At the last jog the brakes caught with a bang that rang through the spoke, and the display settled on 10 metres and 0.03.

"Holding there," said the man. "Brake's clamped and it's not coming off till we swap the solenoid. Hour, maybe. Don't touch the door."

Ten metres from the axis gave a thirty-sixth of the ring's pull... no. Piq redid it, because she was tired of being careless today. Ten over a hundred and twenty was a twelfth. A twelfth of 0.38 was 0.032. Ceres gave 0.029.

Home, near enough. Off by a rounding error.

Her body knew it before her arithmetic finished. The ache in her hips, which had been living there for four days like a lodger, packed up and left. Her spine lengthened by some fraction she could feel. Her hands lifted off her thighs by themselves and stayed lifted, light as cloth, and she let go of the strap and let her weight settle onto the seat the way it settled onto every seat she had ever known before the taxi. She took a breath to the bottom of her lungs without bracing. Then she realised she was smiling, and stopped, and then didn't stop.

Across the car, Hana had both hands on the ceiling ring and her eyes shut.

"Are you all right?"

"Fine." Hana's voice came from somewhere behind her teeth. "Don't move your head."

"I wasn't."

"You were. You turned to look at me. I felt it." She opened her eyes and immediately shut them again. "Your head is going round at one point six eight rpm, and so is mine, and at the ring my body has some weight to set that against, and in the hub I'm floating and it doesn't matter. Here it's neither. Every time anything turns, my inner ear thinks the car is falling over sideways."

Piq looked at her properly. Hana was pale under the light, with a fine sheen on her upper lip, and her knuckles had gone white on the ring. The woman who had answered every question for an hour with a page number did not, apparently, have a page for this.

"You're Mars-born," Piq said.

"Olympus Rise. Point three eight my whole life. I can do zero, zero's honest. This," Hana said, "is a lie."

"It's Ceres," Piq said.

Hana opened one eye.

"Point oh three. That's what it is out there, give or take. This is what I grew up in." Piq unhooked her boots from the seat rail, which was the first time in four days she'd trusted her own weight to do anything sensible, and leaned forward very slowly. "Look at the door seam. Not at me. Pick one rivet and keep it. Move your eyes before your head, always. If you have to turn, turn from the hips, slowly, and keep the rivet."

"That's your advice. Look at a rivet."

"That's what we tell children on the transit cars at Occator. And breathe out longer than you breathe in."

Hana found a rivet. Piq watched her breathe out, long, and in, short, and out again. Some colour came back into her face.

"You're good at this," Hana said, still looking at the rivet.

"I'm good at this," Piq agreed. "I'm bad at your ring. I've walked into the galley door twice. I can't put a cup down. It lands before I've finished deciding to let go of it."

"I saw you with the cup." Something in Hana's mouth moved. "On the first day. I thought you were doing it to make a point."

"What point?"

"That the ship was badly designed."

"Everything's badly designed," Piq said, "if you weren't born in it."

The man on the intercom said something about a solenoid to somebody else, then went quiet. The car ticked as it cooled. Down the spoke, very faintly, the ring's fans hummed, the sound of a whole world turning slowly somewhere beneath their feet.

"What's it like?" Hana asked. "Ceres. I've only seen it on the water-rate charts."

Piq could have given the cover answer. She had one: a pleasant, boring account of a hydrology technician's childhood, rehearsed until it was smooth. She opened her mouth for it, and what came out instead was the truth.

"Tunnels," she said. "Warrens. You live under three hundred metres of ice and rock, because that's the shielding. Everything's lit from the ceiling. The ceilings in the old warrens are painted blue, because somebody's great-grandmother remembered what a sky looked like, but it's paint, and you can see the brush marks. You walk by pushing off. Children fly down the main bores in a sort of long, slow fall, and you can hold a conversation with someone in midair across a whole plaza." She stopped. "I'd never been anywhere with weather until I was twenty-six. I'd never seen the sky."

"You've seen space."

"Space isn't sky. Space is out there. Sky is over you."

Hana was quiet for a while. When she spoke, her voice had changed and lost its edge.

"Mars has auroras," she said. "Did you know that?"

"I thought you needed a magnetic field."

"You need a field to steer the particles. Mars lost the global one a few billion years ago, but bits of it are frozen into the crust, old rock in the southern highlands that remembers which way north used to be. During a big storm the whole night sky goes faintly green, all over, and the first time you see it you think it's your eyes. You think you're tired. You wait, and the horizon sort of breathes." Her eyes stayed on the rivet. Her free hand came off the ceiling ring and shaped something in the air. "Then over the crustal fields you get curtains, real ones. Not big like Earth's, but they move. They hang down exactly where the field lines open and let the particles in, so you're watching the shape of the field drawn on the sky. Where the field opens, the sky lights. Tomas and I..." She stopped, and the hand went back to the ring. "My brother and I used to drive out to Sirenum on storm nights to watch. Three hours each way in a rover with bad heating, to see green."

"I would like to see that," Piq said, and heard that she meant it, which surprised her more than anything else that day.

"You'd have to come to Mars."

"I'd have to stand up on Mars."

"You'd be on the floor in a week." Hana smiled at the rivet. "I'd prop you up."

Neither of them said anything after that. In the long quiet Piq became aware that her hand had drifted, with nothing to hold it down, until it rested on the ceiling ring a few centimetres from Hana's. She could have moved it. At 0.03 g every movement was a decision. She left it where it was.

Hana's knuckle touched hers when the car jolted.

"Brake's off," said the man on the intercom. "Taking you to the hub. Hold on."

The car hummed upward. The display ran down to zero, and the last of Piq's weight lifted off her like a sheet being drawn back. Hana let go of the ring, pushed off the moment the doors opened, and floated out into the hub corridor with relief all over her.

"Thank you," Hana said, turning in the air with the ease of somebody whose body had remembered the rules. "For the rivet." Then her face closed, not unkindly, the way a hatch closes for pressure. "The calibration dates will be addressed in writing."

"I look forward to it."

Hana went one way and Piq went the other. Only at the shelter hatch did Piq realise she had not lied once in the hour. She couldn't remember the last time that had happened, and she did not like how good it had felt.

At 02:13:41 the receiver in her pillow buzzed against her ear.

Piq was awake before she knew she was, already reaching for it. The screen showed a burst: broadband, structured, the same ragged shape as the one on the night of the hen. Duration 1.8 seconds. The first had been 1.7.

She rolled onto her side, which at 0.38 g took effort, and pulled up the ship's public power telemetry on her tablet. Her auditor's credential gave her read access to every bus on the ship, and she had been glad of it for four days. Bus B showed a dip at 02:13:41, three percent, recovering inside two seconds. Bus A was clean. Bus C was clean. Through the open shutter of her cabin's ring window there was nothing to see but stars, but somewhere up the spoke the hub's corridor lights would have sagged and recovered, and nobody awake to notice would have thought it worth logging.

She opened her notebook file and wrote it beside the first:

*02:00:07, 1.7 s, Bus B dip. 02:13:41, 1.8 s, Bus B dip.*

Then she pulled the watch roster. *Comms: O. Brandt, 22:00–04:00.* He had been on both nights.

Thirteen and a half minutes later than the last one, so it wasn't a schedule. Whoever was sending was choosing the moment, perhaps waiting for a relay window or for a quiet board. A comms officer would know when the relays were listening. A comms officer would know how short a burst had to be to get lost in the noise of a long night. A comms officer sat at a station on Bus B.

She lay back and looked at the ceiling. It was a ceiling, not a sky. The receiver was warm in her hand.

It still didn't fit perfectly. Nothing about Osei felt like a man who lied. But she had been told that about people before, by herself, and been wrong.

The morning audit briefing was at 08:00 in B-7, and Hana yawned through it.

It wasn't a theatrical yawn. She fought it down three times behind the back of her wrist and lost the fourth, and her eyes watered. She had a coffee bulb clipped to the binder and drank from it whenever the power lead spoke, which was seldom, so it lasted. There were faint shadows under her eyes, and her hair was tied back more severely than yesterday, as though discipline might stand in for sleep.

Osei wasn't there. He was off watch and presumably asleep, which was what a man would do after a long night at a quiet board.

On page fourteen of the binder there was a new orange tab. Behind it was a typed and signed table of calibration dates for every voltage tap amplifier on the coil, each with its certificate number, and a note at the bottom: *Addressed in writing. H.S., 01:50.*

Piq read the timestamp and understood the yawn. Hana had sat up half the night to answer one small question so completely that it could never be asked again, and in doing so she had paid for a single conceded point with a night's sleep. It was spite, and it was diligence, and in Hana the two were so close as to be the same thing.

"Thank you for this," Piq said, tapping the tab.

"It's in the binder," Hana said, and yawned again, and looked furious about it.

Piq ticked the item closed. She did not mention the rivet. Neither did Hana. When their eyes met over the table, Hana looked away first. Piq had no idea what to do with that, so she wrote *amplifier cal: closed* and went on to the cryo bypass.

She found the notebook at lunch.

Ambrose had come through the galley with a sheaf of paper, announcing to anyone who'd listen that the first full read-through of *Dead Drop at Perihelion* was at 19:00 in the hub observation blister and that attendance was compulsory for "everyone with a soul and several without". He had stopped at Piq's table, looked at her long enough to be strange, said "You especially, Ms Ansari-Holm," and swept on toward the tray return by a route that made no sense. He took the paper with him.

He left the notebook behind.

It lay on the bench where he had sat down for thirty seconds to retie a shoe. It was small and cloth-bound, with a pencil pushed into the spiral, and a thumb's width of the pages were grey with handling. Piq looked at it the way you look at an open hatch.

She told herself she would return it. She would take it to the read-through at 19:00 and hand it over with a little joke about auditors and found property, and he would be grateful. That was all true. She opened it anyway, because she had been opening things that weren't hers for eleven years and her hands had stopped asking permission.

Most of it was dialogue in a cramped, slanting hand, crossed out and rewritten. Stage directions: *She produces the microfilm from the hen.* A sketch of the blister with arrows. Then, in the middle, a page of names in a column, each with a few words beside it.

*BRANDT. Too obvious? Watches the hatch. Knows everything about letters.*

*The rigger, no name. Always there. ALWAYS THERE.*
