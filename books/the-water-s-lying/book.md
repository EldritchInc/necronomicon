# The Water's Lying

> Partial: 6 chapters written of 18 planned. This book is unfinished.

## Chapter 1: Vacated

At three gravities, Piq Ansari-Holm weighed about a hundred times what she had been born to.

She had done the arithmetic before the burn because arithmetic was what she did when she was frightened, and she was not frightened, she told herself. The taxi's couch had moulded itself around her like a fist. The g-suit squeezed her calves and thighs in slow pulses, milking blood back toward her heart. Her ribs felt like the slats of a crate with something heavy sitting on it. On Ceres a person could jump and spend most of a minute coming down. Here a person could not lift her own hand from the armrest. She had tried, once, at the start, to scratch her nose. The hand had risen a centimetre and then lain back down.

*Name.* The word floated at the edge of her vision, where the grey was coming in like fog off a crater lip. *Piq Ansari-Holm.*

*Credential.* IHB-A-4471, Interplanetary Habitability Board, auditor, class two, active since 2191. It was real, and that was the beauty of it. She had sat the examinations, a week of them in a cold room on Vesta, and passed in the top decile. She had audited the scrubber cascades on three Lunar Consortium freighters and failed one of them, which had made her briefly famous among people who cared about scrubber cascades. No one would ever find a seam in the credential, because it had none.

*Purpose of visit.* Certification audit of an experimental active radiation shielding installation, the Umbrella, aboard the Tharsis Transit cycler *Aubade*, for the fast leg Earth to Mars, 146 days.

*Employer.* The Board.

That was the lie, and it sat so comfortably in the sequence that she nearly passed it without a flicker. Her employer was the Ledger, which was the polite name and the only name for the intelligence service of the Ceres Hydrological Compact. Ceres sold water. Every crewed ship in the inner system wrapped its people in water because water stopped protons, and nobody yet sold more of it, cheaper, or cleaner than Ceres. The Umbrella was a superconducting coil that wanted to make water optional. Piq's instructions were to look at it and to report. If a later message told her to, she was to make sure it never worked.

There was a second instruction, and Sabine Okoro had delivered it last and softly, the way she delivered all the things that mattered. *Find out what happened to Teodor.*

The grey closed to a tunnel. Somewhere at the far end of it the pilot's voice said, "Forty seconds left on the burn. Doing all right back there?"

"Fine," her mouth said.

Her mind was already on the next card. *Next of kin.* Mother, Occator warren, Ceres. Father deceased. No partner, no dependants, no one aboard. Her eyes had begun to stream sideways into her ears, and the tunnel had narrowed to the width of a coin. *Medical restrictions: none. Prior travel on Tharsis carriers: none.* She was halfway through her vaccination history before it occurred to her that someone had asked her a question, and by then she couldn't remember what it had been.

---

When the burn cut, her body came up off the couch with a lightness so sudden and so total that she laughed out loud.

The pilot, a squat Earther named Rask who had spent the boost chewing something and saying nothing, twisted round in his straps to look at her. "First time at three?"

"First time this month."

"You went quiet. I had my hand on the cutoff." He held the hand up as proof. "I've had Belters black out on me. One threw up into her own helmet. You don't want to see that in zero g. Nobody wants to see that."

"I'm a professional," Piq said. She unstrapped and drifted a few centimetres off the couch, turned a lazy quarter-roll with two fingertips on the overhead, and caught herself on the bulkhead with no more effort than a sigh. Behind her, in the cargo net, a crate began to cluck.

"Pullets," Rask said, following her glance. "Eight of them. For the farm deck. They hate the burn worse than you do." He tipped his head toward the forward port. "There she is. Have a look before we start closing. She's ugly up close."

Piq pulled herself forward hand over hand and looked.

The *Aubade* was crossing the stars like a wheel lost off some enormous cart. The ring came first: two hundred and forty metres across, a band of grey modules linked into a hoop and turning with a slowness that fooled the eye. It went round once in a little over thirty-five seconds, and at the rim that was enough to pin a body down at 0.38 g. Mars normal, the brochures said, so passengers would arrive already acclimatised. Six spokes ran inward from the hoop to the hub. The hub did not turn at all. It was a fat cylinder, blunt at both ends, crusted with radiators and tankage. Its far cap was a disc of solar array, aimed at the Sun so steadily that the whole ship seemed to be listening for something there.

Around the hub's waist, like a ring on a finger, sat a dark torus in a cradle of struts. It was matte and wrapped in gold-grey blanketing. Frost glittered along the pipes that fed it.

*That's it,* she thought. *That's the thing I'm here to kill.*

It looked like a doughnut wearing a space blanket.

"The coil," Rask said. "The science people's toy. You here for that?"

"I'm here to make sure nobody gets hurt by it."

That one was nearly true, which made it the most dangerous kind. She watched the torus grow. The Ceres-born part of her, the part that had grown up under kilometres of ice and regolith and had never once seen an unfiltered sky, wanted to put a hand over the window. Out here there was only the hull between you and everything the Sun threw. Ceres knew exactly what that was worth, to the gram and the credit.

"Docking in nine," Rask said. "Strap back in. Hub port's on the dark end. She doesn't spin there, so you'll be happy, but the approach gets jerky."

---

The approach got jerky. The docking collar took them with a series of bangs that travelled up through the couch into her teeth. Then came the hiss and pop of equalising pressure, and a smell rolled in through the hatch that every ship had and every ship insisted it didn't: hot electronics, old sweat, a sour green note from the farm-deck air recyclers, and underneath it something like wet stone that she recognised from home. It was water, a lot of water, very close by.

The hatch opened on a reception chamber about the size of a Ceres family kitchen, cylindrical and padded and lit warm. Captain Maelis Odum floated in the middle of it with both feet hooked into a floor loop and a binder held against her chest with both arms.

She was somewhere past fifty. Her face had been handsome before it learned to look tired, and it still was. She wore a Tharsis Transit uniform pressed so sharply that it must have been pressed in gravity and carried up to the hub folded in a bag. Her hair was grey and cropped short enough that it couldn't misbehave in microgravity. It was the binder Piq noticed, though, because the captain held it the way some people held a baby, or a flag.

"Auditor Ansari-Holm." Odum unhooked one foot and offered a hand, and Piq took it. The captain's grip was dry and careful, a handshake that had been taught. "Welcome aboard the *Aubade*. I'm Captain Odum. I apologise for the reception party. The Director of Operations sends his regrets. Mr. Ferro is in conference with Tharsis and the window is narrow." Something crossed her face and was gone. "He'll be glad to meet you when he's free."

"Of course," Piq said warmly. "I'm the one intruding."

"Not at all. The Board is always welcome." Odum said it like a line from a document, which Piq would shortly learn it was. The captain uncurled her arms from the binder and presented it with both hands, the way a person presents an award. "Your welcome protocol."

Piq took it. Even in zero g it had a heft, a sense of mass resisting her as she brought it in. It was printed on real paper, cream-coloured and bound with three brass rings. The cover read *AUBADE: GUEST AND SUPERNUMERARY WELCOME PROTOCOL, REV. 14.* Under that, in smaller type: *Please read in full.* Under that, smaller still: *In full.*

"Forty pages," Odum said. "We print it. People read paper. They don't read screens. I've done studies." She paused. "Informal studies. There's an initials page at the back. Each section must be initialled to indicate you've read and understood it, and I'll need it returned to the purser's office within one ship-day. It's not bureaucracy." This had the polish of something said many times. "It's survival. Every page of that binder is there because something happened to somebody."

Piq opened it at random. *SECTION 4: HANDRAIL ETIQUETTE.* There was a diagram of two stick figures approaching each other along a corridor rail. One was labelled INBOARD HAND and the other was labelled YIELDING PARTY. Beneath it: *4.3: In corridors of the transition zone, the party moving spinward shall yield the rail to the party moving antispinward, except where the antispinward party is carrying livestock, in which case see Section 11.*

She looked up. Odum was watching her with an expression of such naked, hopeful pride that it was faintly embarrassing to see.

"I read the digital edition on the taxi," Piq said. "It's very thorough. I particularly appreciated the decompression section."

"Section nine."

"Nine. Yes."

"Revision fourteen moved decompression to section twelve," the captain said gently. "Nine is now waste handling."

There was a short silence in which the pullets, still in their crate in the taxi behind Piq, clucked accusingly.

"Then I'll be very interested in the revisions," Piq said, without missing a beat, and smiled. It was the smile that had got her through the Vesta examinations and three border inspections and one very bad night on Pallas. Odum's mouth twitched, possibly in amusement, possibly in pain, and she let it go.

"Initials page," she said. "Within one ship-day. And your cabin." She drew a slate from her breast pocket and read it, though she so clearly knew it already that reading it was a courtesy. "Ring, C-section, cabin 41. It's on the antispinward side, quieter. It's been cleaned and inspected. It's vacated."

*Vacated.* The word had a little procedural weight to it, the weight of a box ticked on a form. Piq knew whose cabin it was. She had known since before the taxi, since the Ledger's arrangements had quietly made it so. Watching the captain say it, she wondered whether Odum knew it was an arrangement, or thought it was luck, or simply hadn't let herself wonder.

"Vacated," Piq repeated.

"The previous occupant was our water quartermaster. Mr. Vasz." Odum's eyes didn't move from the slate. "He died on the long loop. An EVA accident, a tether failure. It's in the record. If you'd prefer a different cabin, I can—"

"No." Piq said it gently, a guest who wanted no fuss. "I'm sorry for your loss. A cabin's a cabin."

Odum looked up at last. For a moment Piq thought she would say something more, something that wasn't in the binder. Then the captain nodded once and said, "The spin-transfer elevator is through there. Mind the handrails." She almost smiled. "Section four."

---

The elevator vestibule was a short tube with a round car door at the far end. Beside the door a strip of lights counted the car's slow way up a spoke, from the ring toward the hub. Someone was waiting there. Piq saw a binder before she saw a face.

It was a thinner binder than Odum's and far more aggressive. Its edges bristled with colour tabs: red, blue, green, a long run of yellow, and a single black one at the very back. The woman holding it had braced one foot against the wall with the absent competence of someone who spent a lot of time in microgravity but did not love it. She was tall in the way of the Mars-born, long-boned and narrow, with dark hair tied back hard and a few strands escaping anyway to drift round her face like smoke. She wore a shipboard coverall with the sleeves pushed up. The skin of one forearm was dotted with small, neat burn marks. Cryogenics did that, Piq thought, or impatience with cryogenics.

"You're the auditor," the woman said. It was not a question.

"Piq Ansari-Holm." Piq offered a hand. The woman looked at it, then put the binder into it instead.

"Hana Szelig. I run the Umbrella. That's for you."

Piq turned the binder over. *UMBRELLA ACTIVE SHIELDING PROTOTYPE: RESPONSES TO ANTICIPATED AUDIT FINDINGS.*

"Anticipated," Piq said.

"Red is quench protection. Blue is dose modelling. Green is structural loading on the hub cradle. Yellow," and here Szelig's voice took on an edge like a scalpel being turned toward the light, "is every objection raised by every Board auditor who has looked at active shielding in the last eleven years. With my rebuttals. Page references are in the margins. You'll want to start with yellow. It'll save us both time."

"And black?"

"Black is the list of programmes the Board has ended by signature." Szelig said it flatly. "Eleven of them. Some of them were bad. Some of them weren't. None of the auditors who signed them ever built anything."

The elevator chimed. The door irised open on a car not much bigger than a coffin set on end, with a padded bench and four foot-loops and a stencilled sign: *OBSERVE PROTOCOL SECTION 6 DURING TRANSFER.* Szelig pulled herself in and hooked her feet without looking. After a moment Piq followed, binders under her arm, and turned in the doorway with a neatness she immediately regretted, because Szelig's eyes flicked to her feet and stayed there a moment too long.

"You move well," Szelig said, "for a Board auditor."

"I've spent a lot of time on freighters."

"Freighters don't have zero-g hubs."

"Lunar ones do, the ore haulers. I failed one." True. Piq smiled. "Their scrubbers were a disgrace. I'm told I'm still hated in Copernicus."

Szelig said nothing. The door closed. The car shivered and began to move.

At first there was no gravity at all, only the sense of being carried outward. Then, gradually, there was a floor. Piq's heels settled against the bench. Her binder, which had been floating at her elbow, drifted gently but definitely downward, and also, strangely, sideways, as though some invisible hand were pushing it toward the car wall.

"Coriolis," Szelig said, watching the binder. "The car's picking up the ring's spin as it descends. You'll feel it most in the transfer. Hold the rail."

"I know what Coriolis is."

"Everyone knows what Coriolis is. Not everyone knows what it does to the inner ear when you're dropping a hundred metres in ninety seconds." Szelig had not taken hold of the rail herself, but she had moved her feet apart and slightly forward, the way a sailor stands. "So. What will it be?"

"What will what be?"

"Your finding. The board you came from has a pattern. A new technology comes along that might work, and someone writes a forty-page report about what happens if it doesn't. The report goes to a committee. The committee has four people on it from carriers that run water shielding and one from Ceres. The programme loses its certification. Six months later the money is gone and the engineers are doing ice-mining logistics on Callisto." Her jaw worked. "You've got that look."

"Which look?"

"The look of someone who's already written the summary paragraph."

Piq let a beat go by. It was something she had learned early: let them hear their own words in the quiet, and see whether they wanted them back. Szelig did not. Her eyes were dark and very steady, and Piq thought they had probably always been like that, even as a child. They were the eyes of a person who had decided long ago that the universe was a set of problems and that problems could be beaten if you refused to blink first.

"I haven't written anything," Piq said. "I've been aboard eleven minutes. Ten of them were spent being told about handrails."

"The captain gave you the protocol."

"The captain gave me the protocol."

For the first time something like a smile touched the corner of Szelig's mouth. It was gone so fast it could have been a muscle spasm. "Did she ask you what section decompression was in?"

"She did."

"What did you say?"

"Nine."

"It's twelve now."

"So I've been told."

The car's floor was very real now, pushing against Piq's heels, her knees, her spine, and with it came the first low ache. It was nothing like the taxi's three gravities, only a little over a third of one, but her body didn't care for arithmetic. It knew only that it was heavier than it had any right to be. The ache came up through the long bones of her shins and settled in her hips. Her shoulders rolled forward in protest, as though her own arms were shopping bags. She had trained for this. She had spent four months in the centrifuge on Vesta, with calcium boluses and a bone-density regimen she would not wish on anyone, and her bones were officially rated for six months at Mars normal. Her bones had not read the rating.

"I'm going to give you a fair audit, Dr. Szelig," Piq said. "That's what I'm for."

"Everyone says that."

"Not everyone means it."

"No," Szelig agreed. "Not everyone does."

The car slowed. The binder at Piq's elbow made a last little skid sideways and came to rest against her hip. The door opened on a curved corridor with a floor that rose ahead in both directions, so that the corridor seemed to climb gently away from wherever you stood. It was lit in the dim, warm amber of a ship's evening watch. A scent of soil and growing things drifted down it from somewhere spinward.

Szelig stepped out and walked away without looking back, moving the way the Mars-born moved in their native weight, unhurried and light.

Piq stepped out after her and her left knee buckled.

It was not dramatic. It was the small, treacherous fold of a joint asked for something it had not been asked for in too long. She put out a hand, found a rail, and caught herself before anything worse than a lurch. The motion had felt straight down. The ring disagreed, and threw her a hand's breadth sideways against the wall, as though the corridor had shrugged.

Ten metres along, Szelig had stopped and turned.

"Section four," she said. "Yield the rail to the inboard hand."

"I'll make a note."

"Do." Szelig studied her a moment longer. Her face gave nothing away, but something behind it was clearly arranging a column of figures. "Morning briefing at 08:00, lab B-7, the hub end of spoke two. Read yellow first."

Then she was gone round the rising curve of the corridor, and Piq was alone with two binders, a knee that throbbed, and the slow, enormous, almost imperceptible sense of the ship turning beneath her feet.

---

Cabin C-41 was exactly what Odum had said. It had been cleaned and inspected, and it was vacated.

It was three metres by two. A bunk was set into the wall at waist height, with drawers under it and a fold-down desk above it, and there was a hygiene cubicle the size of a coat cupboard. A locker stood in the corner, floor to ceiling. The walls were the pale grey-green of every Tharsis interior. There was a little screen set beside the bunk where a window would have been on a planet, showing a rendering of a Martian dawn on a loop. Someone had thoughtfully switched it to the low setting. The air smelled of disinfectant and of nothing else, the way rooms smell when someone has been told to make sure there is nothing to smell.

Piq stood in the doorway for a long moment and let herself feel the ache in her legs. Then she stepped inside, closed the door and locked it. She sat down on the bunk, put her face in her hands and breathed for thirty seconds, because nobody could see her. When the thirty seconds were done, she started work.

She did the room first, the way she'd been trained. She did the obvious places, the places a cleaner would have done, and then the places a cleaner would not. She ran her hands along the seams of the bunk, under the drawers, behind the fold of the desk. She checked the vents, the screen housing, the junction box under the sink. She found the things she expected: two dead insects in the vent filter, a bent stylus wedged behind a drawer runner, a single grey hair caught in the desk hinge. It might have been Teodor's. It might have been anyone's. She bagged it anyway.

Then she unpacked.

Most of what she had brought was exactly what an auditor would bring: dosimeters, three of them, Board-calibrated and sealed, each with a certificate she would have to countersign weekly; a gamma spectrometer the size of a sandwich; sample vials in a foam tray; a cryo-rated contact thermometer; an audit slate with the Board's insignia burned into its case; a roll of tamper tape in IHB orange, which was the most feared colour in the inner system among people who maintained scrubbers. She laid them out on the desk in a neat row, because a neat row was what anyone searching the cabin later would expect to find.

Last came the field survey meter. It was a scuffed grey box with a folding antenna and a magnetometer probe on a coiled lead, the kind of instrument any auditor of a superconducting installation would carry to map stray fields and switching noise. It did map stray fields. It also did a great deal else. Behind its front panel, in a cavity the Vesta workshop had machined out of the battery housing, sat a broadband receiver that would sweep from a few kilohertz to well past the ship's comms bands, log anything that rose above the floor, and flag anything with the structure of an encrypted burst. Sabine had insisted on it. *Teodor sent us three words in eleven months*, she had said. *If somebody aboard is talking, I want you to hear them.*

Piq clipped the probe to the desk edge, extended the antenna to its full, unremarkable length, and set the meter to its routine survey mode. The screen showed a lazy green trace: the ring's lighting harmonics, the hum of the farm-deck pumps, a faint comb of spikes from the elevator motors in the spoke. Underneath, invisibly, the second channel woke and began to listen. She tapped the meter twice, as though it had been sulking, and left it running.

Then she took the memory chip from its slot in the lining of her boot.

It held one file, and the file held one message, and she had read it so often that she no longer needed to open it. She opened it anyway. That was a kind of procedure too.

The slate showed the Ledger's grey decryption frame, then the packet itself, stripped bare. There was no sender block, no route, and no timestamp except the one Ceres had stamped on arrival. The conjunction relays took everything off as a matter of security. A message from the *Aubade* to Ceres in this season didn't travel so much as wait. It sat in a Mars-trailing relay until a window opened, jumped, and sat again. When it finally came out the far end it had lost every mark of where it had been. This one had reached Occator nineteen hours after the *Aubade*'s official notice that her water quartermaster was dead. It had come in the same bundle as a routine consumables report. No one on Ceres could say whether he had sent it an hour before he went outside or a day.

THE WATER'S LYING.

That was all. It had no signature, no context, and not even the duress code he would have used if he'd known someone was reading over his shoulder.

She had never met Teodor Vasz. She knew him the way the Ledger knew its people at a distance, through eleven months of quartermaster's reports filed under a cover routine. They were precise, conscientious and so dull that the analyst assigned to them had asked twice to be reassigned. They gave water inventory to the tonne, consumption to the litre, and the recycling efficiency of every loop on the ship to three decimal places. A man who wrote like that did not send three words unless three words were all he had time for.

*Water can't lie*, Piq thought. Water was the most honest thing in the solar system. It had mass, and mass stopped protons, and you could weigh it. Numbers lied. Logs lied. People lied. She lied, and was good at it, and was paid to be good at it. Water just sat in its tanks and did what physics told it to.

So which had he meant? She turned the slate face down on the desk and sat looking at the locker.

---

She had left it for last on purpose, because it was the place she most wanted to search, and wanting things made her careless.

The locker door swung open on a hanging rail, two shelves and a mesh pouch for boots. It was empty and wiped down, and it smelled faintly of the same disinfectant as the rest of the room. She went over it the way she had gone over everything else, by eye, then by hand, then by light. She took the little inspection torch from her kit, laid its beam almost flat against each surface and watched for the shadows that flat light throws.

The shelves were clean. The back panel was clean. The hanging rail had been unscrewed at some point and put back with one screw not quite home, which meant someone had checked inside its brackets and found nothing worth taking. Piq checked anyway. She found nothing either.

Then she knelt on the deck, which her knees told her about at length, and ran the torch beam up the inside of the door from the bottom.

Near the lower hinge, where the door met the frame and anyone standing would have had to stoop to look, the paint had been scored.

It wasn't graffiti. It was a column of small, careful marks, scratched with something hard and pointed, the tip of a screwdriver perhaps, or a stylus like the one she'd found wedged behind the drawer. Each line was a three-digit number with a short tick beside it. The column was nine lines long. The cleaners had painted over the door at some point, or meant to. A thin skin of grey-green lay across the marks, and the scratches showed through it only as hairlines of shadow when the light came in low. From any normal angle they were invisible.

041. 067. 102. 139. 160. 188. 204. 219. 231.

Ship-days, she thought. Tharsis cyclers counted from flyby. Day zero was closest approach, and every log on the ship ran from there. She held the torch steady and made herself do it properly, rather than guessing.

The *Aubade*'s long loop had begun at Mars flyby on the first of March, 2196. She had it from the briefing pack, from the dry Ledger appendix of dates she had learned the way she learned everything, by rote, against the day she would need it. The briefing pack had also given her Teodor's date. The ship's own incident report, the one that said *tether failure* and nothing more, put his death on 18 October 2196.

March had thirty-one days. That put the first of April at day thirty-one. Then came thirty for April, thirty-one for May, thirty for June, thirty-one for July, thirty-one for August and thirty for September, which made the first of October day two hundred and fourteen. Seventeen days more brought her to the eighteenth.

Two hundred and thirty-one.

She sat back on her heels. The Martian dawn went on rising on its loop beside the bunk, pink light washing the grey-green walls and draining away to begin again. Somewhere beneath her the ring went round, once every thirty-five seconds. It had gone round like that on day two hundred and thirty-one too, while a man who wrote consumables reports to three decimal places went out onto the hub truss alone and did not come back.

The last mark had a tick beside it, the same as the others.

He had scratched it before he went outside. Whatever he had been counting, he had expected to come back to this cabin and count again. Eight of the nine dates were days she knew nothing about, days on which nothing had happened, according to every record the Ledger had been able to get.

Piq turned off the torch. Then she turned it back on, photographed the column three times from three angles, and began, very quietly, to find out what Teodor Vasz had done on the other eight.

## Chapter 2: The Blank Line

The tea went sideways.

Piq had poured it the way she poured everything at home. She held the bulb-spouted pot high, a forearm's length above the cup, and aimed the spout well short of the rim so that the long, lazy Ceres arc would carry the stream across and in. The stream left the spout and did not arc. It dropped, a dozen times faster than her hands expected, and struck the near rim. Half of it slopped over the lip. The rest bent away from her like a sulking animal and splashed across the galley counter, over the edge and onto her left boot.

She stood very still and considered it.

The aim was her own fault. Ceres reflexes in a 0.38 g kitchen. But the sideways bend was the ship's. The stream had come out of the spout moving at the spout's speed around the ring's axis, and the counter, a few centimetres farther out, was moving slightly faster. Twenty-odd metres per second at the rim, less a sliver for every centimetre inward. The cup had travelled on and left the tea behind. Antispinward drift, a fraction of a centimetre per ten centimetres of fall, and it grew faster than the fall did. From the height she'd poured, call it a centimetre and a half, all of it in the wrong direction and all of it at the rim she had already hit. She had known all of it in the centrifuge on Vesta. She had poured water there for four months. She had simply forgotten, at 07:20 with a body that felt filled with wet sand, that the world was turning under her.

"Pour low," said someone behind her.

A crewman in Tharsis grey-green was waiting with an empty cup. He was young and had the long, slightly loose build of the Mars-born, and he was trying not to grin. He took the pot from her, set the spout almost on the cup's rim and filled it without a drop going astray. "First week, everybody does that. Second week, you do it to the new ones on purpose."

"Is that in the protocol?"

"Section fourteen. Beverage service." The grin got away from him. "I'm kidding. Probably."

She thanked him, mopped the counter with the galley's grey reusable cloth, and carried the cup back toward her cabin. She carried it close to her chest and walked like a woman crossing ice. Her knees had opinions. Her hips had opinions. At 0.38 g she weighed something close to twelve times what her body had been built for, and every one of her bones was reporting it at shift change. The Vesta medics had rated her for six months. They had not said the six months would be comfortable.

The forty-page protocol lay on her desk with its brass rings shining. She had not initialled it. Due within one ship-day, the cover sheet said, which meant by about 15:00. She drank half the tea standing up, because sitting down and then standing again was a negotiation she wanted to save for later. Then she put the cup in the rack, clipped the survey meter to her belt and went to start the audit.

---

The spin-transfer elevator dropped her up the spoke in ninety seconds of slowly fading weight. The Coriolis pressed her against the car's side wall as she went inward, a long steady lean that made her feel she was being tipped politely out of her own life. Then the car slowed, the lean eased, and the last of her weight ran out through the soles of her feet like water through sand.

The doors opened on the hub, and Piq came home.

She did not mean to. She had planned to be careful. She had told herself in the elevator to grab the handrail with both hands, to be a little awkward, to look like a woman whose zero-g hours came from training modules and ore-hauler hubs. But the hub's air smelled of water and cold stone, the light was flat and shadowless, and there was no up. Her body knew what to do before she decided anything. One fingertip touched the door frame, a fraction of a newton, and she was moving: across the transfer chamber, a half-turn at the midpoint so her feet led, a two-finger catch on the far rail that killed her drift completely.

It felt like taking off a coat she had been wearing for a week.

"Huh," someone said.

A rigger was hanging from a cargo net at the chamber's edge, feet hooked in the mesh, sorting a tangle of tether lines. She was a woman perhaps in her late twenties, with cropped sandy hair and a rigger's harness hung with colour-coded clips. A strip of black tape covered most of her name tape, as if she had pulled off a patch and never replaced it. The lines she was sorting had drifted into a slow lazy spiral around her while she watched Piq.

"Good morning," Piq said, and gave the rail a respectable two-handed grip, a little late.

"You've done that before."

"Lunar ore haulers. They have zero-g hubs. You learn or you bruise."

The rigger thought about it. Her eyes were a pale grey that missed very little. "Lunar haulers spin," she said. "The whole hull. Have since the thirties. Cheaper than a bearing."

"The old ones don't."

"Mm." The rigger caught her spiralling tether lines in a single sweep without looking at them and went back to sorting. "Old ones, then."

Piq drifted on down the hub's main tube toward the lab sections, and she could feel the woman's gaze on her back the whole way. She made herself bump an elbow on a junction box at the first turn. It hurt and was far too late to matter.

*Stop correcting,* she told herself. *Every correction is a second lie, and second lies get noticed.* A Ledger instructor on Vesta had said that, a man with a face like a closed door. *Pick one lie and wear it like a skin. Stop adjusting it in mirrors.*

She had been adjusting all morning.

Lab B-7 sat where spoke 2 met the hub. It was a non-rotating room shaped like a squashed drum, with a work surface wrapped around its inner wall and a dozen people clipped to it by foot loops or belt tethers. Five of them were Hana's team: two postdocs, a cryogenic technician, a quiet older man who turned out to be the power systems lead, and a woman with a shaved head who did nothing for forty minutes but watch a temperature plot and drink something through a straw. The others were Tharsis staff, there to be seen being cooperative. The Director of Operations was not among them.

"Mr. Ferro sends regrets," said a man in a Tharsis jacket with a lanyard. "He's in conference with the company."

"He was in conference yesterday."

"It's a long conference."

Hana Szelig had arranged herself at the head of the room, insofar as a room with no up has a head. She was anchored by one foot under a bar with the binder open against her forearm. She had not slept well, or else she always looked like that: shadows under the eyes, hair pulled back so tightly that the escaping strands seemed to be fleeing on purpose. The small neat cryo burns on her forearm were pale in the flat hub light.

"Auditor Ansari-Holm," she said. "Welcome to B-7. The binder answers everything. I'm here to tell you which page."

There was a small ripple of laughter from her team. It was not entirely friendly, and not entirely at Piq's expense either.

"Then let's see how good the binder is." Piq woke her audit slate. The Board checklist ran to three hundred and twelve items for a prototype shielding system in transit. She had marked forty for the first day. "Quench detection. What's your threshold voltage and your detection latency?"

"Red tab, page fourteen."

Piq turned to red, page fourteen. Threshold voltage and latency, with a figure and three references. She read it, then read it again in case the second reading turned up a gap. It didn't.

"Energy extraction in a quench. Where does the stored energy go?"

"Red, page twenty-two. Dump resistors and a cryogenic bypass. Schematic on twenty-three."

"How much stored energy at full field?"

"Twenty-two."

It was on twenty-two. Piq did the arithmetic in the margin of her slate and got a number big enough to make her want to know exactly where the dump resistors were mounted. "Dump resistor thermal rejection, where does the heat go?"

"Green, page nine. Into the hub cradle radiator panels, darkside."

"Personnel exclusion zone around the coil housing during operation."

"Red, page thirty. And a reminder that the coil is not in operation."

Piq looked up.

Hana's face showed nothing. "The coil has been in cold soak since departure from Mars on the long loop. Superconductor at operating temperature, no current. First ramp is scheduled for the Board-witnessed test after the Mars flyby. It's in green, page three. You'll want to witness it. Your Board has insisted on witnessing every first ramp since 2186." A small pause. "Black tab, item seven, explains why."

Piq didn't need to look at black tab item seven. She had read it last night, between searching the locker and lying awake. It was a programme on Luna that had been ended after an unwitnessed ramp went wrong, not catastrophically, but expensively and publicly. The Board had added a rule. The rule had outlived the programme.

"Then I'll be witnessing," she said. "Until then I'll inspect the coil housing, the cryo plant, the cradle and the controls, in that order."

"The cradle and controls today. The housing is exterior. EVA scheduling goes through hub operations, and hub operations goes through Mr. Ferro's office."

"Who is in conference."

Hana's expression didn't change, but something at the corner of her mouth did, the same small flicker as yesterday over the decompression section. Then it was gone. "Who is in conference," she agreed.

Piq asked thirty-nine more questions. She got thirty-nine page references. By the end of it the room had gone from wary to bored to secretly entertained, and the shaven-headed woman watching the temperature plot had started mouthing the page numbers just before Hana said them. Piq began asking out of order to see if Hana would stumble. She didn't. Piq asked about the cradle bolts' preload torque and got green, page sixteen. She asked about the insurance underwriter's name and got yellow, page eighty-one, with a note on the underwriter's history of litigation against Board auditors.

"Last one," Piq said. "If I asked you something that isn't in the binder, what would you say?"

"I'd say it's in the next revision."

That got a real laugh from the room. Piq laughed too, and found she meant it, which irritated her.

Afterward, while the room unclipped and dispersed, she pulled herself over to Hana's end of the work surface. Hana was closing the binder with the care of someone putting away a weapon.

"You prepared for an enemy," Piq said.

"I prepared for an auditor."

"It's the same to you."

Hana looked at her properly for the first time that morning. Her eyes were a very dark brown, almost black, and they had the same quality Piq had noticed at the elevator, of a column being added. "The last Board auditor I met," she said, "was on Luna in 2189. He was kind. He asked good questions. He told me the work was promising. Then he went home and signed a finding that killed eleven years of research in a paragraph. He sent me a personal note afterward saying he hoped I'd land on my feet." Her voice was perfectly level. "So, no. Not the same. The enemy at least tells you what it wants."

"What do you think I want?"

"I think you want to go home." Hana tucked the binder under her arm. "Everybody does. How the report reads is mostly a matter of how fast you'd like to get there."

She pushed off from the bar and drifted away down the hub tube without looking back, a long-boned shape that moved competently and without joy, as if zero g were a colleague she respected and did not like.

Piq stayed where she was and noticed her pulse. It was elevated. That was annoying too.

The storm shelter was on the axis. That was the first thing anyone learned about an Aldrin cycler, and the Aubade's shelter was a good one: a cylinder eleven metres across and thirty long, lying along the spin axis in the middle of the hub, so that every particle coming from any direction had to pass through as much water as the ship could carry. The tanks wrapped around it like the layers of an onion. Water above, water below, water on every side. Beyond them, sunward, was the hub cap and the main solar array, angled to face a Sun the ship kept dead ahead of its nose.

"Spin axis sunward, always," said the hub systems chief who walked her through. Her name was Dagny Wirth, a thickset Lunar woman of sixty with a tablet strapped to her wrist and the manner of someone who had given this tour often and enjoyed it more each time. "Array stays lit, no articulation needed, no bearings to fail. Sun's up there." She pointed along the cylinder's length. "Eight thousand tonnes between you and it, give or take. Best shelter on any cycler running. People sleep in here during a storm and complain it's too quiet."

Piq floated in the middle of the shelter and let the place settle into her. Bunks folded into the walls in rows. Water bladders and food stores were lashed in netting. There was a hygiene module at one end and an emergency comms panel at the other, and above it a gauge array showing tank fill levels, eight tank sections in a ring. Each showed a green bar near the top. Ninety-seven percent. Ninety-six. Ninety-seven. Ninety-eight.

The air was cool and smelled faintly of plastic and stale breath, the smell of a room that was cleaned regularly and used rarely. Somewhere behind the walls she could hear the faint, slow movement of liquid in the tanks, the tidal shift of tonnes of water nudged by the hub's tiny residual accelerations. It was not a sound. It was a pressure in the ears, felt more than heard.

She unpacked her kit. The three Board dosimeters came out in their sealed cases, and she broke the first seal in front of Dagny so it would be witnessed, logged the serial number and set the device on a clip at the shelter's geometric centre. The second she wore on her collar, as she would for the whole transit. The third stayed sealed as a control.

"Integration time?" Dagny asked.

"Ten minutes for a first read. I'll leave the fixed unit for a week."

They waited. Dagny talked about the shelter's ventilation redundancy, which was excellent, and its psychological design, which was less so. "The colour scheme came out of a study. The study was done by a man who'd never been in a storm. Whole thing's the colour of hospital broth." Piq agreed that it was. She watched the integrating number on the dosimeter crawl.

At ten minutes she read it and frowned.

"Problem?" said Dagny.

"It's reading above spec." Piq showed her. The Board's design specification for this shelter, published in the Aubade's certification file, put quiet-Sun background behind full tanks at well under a third of what the instrument was showing. Not a dangerous number. Nobody was going to get sick from a week of it. But it was wrong, and wrong in a direction that mattered.

Dagny looked at it and shrugged with her whole upper body, a Lunar shrug. "That's your calibration. Board units read high in here every time. Last auditor's did the same, two years ago. Something about the source they calibrate against at Copernicus. We had a long correspondence about it. I have the letters." She tapped her tablet. "I'll send them."

"Did anyone recalibrate?"

"We had them checked against ours. Ours read spec." Dagny showed her the ship's own fixed monitors on the comms panel, which were indeed reading spec, a nice low number in a green box. "Drift. It's always drift. You should see what the Board units say about the ring."

Piq broke the seal on the control unit, which was a breach of procedure she would have to explain in writing, and held it next to the first. It read the same within a few percent. Both her units agreed with each other and disagreed with the ship's.

"Your instruments came from the same batch," Dagny said kindly. "Same source, same bias. It's in the letters."

"I'll read the letters."

She logged it: SHELTER BACKGROUND ABOVE SPEC. BOARD UNITS CONCORDANT. SHIP MONITORS AT SPEC. ATTRIBUTED BY CREW TO CALIBRATION DRIFT. FOLLOW UP. Then she re-sealed the control unit with orange tamper tape and wrote the time across the seam.

Before she left she turned once more toward the gauges. Ninety-seven, ninety-six, ninety-seven, ninety-eight. Eight thousand tonnes. A full ship.

*The water's lying,* Teodor had written, and he had been the quartermaster. If the water were going to lie to anyone, it would have lied to him first.

She looked up the length of the cylinder toward the Sun she could not see. Water and more water, and beyond it the array, and beyond that a star near the peak of its eleven-year temper. Then she drifted out of the shelter, through the hatch with its triple seals, and asked Dagny where she could find the EVA records.

The purser's office was a narrow room on the ring with one desk, two chairs and a man named Halloran who had a gentle grey moustache and a stack of initialled protocol binders tied with string.

"Ah," he said when she came in, with deep sympathy. "You've come about your initials."

She had not. She had forgotten. It was 14:20.

"I've come about records access," she said, "and also, yes, my initials."

He produced a pen, an actual ink pen, the kind used on ships where paper had been decreed. She sat down in the chair, which was a long and graceless process of bending her knees under 0.38 g, and opened the binder on her lap.

Each section had a box in the lower right corner. Each box had a small printed line: *I have read and understood Section __.* Forty pages, twenty-two sections. She initialled section 1, general conduct. Section 2, emergency signals. Section 3, cabin assignment and personal stowage. Section 4, handrail etiquette. She paused over the livestock exception and initialled it anyway. Section 5, mealtimes. Section 6, elevator transfer, which advised passengers not to eat during transfer due to "documented Coriolis-related events".

"You don't have to read every page," Halloran said, watching her. "Nobody does."

"The captain says people read paper."

"The captain is an optimist." He leaned in a little and lowered his voice. "She's got a study."

"She mentioned."

"Twelve passengers. She asked them if they'd read it. They all said yes." He sat back. "Of course they all said yes. She was the captain."

Piq initialled the remaining sections in a steady rhythm: AH-PIQ, AH-PIQ, AH-PIQ. She stopped at section 12, decompression, and read it in full, because that one was real and she had already been caught on it once. It was well written. Clear, short sentences. Pressure doors in the order they would close. The meeting points. The suits. Somebody had cared very much about getting it right. She found herself, unexpectedly, liking Captain Odum for it.

She handed the binder back. "Records. I need EVA logs for the long loop, plus the incident file on Quartermaster Vasz."

Halloran's moustache settled into a different shape. "The Vasz file is closed."

"Closed to the public. I'm the Board."

"Of course." He turned to his terminal and typed, and after a moment he angled the screen toward her. "Read-only. You can't copy it out. That's Mr. Ferro's office's ruling."

"Mr. Ferro is in conference."

"Mr. Ferro," said Halloran, with delicate neutrality, "is always in conference."

The incident file was eleven pages long. She read it twice.

Teodor Vasz, quartermaster, fifty-six years old, Lunar-born, EVA certified level two. On ship-day 231 of the long loop he had gone out alone through the darkside hub airlock to perform what the log described as "QM external stores inventory, hub truss". The EVA was logged and approved after the fact by the Office of Operations, with a note that verbal approval had been given beforehand. No spotter had been assigned. The airlock cycle-out was recorded at 01:31. His suit telemetry had dropped out sometime in the next forty minutes. The file said "in the 01:40 to 02:10 window", because the telemetry relay on that section of truss had been under maintenance and logging only intermittently. He had been found at 03:05 by a rigger checking why the airlock showed an open cycle. He was drifting two metres off the truss, near the coil housing on the Umbrella cradle, with his suit breached and his tether cut through.

Ruling: tether failure. Fatigue fracture of the tether line at an anchor point, cause probably abrasion against a truss edge. Death by decompression. The suit breach was attributed to secondary contact with the truss as he tumbled.

There was a photo of the tether. It was frayed and parted at one end, with the strands splayed. Piq looked at it for a long time. She had seen fatigue fractures in tether line. They usually looked like that. She had also seen line cut by a sharp edge at speed, and those looked like that too.

The last page was the kit inventory. It listed every item issued from stores to Teodor Vasz for that EVA, and beside it the items recovered with the body. Suit, gloves, helmet. Tether, one, as noted. Tool belt. Inspection lamp. A tablet in a sealed pouch. Sample bags, four, empty. Three small titanium hex drivers. A nylon-handled cutter.

And at the bottom of the issue column, one line with no matching entry beside it:

*Torque wrench, 3/8 drive, steel. Qty 1.*

The recovery column beside it was blank. Not marked lost. Not marked "not recovered", which was the phrase used in such files when something had drifted away into the dark. Simply blank, as though whoever had compiled the list had reached the bottom and forgotten there was one line left.

"Steel," Piq said aloud.

Halloran looked up.

"The torque wrench was steel. Is that normal? I'd have thought anything near a superconducting coil would be titanium. Non-ferrous."

"Coil's off, though. Has been since Mars." Halloran shrugged. "Teodor liked his own tools. Old Lunar habits. He said the titanium ones were good for nothing but making noise."

"Where is it now?"

"Gone, I suppose. Out there." Halloran waved vaguely at the bulkhead, at the dark beyond it. "Things get away from you on the truss."

Piq nodded slowly. She looked at the blank line a little longer. Somebody had issued a steel wrench and nobody had noticed it hadn't come back. A small hole. It meant nothing yet. She filed it alongside the loose screw on her locker rail and the eight numbers scratched behind the hinge.

"One more thing," she said. "The EVA log for the whole long loop. Every logged egress, every crew member."

Halloran called it up. It was not long. Riggers out on scheduled truss work. A pair of technicians replacing a sensor. Dagny Wirth twice, for a radiator inspection. Teodor Vasz once, on day 231.

Piq checked it against the numbers she had photographed and committed to memory. 041. 067. 102. 139. 160. 188. 219. 231.

Seven of them matched nothing. There were no EVAs on any of those days, not by Teodor and not by anyone. The eighth was day 231, the only egress Teodor Vasz had ever logged, and the one that had killed him.

So either the numbers on the locker meant something else entirely, or Teodor Vasz had gone outside seven times without anyone logging it, scratched a mark each time he came back, and made his last mark before he went out the eighth time.

The commissary at evening watch was the warmest room on the ring. It was long and low, with tables bolted in rows and a serving line at the antispinward end, and it smelled of reconstituted lentils, real bread and the faint damp green of the farm deck next door. Someone had put potted basil on the tables. Passengers and researchers mixed with off-shift crew, and the noise of a hundred conversations lay over everything like a blanket.

Piq sat at the end of a table near the wall with a bowl of lentils and a slab of bread. She used both hands to set them down and lowered herself onto the bench with a care she hoped looked like dignity. Her hips filed a formal complaint. Then she ate slowly and watched the room.

She had a list in her head of everyone who might be someone. It was not a short list. A ship of two hundred and twenty people under the long, slow pressure of five months was a ship of two hundred and twenty secrets, and only a few of them would matter.

"You," said a voice above her. "You have a face for intrigue."

She looked up.

The man standing over her was very old and very thin, with a sharp beaky nose and a halo of white hair that had decided against gravity long ago and seemed to be holding out even at 0.38. He wore a velvet waistcoat of deep plum over a ship's coverall, and a reading lens hung around his neck on a chain. He held a battered paper notebook in one hand and a pencil in the other, and he was examining her with the full attention of a man choosing fruit.

"I'm sorry?" said Piq.

"Intrigue. It's the eyes. The way they keep moving without the head moving." He swept onto the bench opposite her. He did not sit so much as fold, like a deckchair. "Ambrose Tull. Lunar. Copernicus, retired. Playwright, not retired. I'm directing this transit's passenger play, and you're going to be in it."

"I don't think—"

"Nobody thinks. That's the joy of it." He opened the notebook and wrote something quickly without letting her see. "Every Aldrin transit since the fifties has had a passenger play. You know this, surely. It's tradition. Spacers are mad for tradition. Outbound from Mars we do something serious. Inbound to Mars, the way Tharsis counts it, we do a farce. This is the inbound, and this year it's mine." He tapped the cover of the notebook. "*Dead Drop at Perihelion.* A spy comedy in three acts. Do you know the genre?"

"I'm an auditor."

"Then you'll be a natural. Auditors and spies, both of them stand in corners and judge people." He beamed at her. His teeth were his own and very yellow. "We rehearse in the hub. Tuesdays and Fridays, after evening watch. The zero-g scenes are the best part. Nobody can do pratfalls in zero g. You just drift into the furniture very slowly. It's devastating."

"Mr. Tull—"

"Ambrose."

"Ambrose. I'm here to work. I really can't—"

"You can, and you will, because if you refuse I'll be wounded, and I am eighty-one years old, and wounding me is the sort of thing people regret." He leaned in. His voice dropped, not quite to a whisper, more the stage version of one, pitched to carry to the back row. "Besides. I need someone I can trust."

Piq put down her spoon. "Trust?"

Ambrose looked left and then right. Then he looked behind him, past the end of the table, at the rest of the commissary, with the exaggerated care of a man performing caution to an audience. "Someone on this ship," he said, "is following me."

"Following you."

"Since Earth flyby. Two corridors back. Always two corridors. Coveralls. Hood up. I turn around, they're reading a notice board. I go to the farm deck, there they are, looking at tomatoes." He tapped the table with his pencil. "Nobody looks at tomatoes that long."

Piq kept her face perfectly still, which took effort. "Did you report it?"

"To whom? Security? The ship's security officer is a nice young man who thinks I'm senile. The captain would give me a protocol." He sniffed. "Section seventeen, I expect. Paranoid passengers, management of."

"I'm not sure that's a section."

"It will be by Rev. 15." He sat back and studied her again with his choosing-fruit look. "You don't believe me."

"I believe you've seen someone."

"Ha." He jabbed the pencil at her, delighted. "That's an auditor's answer. Precise. Commits to nothing. I'll use it." He scribbled again. "You'll be marvellous. I'll tell you your part on Friday. Don't be late, and don't learn your lines in advance. I'll have changed them."

He unfolded from the bench, tucked the notebook into his waistcoat, and moved off down the commissary with surprising speed, nodding to people as he went like a minor monarch reviewing troops. At the serving line he stopped abruptly, turned, and stared hard at the doorway. Piq followed his gaze. There was nobody there. Ambrose narrowed his eyes at the empty doorway, then went on through it.

Piq ate the rest of her lentils and thought about it.

An old man who believed he was being followed. Old men often did, and they were usually wrong. But this was a ship where a quartermaster had gone outside seven times without anyone logging it and come back the eighth time dead. A ship where the dosimeters read high and the gauges read full. A ship where the Director of Operations had been in conference for two days running.

She had come aboard expecting to be the only person on the Aubade with a secret worth killing for. It was beginning to look like a crowded field.

She got back to C-41 at 22:40, tired in the deep, total way that ring gravity produced, a tiredness in the skeleton rather than the muscles. She took two calcium boluses with water, poured low, and sat on the edge of the bunk for a while without moving. The Martian dawn on the wall screen had looped through its sunrise and begun again.

The survey meter was where she had left it, clipped to the desk with its folding antenna up and its little screen glowing amber. She unclipped it and looked at the day's log.

It was a tapestry of the ship's ordinary noise. The ring lighting ran on a switched supply that hummed at a dozen harmonics. The farm deck pumps pulsed every few seconds in a lazy pattern. The elevator motors made tall spikes each time a car went up or down a spoke. All of it was there in the receiver's waterfall display, coloured bands and spikes, every one of them innocent and identifiable, and the burst-detection algorithm had flagged none of them. That was what it was for. It looked for the thing that didn't fit: high-entropy noise at a structured repetition rate, the fingerprint of encrypted traffic.

Nothing. A clean day.

She set the meter back on the desk and lay down in the bunk fully dressed, which was a lapse of discipline, and looked at the ceiling. She thought about Teodor's eleven-page file and the blank line at the bottom of it. She thought about the eight numbers scratched behind the hinge. She thought, briefly and against her will, about Hana Szelig saying *the enemy at least tells you what it wants*, and the column-of-figures look, and the way her hair had come loose at the temple when she pushed off.

She was asleep before she noticed she was falling.

The meter woke her.

It was a soft sound, a three-tone chirp she had chosen herself because it was the kind of sound nobody wakes to except the one person listening for it. She was on her feet before she had decided to be, her knees complaining. The cabin was dark except for the dawn screen and the meter's amber glow. She grabbed the meter off the desk.

02:07.

The waterfall display showed a single sharp band, very short, maybe four hundred milliseconds, spread across a wide swath of frequencies in the low kilohertz. High entropy. Repetitive structure inside it, a pulse train. The detection algorithm had flagged it in red: POSSIBLE ENCRYPTED BURST.

She stared at it. Her heart was going hard.

She had expected traffic. That was why she carried the receiver. Sabine had expected it too. *Teodor sent us three words in eleven months. Find out who else is talking.* But expecting a thing and seeing it were not the same.

She checked the signal strength. It was strong, much stronger than anything that should have reached the ring from outside the hull. The Aubade's skin and water and the ring's own structure would have attenuated anything from outside. This was near-field. Close. The ship itself was talking. Something aboard had just sent four hundred milliseconds of very dense, very structured noise into the dark at seven minutes past two in the morning.

She sat at the desk with the meter in her hands and waited, barely breathing, for a second burst. It didn't come. She stayed until 02:40. Nothing. Just the hum of the lights and the slow pulse of the farm pumps and, far below her feet, the ring turning through the dark at twenty metres a second.

She marked the timestamp, saved the waveform to the hidden partition of the meter's memory and pulled up a fresh page of her private notebook. At the top she wrote: 02:07, DAY 2. BURST. NEAR-FIELD. SOURCE INTERNAL.

Under it, after a moment, she wrote: *Somebody else aboard is reporting.*

She looked at the words for a long time. Then she turned off the dawn screen, lay back down in the dark with the meter on the pillow beside her head, and didn't sleep at all.

## Chapter 3: Livestock Delay

The hub lights went out at 02:00 and came back before she could be sure they had gone.

Piq had come up at half past one because her hips would not let her lie down any longer. She was in the axial corridor beside the observation blister with one hand looped loosely through a rail. Her body was floating free of itself for the first time in thirty hours, and she was thinking about nothing more dangerous than whether the blister's frost would clear before the end of watch. The strip lights along the corridor wall dipped. It was not a blackout. The light sagged, as though something big had drawn breath from the same lungs, and in that sag the receiver in her sleeve pocket buzzed twice against the inside of her wrist.

She did not look at it straight away. Looking at a thing in a hurry was how you got seen looking. She let her body turn on the rail as if she were stretching, counted four, and then slid the little case out. To anyone else it was a sleep tracker, with a soft grey shell and a wristband she never wore. Underneath it was a broadband receiver the Ledger had built to listen to everything a ship said when it thought no one was listening.

*02:00:07. Burst, 1.8 s, broadband, structured.* It was the second in two nights.

The lights had already steadied. She watched them for a full minute anyway, the way she would watch a crack in a pressure seal, but they did nothing more.

Someone aboard was transmitting, and the transmitter was big enough to pull current off the hub lighting bus when it keyed. A strip light did not sag for a handset or a tablet. It sagged when a high-power amplifier came up out of standby and grabbed. She thought it might be in the hub, or on a bus the hub shared. It had run for under two seconds, because a burst was cheaper and harder to find than a conversation.

She wrote the timestamp on the inside of her forearm in pen. It was an old habit from the warrens, where tablets got lost and skin did not. Then she went back down the spoke to the ring and its gravity, and sat in her cabin until morning without sleeping.

The drill tones came at 09:00, three rising notes and a fourth that held. Piq was already standing when they started. All she had gained from the extra hours was a sharper sense of how much her hips disliked the floor.

Section two of the protocol said the tones meant *proceed to the storm shelter by assigned route without running*. A footnote, which Piq had read twice, said that drills would be followed by a spoken announcement from the captain *identifying the drill as a drill, so that no one is unduly alarmed, but not immediately, so that everyone is duly alarmed*. She had liked Odum a little more for that footnote.

The ring corridor filled with people who had done this before. Crew moved with the bored efficiency of a fire drill on a station that had never burned. Passengers moved in clumps and asked each other which spoke was theirs. Piq had her audit tablet out and a stopwatch running. Item 211: *Shelter muster time, full complement, against design figure of 12:00*. For once the job and the cover were the same thing.

She rode the spoke-three elevator up with a family from Hellas and a boy of about six who wanted to know if this one was real. As the car climbed, the weight came off her spine vertebra by vertebra, like a coat lifted from her shoulders. By the time the doors opened onto the hub she could have laughed. She didn't. She held the rail as everyone else did and pulled herself along in the short, cautious, ring-born tugs she had practised in front of a mirror.

Captain Odum floated beside the open shelter hatch with one foot hooked under a toe bar and a stopwatch in her hand, counting heads with small movements of her chin. Her uniform was pressed. Piq suspected she had pressed it on the ring and carried it up very carefully.

"Auditor," Odum said. "You're timing us."

"Item two-eleven."

"We do it in eleven-forty, as a rule." A pause. "Eleven-fifty on a bad day. I'd be grateful if you didn't write down the bad days."

"I write down the day I'm given, Captain."

Odum's mouth flattened into something that might have been approval. "Section two," she said, and went back to counting.

Inside, the shelter was the long honey-coloured cylinder it had been the day before, now filling with bodies. Dagny Wirth moved among them checking the gauge array on her wrist tablet. Piq glanced at the eight section readouts as she passed: 97, 96, 98, 97, 97, 96, 98, 97. Then she glanced at her own dosimeter at the shelter centre. The orange-taped seal was intact. The cumulative number in its little window was higher than it should have been.

She stopped and looked again. It was not much higher, but it was not nothing. Eight thousand tonnes of water stood between this room and the sky, and at ninety-seven percent full the shelter should have been the quietest place in the inner system. A badge left there for a day should have read close to the floor of its own noise. This one read like a badge that had spent the afternoon in a ring cabin.

Then she remembered Dagny the day before, laughing as she pressed the seal down: *Don't panic when that reads warm. All the shelter badges have drifted since the last Earth pass. Calibration's flagged, it's on the list.* It was on the list. Piq had seen the work order.

She wrote down the number, then *cal. drift — WO ref.*, and moved on.

At the far end, by the emergency comms panel, a tall man in a cardigan with a headset slung round his neck was speaking into a handset in the patient, carrying voice of someone who expected nobody to listen. "Shelter to bridge, comms check, reading you five by five, please confirm you are also reading me five by five, because last drill you did not." The fingers of his free hand tapped the panel edge, tap-tap, tap, tap-tap-tap, like someone counting packets.

Piq had drifted most of the way toward him when the screaming started.

It came from the hatch. It was high and indignant and did not stop for breath, and under it a man's voice was saying "No no no no no." Every head turned as a hen came through the hatch at about two metres a second. Her wings were fully extended, her feet were bicycling at nothing, and she rotated slowly end over end like a thrown glove.

She was russet, with black flecks across a comb that was still growing in. Piq recognised her with a jolt of something close to affection. She had ridden up from Earth orbit with eight pullets in a crate with air holes, at three gravities, and one of them had pecked her through the slats twice during the burn and once more during unloading, out of what had seemed like principle.

Behind the hen came a farm-deck crewman with a pet carrier in each hand. One was latched and full of clucking. The other was empty and its door flapped. "She was *in* it," he told the shelter. "She was in it at the elevator."

The hen struck the first bunk rail chest-first, rebounded, shrieked, and beat her wings in a way that would have meant something with weight under her. Without weight it bought her a faster spin and a shower of downy feathers that hung in the air and stayed there.

"Nobody chase her," said the farm hand. "If you chase her she—"

Three people chased her. The air in the shelter turned into weather. A child cheered. A woman near the hygiene module said, with feeling, "She's *going*," and the hen produced a small comet-shaped contribution that travelled in a dignified straight line into the back of a Tharsis jacket.

Odum had come through the hatch and was scrolling her tablet with tremendous calm. "Section twenty-one. Livestock and Companion Animals. Subsection three, Escapes in Microgravity." The shelter fell quiet enough to hear her. "'Personnel shall not pursue the animal. Personnel shall remain still and await the animal's arrival at a surface, at which point the handler shall secure it with a towel or similar.'"

The hen had arrived at no surface and showed no sign of planning to. She had found the slow eddy of the circulation fans, from the intake at the hygiene end to the return near the comms panel, and she was riding it like a leaf in a gutter.

"Does anyone have a towel," Odum said, "or similar."

Piq hadn't meant to move. She would remember that afterward, the way you remember not meaning to say something true. The hen was coming down the eddy on a line Piq could read the way she read a corridor at home. She could see the arc that would carry the bird past the man in the cardigan and into the bulkhead in two seconds and a little more.

She had her toes under a rail before she'd decided anything. She pushed off with one foot and no hands, which was wrong for a ring-born auditor, who would have hauled hand over hand. She crossed four metres on a line that didn't wobble and tucked a shoulder to start a quarter-turn, so that she arrived feet first to the bulkhead and facing back up the eddy. Then she put her left hand into the space where the hen was about to be. The hen arrived. Piq closed her fingers gently over the bird's back to pin the wings, and let her arm give with the momentum the way you take a catch on Ceres. Her heels touched the bulkhead and stopped them both.

The hen made one more remark and went silent, her eyes like beads of tar. Piq noticed the silence in the rest of the room a second after the hen's.

"Towel," she said, "or similar," and held the bird out.

The farm hand pulled himself over and folded the hen against his chest, where she settled with an air of having intended it all along. "You've got a hand on you. Where'd you learn that?"

"I've caught a lot of things," Piq said, which was true and meant nothing. "You'd be amazed what floats off in a Lunar hauler hub." The old ones. She was wearing that lie now whether she liked it or not.

"Hah. Admiral," he told the hen. "She's been ordering the rest of them about since the crate. She was one of yours, wasn't she? The auditor's chickens."

"She pecked me. During the burn."

"That's her," he said proudly.

Three bunks down, the rigger with cropped sandy hair and a taped-over name tape was watching Piq without any expression at all. She held the look for a full second, then turned and said something to the passenger beside her. He laughed. The rigger didn't.

Across the shelter Hana Szelig was watching too, from among her team. She raised one eyebrow by a few millimetres. Piq read it as *page reference to follow*.

"Thirteen-oh-two," Odum said, looking at her stopwatch. "Livestock delay. I'll be adding a subsection."

Piq wrote the time down, because she wrote down the day she was given. Under it, smaller, she wrote: *Chicken*.

---

After the all-clear she stayed by the comms panel and watched the man in the cardigan shut down his check, one switch at a time, a word for each.

"Item two-fourteen," she said. "Shelter emergency comms."

"Ah." His whole face changed. "That's the easy part. Bridge is sixty metres away and there's a wire." He held out a hand. "Osei Brandt. Communications. There are three of us, so mostly I'm the one who's awake."

"Ansari-Holm. Board."

"I know. There's a notice in the crew mess with your photograph on it. Informational." He winced, as if there was more to it, and didn't say what.

The trick with men like Osei Brandt was to let them be the expert. "What's the hard part, if the bridge is the easy part?"

"Do you really want to know?"

"My mother's in the Occator warren. I sent a note on boarding. I've no idea when it'll get there."

"Ceres," Osei said, with real pleasure. "Ceres is the *hardest* part, this season." He made a fist for the Sun and a fingertip for the Aubade, and stretched his other hand out past the fist. "A few degrees off the limb, from us. For radio that's the same as through it. The corona chews the signal, and solar max makes it worse. So nothing goes straight. It goes round, through the Mars-trailing relays. Store-and-forward. It's how mail went in the age of sail, except the ships are photons and the ports are aluminium." Tap-tap, tap. "And the headers are stripped. Every hop strips the routing for security, so nobody can map who's talking to whom. When a packet arrives I know when it arrived and nothing else. No send time unless the sender wrote one in the body. Not even the hop count. It's a letter with the postmark torn off."

"So you can't know the delay."

"We know the planning figure." He said it the way some people said scripture. "Thirty-four minutes, door to door, Ceres to a ship at our position this month. That's what the scheduling tables use. Of course, the queue—"

"Thirty-four." Piq wrote it down and felt the small click of a number settling into place. Thirty-four out and thirty-four back, plus whatever time the other end took to think. She could work with that. "That's better than I thought. You've been very helpful."

Osei looked, for a moment, like a man who had been about to show photographs of his grandchildren and had the album closed gently in his hands. He rallied. "If your mother's messages come in lumpy, ask me. I'm on late watch most nights. I don't sleep much."

"Nobody seems to on this ship," Piq said, and filed that too.

The message came at 15:52, sorted into her cabin queue under the header PERSONAL / ANSARI-HOLM, S. (OCCATOR). Piq was at the little desk in C-41, because standing had become a negotiation and sitting was merely a complaint.

It was eleven hundred words long. Her mother wrote about the warren's new hydroponics manager, who was unbearable. She wrote that the drip line in the east gallery had been fixed, finally, by someone's nephew, and that the lichen beds were coming in thin this cycle and everyone blamed the new nutrient mix. *Your aunt sends her love and says to eat properly, and to remember that the stew can wait but the bread can't*. There was a recipe. There was always a recipe.

Piq read it twice as a daughter would, smiling at the right places in case anyone was watching through a lens she hadn't found. Then she put her receiver on the desk beside the tablet, as if comparing her sleep data to the day's step count, and ran the text through the pad that lived in the receiver's memory and nowhere else.

The recipe came apart first, as it was meant to. The quantities were indices. The method was the key.

> **TV LOSS ACKNOWLEDGED. EVA FINDING NOT ACCEPTED HERE. TAKE TIME.**
>
> **PRIMARY: UMBRELLA. ASSESS STATUS, CAPABILITY, SCHEDULE. DISPOSITION A IN FORCE: OBSERVE AND REPORT. NO ACTION.**
>
> **DISPOSITION B HELD IN RESERVE: DISABLE. MEANS AT OFFICER DISCRETION. NO CASUALTIES, NO ATTRIBUTION. DO NOT EXECUTE WITHOUT EXPLICIT B ORDER UNDER THIS KEY.**
>
> **STEW CAN WAIT. BREAD CAN'T. — S.**

Piq sat back, and the chair creaked under a weight it had not been built for.

So: A. Observe. She had expected A. Sabine never sent an officer anywhere with B already loaded. It was still there in the second paragraph, folded and waiting. *Means at officer discretion.* That was the Ledger's way of telling you it trusted you, and also that it would not be in the room.

*The stew can wait but the bread can't.* That was Sabine and nobody else. The Umbrella could be dealt with later. Teodor could not.

She did the arithmetic because she always did. Her boarding note had gone out at 13:40 the day before. With thirty-four minutes each way, Sabine would have had it in hand by 14:14 Ceres-side yesterday, and she had sat on it for more than a day before answering. That was like Sabine. She would have walked it round three offices and slept on it. Piq found she minded that less than she expected. A considered order was a better order.

Then she pulled up the hub power-distribution schedule from her audit pack, because Sabine had said to take time and Piq had never known how to take time except by spending it on something.

The hub lighting ran on two buses. Bus A fed the axial corridor sunward of the shelter, the spoke heads and the docking collar. Bus B fed the shelter's own lighting, the observation blister, the antisunward corridor, and a block labelled **UMBRELLA PROJECT — LAB / CONTROL / AUX**. It was the observation blister's corridor where she had watched the lights sag.

She laid it out on the desk as if it were someone else's case.

There were two bursts, both near 02:00, both under two seconds, both broadband and structured. They were not ship's comms, which ran through Osei's panel on scheduled windows and arrived with routing tags. These had nothing she could recognise, and they were encrypted, or something that looked enough like encryption to make no difference. Each time, a lighting bus had drooped as they fired. That meant power, and real power, keyed for exactly as long as the burst and on the same bus as the one laboratory on this ship that Ceres had sent her to watch.

It was not Ledger traffic. Ledger traffic aboard had been Teodor, and Teodor was dead.

So someone else was talking. They talked in the dead middle of the night, in short bursts that would be hard to triangulate, from a source near the most valuable experiment in the inner system. Who would they report to? The Directorate had the relays. The Consortium had money and patience. Tharsis Transit had motive enough to report on its own lab. There was a mole aboard, and the mole was not hers.

Teodor had written *The water's lying* and then gone outside on the hub truss near the coil housing and not come back.

Piq looked at the ink on her forearm, *02:00:07*, and did the last piece of arithmetic, which was not hard. Hana Szelig had told her that afternoon, with the binder held across her chest like a breastplate, that the Umbrella lab was *dark outside working hours, Auditor, because I have a team who need sleep even if I don't*. The lab would be dark tonight, and the auditor's scheduled inspection was not for nine days.

She went up at 01:20, in soft-soled shoes, with a stylus case in her breast pocket that did not contain styluses.

The hub at night was a different ship. The circulation fans ran a half-step lower. The lights along the axial corridor were dimmed to a third, and they made long pale stripes across the curved walls that the eye kept reading as handholds. Frost had furred the coolant lines where they ran along the antisunward bulkhead. Piq could see the white bloom on the steel where the lines passed behind the shelter, and she could smell the faint tin smell of very cold metal in recycled air.

She did not tug ring-born along the rails up here. No one was watching, so she moved the way she had learned to move before she could walk. A fingertip on the wall was enough for steering, a knee tucked to turn, and she slid past the shelter hatch and the blister in near silence.

The Umbrella lab door was a plain grey sliding panel set into the antisunward end of the corridor. It had a keypad and a mechanical override behind a flip cover, as fire regulations required. The project's tag was laminated beside it, and under it was a handwritten card: *NO AUDITORS WITHOUT APPOINTMENT. APPOINTMENTS BY BINDER (TAB 7, ORANGE). THE BINDER IS NOT A SUGGESTION. — H.S.*

The hand was tidy and furious. Piq found she was smiling at it, and stopped.

She left the keypad alone. Keypads kept logs, and logs were where people like her ended up. The override was an old six-wafer cylinder of the kind fire codes favoured because it went on working after everything clever had failed. She thumbed the flip cover up and opened the stylus case. The tension wrench was a sliver of spring steel, and the pick was filed down from a dental probe she had bought in a Kerberos market at nineteen.

In weight you could lean on a lock. Here every push came back at you. The first time she set the wrench her whole body eased half a centimetre away from the door, and she had to start again. She hooked both feet under the toe bar below the panel, wedged a knee against the frame, and made herself into a bracket. She had learned that in the warrens too, picking a ration-store hatch at fourteen on a dare, with her cousin keeping watch and the hatch keeping its secrets for forty minutes.

This one took her four. The first wafer set with a click she felt in her teeth. The third was stiff, and she worked it with the corridor lights striping her hands and her pulse counting along. The fifth wafer was the one you always overshot. Then the cylinder turned under the wrench as though it had been waiting for her, and the panel came loose in its track.

She slid it a hand's width and stopped.

The lab was dark, as Hana had said it would be, but it did not feel like an empty room. Air came through the gap colder than the corridor by more than a sleeping room had any business being, and it carried the same tin smell, only stronger. Across the room a console bank glowed with a low row of amber standby lights. The frost on the line behind the shelter was a light fur. On the feedthrough above that console it had grown into something like a white beard.

*Standby*, she thought. Something big was lying in there asleep, something cooled and kept warm enough to wake fast, and she knew what woke fast at 02:00 and pulled down a lighting bus to do it. She reached for the edge of the panel to open it the rest of the way.

"*You thought me dead, Inspector?*"

The voice came booming up the corridor from the sunward end, round and enormous.

"*Dead men keep the most excellent books!*"

Piq had the panel shut and the cylinder turned back before the second line had finished echoing. The wrench went into her sleeve and the cover came down, and by the time she pushed off from the door she was a woman drifting along a corridor, badly, with one hand on the rail.

Ambrose Tull was at the bulkhead beside the observation blister. Both slippered feet were hooked into a toe loop and his back was to her. He wore a Lunar-blue dressing gown whose hem stood out around his shins like a bell, and his reading glasses floated at the end of their chain. A script was clipped to the rail in front of him, with its pages fanning slowly in the fan draught. He was addressing the bulkhead with one hand raised, as though it owed him money.

"*Your water*," he told it, "*is as hollow as your*—no. No, that's dreadful. Hollow as your *heart*. Better." He paused, and the bulkhead gave him the end of the word back, a little late and a little softer. "There. Hear that? The hub *answers*."

Then he turned, the whole of him, toe loop and all, and saw her.

For a moment his face was a pure old man's alarm. Then the playwright moved in behind it and was delighted.

"Ms Ansari-Holm." He said it as if introducing her to a full house. "Creeping."

"I couldn't sleep," Piq said. It was true. "The ring."

"Nobody sleeps on a cycler, my dear. That's why there's theatre." He unhooked one foot, thought better of it and hooked it back. "I come up at night because we'll be staging in the hub and I have to learn how the room hears. A space has a voice, you know. This one has a delay in it. Half a beat. You can hide a whole line in half a beat." He peered at her over the glasses, which had drifted up in front of his eyes on their own. "But you. Don't tell me. You're getting into character."

"I'm—"

"No, no, it's splendid. I haven't given you your part yet, I'm still deciding, but you've plainly got the *spirit* of the piece. All that business at the door." He waved a hand at the antisunward end and Piq felt her stomach go cold. "Gliding about in the dark with your shoulders down. Very good. Too good, really. An auditor wouldn't move like that, would she? Unless—" He stopped and his eyes went somewhere else. "Unless. Hm."

"I was looking at the frost," Piq said. "On the coolant lines. It's an item."

"Of course it is." He beamed at her with enormous kindness and no belief whatsoever. "Tell me. On your way up, did you pass anyone? On the spoke, or on the ring before it?"

"No."

"Someone's been behind me." He said it lightly, but his hand had gone to the script and was holding the page still. "Three corridors in a row on Tuesday. I turn round and there's nobody, which is exactly what a professional would arrange." He laughed, a little too long, so that the bulkhead laughed back. "Old man's nonsense. Pay no attention. The play's got into my blood."

From his dressing-gown pocket he took a small notebook with a pencil tied to its spine on a length of string. He wrote something short in it, glancing up at her once in the middle, and put it away.

"Thursday," he said. "Nineteen hundred, galley annex. First read-through. Bring that walk."

"I'll try to find it again."

"Oh, you won't have to *find* it," said Ambrose Tull, and turned back to the bulkhead. "*You thought me dead, Inspector?*"

Piq went down spoke three with her hands on the ladder rungs, ring-born and clumsy and much too late. The weight came back up her spine a vertebra at a time and settled in her hips like a debt. She did not know how long he had been at that bulkhead. She did not know whether he had kept his back to the corridor the whole time, or what the half-beat delay in the hub's voice had carried to him while she worked the fifth wafer.

In C-41 she sat down without turning on the light. She found the pen by feel and wrote under *02:00:07* on her forearm, small and careful, in the warren way.

*Tull. 01:40. Hub. Why?*

Then, after a moment, she wrote something she had never had to write on a mission before. It was a question about herself.

*What did he see?*

## Chapter 4: Housekeeping

The bug was the size of a lentil and cost more than Piq's first year of salary. It was a disc of piezo film backed with gel, built to stick to the inside wall of a duct and listen to the whole ship through it. Ducts carried voices better than most people knew. A ship's ventilation was one long ear, and the Ledger had spent a great deal of money learning how to put something at the end of it.

She held it between thumb and forefinger at 10:40 on her fourth morning aboard, floating in the hub's axial corridor beside access panel H-14. She was thinking about Osei Brandt.

He had told her, kindly and at length, exactly how messages moved through the conjunction queue. He sat on the late watch most nights and didn't sleep much. He tapped the edge of every panel he touched as though counting packets. The comms station in the shelter ran off Bus B, and so did the lights that had sagged at 02:00:07. A comms officer was the person best placed to hide a burst, and the person best placed to explain one away. Anyone who could talk that warmly about stripped headers knew exactly what a header was worth with the postmark torn off.

That was one reason. The other was Ambrose Tull.

She had tried tailing him after breakfast, which had seemed a modest ambition for a trained officer against an eighty-one-year-old in a plum waistcoat.

At breakfast he had checked the room in the curved steel of the urn before he sat. He took the chair with its back to the bulkhead and wrote in pencil in a paper notebook, and paper was the one medium nobody aboard could read remotely. When he left, Piq left forty seconds later, with her tray, and followed him anticlockwise around the ring.

The ring did not want her following anyone. At 0.38 g every stride came down harder than her body expected and landed a fraction late, so that she walked like someone descending stairs that weren't there. Ahead of her Ambrose walked as if he had been born on the Moon and found everything since then a little rich. He stopped at a polished stretch of bulkhead outside the gym and studied his reflection, adjusting his collar with great care. Piq recognised it at once as a man checking behind him.

She turned aside into the nearest open hatch.

It was the laundry. Warm air, the smell of hot lint and citrus soap, and a young crewman with a stack of folded towels as tall as his chin, who looked at her with relief.

"Twelve for C-deck," he said, and put them in her arms. "You're late. Ofori's been asking."

"I'm the auditor," Piq said.

"Then you can audit them on the way." He went back to the folding table.

When she stepped out, Ambrose was no longer at the bulkhead. He was coming back toward her, very quickly for his age, and he stopped a metre away and looked at the towels, then at her face, then at the towels again.

"Ms Ansari-Holm," he said. "Auditing the linen?"

"Item one-seventy-one," Piq said. "Textile hygiene."

There was an item 171. It covered biofilm in humid storage. It said nothing about carrying anything.

Ambrose leaned close. He smelled of pencil shavings and an old-fashioned cologne. "Did you see anyone? Behind me. Just now."

"Only me."

"Hm," Ambrose said. He took out the notebook, wrote a line without letting her see it, and went on his way at his own speed. He did not look back, which was either innocence or very good training.

Piq delivered the towels to C-deck, where a woman called Ofori told her she was late.

In anyone else she would have called his behaviour tradecraft. In an eighty-one-year-old playwright she called it a possibility, and disliked herself for it.

He had also seen her at Hana's door last night, with her picks barely back in her sleeve, as he came round the curve declaiming somebody's death scene to the empty corridor. She didn't know how much he had seen. She knew how little she had found. The dark lab had been exactly that: three consoles locked to a biometric she didn't have, a cryostat readout reporting 4.2 kelvin, which was what a coil in cold soak was supposed to report, and frost furring the coolant lines, as it would on any night of the voyage. There was a field-line printout pinned to the wall with no date on it. There was no transmitter, no hidden antenna, nothing warm. If a transmitter existed anywhere in Hana's lab, it wasn't where a lockpick could reach it, and the burst had come from somewhere.

The lentil could settle one of those questions. H-14 was the junction where the shelter's return air met the main run toward the bridge, so anything said loudly at the comms panel would come down this branch. With a night of recording she would know whether Osei talked to anyone at two in the morning, or only to his queue.

She checked the corridor both ways. It was empty: flat light, the smell of water and cold stone, the fans' steady breath. The panel came off with a quarter-turn of four captive screws. Behind it the duct throat was square and grey. A damper sat halfway along on its little actuator, with a flow vane beside it like a weathercock. The air moved past her fingers at the speed of a sigh.

She peeled the gel backing, held the lentil up to the airflow, and let it go.

The air should have carried it a few centimetres and pressed it face-first against the duct wall. That was the physics in the manual. The manual had been written on Vesta, where the Board trained its auditors and through whose stores the Ledger quietly routed its kit, so that everything Piq carried came stamped as Board inspection equipment. Nobody on Vesta had been inside the Aubade, and its duct walls carried a film of condensate that had been building for nineteen years. The gel touched, failed to grip, and skated. The lentil tumbled off the wall in a slow wobble, rode the airstream two hand-spans downstream, and lodged neatly in the hinge of the flow vane like a seed caught in a tooth.

The vane stopped.

Piq watched it not move for one long second.

Somewhere above her a soft two-tone chime sounded, polite as a waiter. A panel light by the hatch turned from green to amber. A voice that had been recorded by someone who had clearly never been in a hurry said, "Ventilation junction H-fourteen. Flow sensor fault. Damper reverting to safe position." With a small, satisfied clunk, the damper swung fully open.

Air changed direction around her ankles. The duct seemed to breathe in. Down the branch from the bridge, carried with startling clarity by a pipe that had suddenly connected two rooms that were never meant to be connected, came a man's voice.

"—not asking you to sign anything irregular, Maelis. I'm asking you to sign a number."

The voice was warm and well modulated, the voice of someone accustomed to being agreed with in meeting rooms. Piq had never heard it before. She knew whose it was anyway, the way you know a ship by its silhouette.

"You're asking me to sign a trim budget that doesn't appear in the published schedule." Captain Odum sounded tired and very precise. "That makes it a number I have to explain."

"It's housekeeping."

"Then housekeeping can sign it."

Piq realised she had stopped breathing. She started again quietly and pressed her shoulder against the frame of the access hatch so she wouldn't drift. Her receiver was in her sleeve pocket. It was useless here, a broadband ear tuned for transmissions, not voices. The bug was in the duct, but it was jammed in a vane, and she had no way of knowing what it was hearing. She had her own memory. That was going to have to do.

"Maelis." The warm voice dropped half a register, and the duct carried that too. "We have been through this. The Board doesn't need to see attitude trim. Nobody files attitude trim. You'll have an auditor reading line items she doesn't understand and writing us up for a—"

"Three signatures." It came flat, from a woman who had said it before. "Mine, the chief engineer's and the navigator's, for any propellant allocation outside the published plan. Section thirty-one. I wrote it."

"I know you wrote it."

"Then you know I'm not going to be the first person to break it." A pause. "Get me two more signatures, Lucan, and I'll sign. Until then it's a number in a folder, and the folder stays in your office."

The warm voice laughed, and it was a nice laugh, which made it worse. "You'd rather wait for the paper than—"

The chime sounded again. The damper actuator whined, tried to close, found it could not trust its sensor, and opened again with a clank. The voices became the shapes of voices, then the fans, then nothing.

Piq stayed where she was, holding the hatch frame, while the amber light pulsed.

Trim budget. Propellant outside the published plan. Something that needed three signatures and was being offered with one. A director of operations who called it housekeeping, and a captain who would not keep the house.

It could be nothing. Ships trimmed attitude all the time. The Aubade held its spin axis sunward, and that took tiny corrections through the electrothermal thrusters, and every correction burned water. The water was booked, budgeted and published, because water was shielding and shielding was life. Nobody files attitude trim, Ferro had said. That was true. Nobody needed to hide it, either, and he was hiding it.

She pushed the thought into her pocket next to the receiver.

The bug was still wedged in the vane. She reached into the duct throat and felt for it with two fingers. Her hand was half inside when she heard a carabiner click behind her, back along the axial corridor, the confident snap of someone who handled clips the way other people handled pens.

Piq took her hand out of the duct. She had the lentil pinched between her fingers. She pressed it into the cuff of her sleeve and turned, already a little embarrassed, an auditor caught doing an auditor's thing.

It was the rigger, the one with the cropped sandy hair and black tape over her name tape. She came up the corridor hand over hand without hurrying, a toolbag strapped to her hip, and stopped a body length away with one boot hooked under a rail. Her pale grey eyes took in the open panel, the amber light, the four captive screws Piq had threaded onto her knuckle like rings, and Piq.

"H-fourteen," the rigger said.

"It started chiming," Piq said. "I was passing. I thought I'd look before I wrote it up. Item two-forty-four is ventilation fault response."

That was true. There was an item 244. It had nothing to do with whether auditors should open access panels with their own hands, which they shouldn't.

The rigger looked at the vane, now swinging freely in the restored airflow, and then at Piq's sleeve.

"Fault cleared," she said. "Must've been ice."

"Must have been."

The rigger said nothing else. She pulled a tablet from her bag, tapped the fault closed, and fitted the panel back over the throat with four quick turns of a driver. Piq handed her the screws one at a time from her knuckle, like a nurse passing instruments, and felt a fool. When the panel was on, the rigger looked at her for exactly as long as she had looked at her after the hen. Then she turned and went back the way she had come. She didn't push off. She climbed, as if speed were a conversation she didn't want to start.

Piq went to the nearest hygiene alcove, unrolled her sleeve, and looked at the lentil.

The gel was smeared with grey condensate. The piezo film had creased across the middle. When she held it to her receiver and pressed the test sequence, it gave her a single long rattle, the sound of a flow vane trying to turn against a stone. It had recorded three minutes and forty seconds of that and nothing else. The grinding had driven the film to full scale from the moment the vane jammed, so two people arguing a few metres up the duct had been, to the bug, a silence under a scream.

The bug had failed completely. It had also given her a trim budget, which was better. She didn't yet know why it was better, and that kept her quiet all through lunch.

Orange tab seven scheduled the controls and protection review for 14:00 in B-7, with attendance limited to the auditor, the lead physicist and one systems representative. Beneath that, in a typeface somebody had chosen on purpose: *Questions outside the scope of the binder will be noted and addressed in writing.*

The systems representative was the quiet older man, the power systems lead, who had a soft Tharsis accent and a habit of laying his hands flat on the table as though calming it. The shaved-head woman with the straw came too, though the tab said one. She sat in the corner and moved her lips with page numbers, and Hana didn't send her out.

"Quench protection," Piq said, and opened the binder at red fourteen.

"Quench detection," Hana said, "is page fourteen. Protection is pages twenty-two and twenty-three. You'll want both, so you'll want to keep two fingers in."

Piq kept two fingers in.

The design was good. Her first discomfort was how good it was. A superconducting coil carried its current with no resistance at all, so long as every centimetre of it stayed cold. If one centimetre warmed past its critical temperature, that centimetre became an ordinary resistor carrying an extraordinary current. It heated, the heat spread, and more of the coil went normal. The energy stored in the field, which was enormous and patient, would try to leave through that one hot spot. A quench was the magnet's version of a dam bursting at a crack. Protection meant detecting the crack fast and opening other gates before the water found it.

Voltage taps ran along the windings, page fourteen. If a section showed a resistive voltage above threshold for longer than the validation window, the system called a quench. On page twenty-two, it fired heaters along the whole winding so that the coil went normal everywhere at once and spread the heat. On page twenty-three, it switched the current out of the coil into a bank of dump resistors, which turned the field's energy into heat. That heat ran by bypass line to the radiators on the darkside hub cradle, green nine, and from there into the dark.

"Stored energy at full field," Piq said.

"One point nine gigajoules."

Piq did the arithmetic without meaning to. It was roughly half a tonne of chemical explosive, held in a ring of cold wire round the waist of the hub, a few metres from a shelter where two hundred and twenty people were supposed to go when the Sun turned against them.

"Dump time constant," she said.

"Thirty-eight seconds to one over e." Hana didn't look at the binder. "Peak terminal voltage under eight hundred volts. Dump resistor peak temperature under five hundred kelvin with margin. It's in the table."

"It's in the table as calculated."

The power lead's hands went a little flatter on the table.

"It's in the table," Hana said, "as calculated, as modelled, and as tested at full scale on the ground at Tharsis Station, eleven times, with Board witnesses. Black tab, item four. You'll find the witness signatures on the facing page, unless you've decided the Board's witnesses are also suspect."

"I've decided nothing," Piq said. "I'm asking what happens if it quenches during a storm."

"It doesn't quench during a storm."

"Everything quenches eventually."

"Not because of a storm. Particle heating on the windings at storm flux is two orders of magnitude below the margin." Hana's voice had gone quiet, which in her was worse than loud. "A quench would come from a mechanical event, a cryo failure or a control error. Those are pages twenty-six, twenty-seven and thirty respectively. And if it quenches during a storm, then the coil sheds its energy into the radiators in thirty-eight seconds and the ship loses the active shield and falls back on its water, the way it would if the Umbrella had never been built. Which is your preferred outcome anyway."

The woman with the straw stopped mouthing page numbers.

"My preferred outcome," Piq said evenly, "is not to find that a magnet holding half a tonne of explosive's worth of energy depends for its safety on detection thresholds nobody has validated in flight. A shield that can't fail safely is a bomb strapped to the shelter."

She heard herself say it and knew it was the right thing to say, because it was an auditor's sentence through and through. She also knew she would have said it even if she hadn't believed it, because the Ledger needed to know whether the thing could fail, and how. She couldn't separate the two, and she noticed that she couldn't, and she put the noticing away with everything else.

Hana looked at her for a long moment.

"You wrote that sentence before you came in," Hana said.

"I wrote it on Vesta." That was true as well. The Board's certification course had made her write one hazard statement a day for eight months, and this had been one of the better ones.

"I believe you." Hana turned to fourteen and pushed the binder across the table so the page faced Piq. "Detection self-test. Bottom of the page. The detection circuit injects a pulse across each tap pair to confirm the taps are alive. In a live magnet the self-test runs continuously, two seconds maximum per pulse, at a variable interval set by the field conditions. It's in your checklist as item one-nineteen, and you'll be witnessing it after the Mars flyby, along with everything else. When the coil is live. Which it isn't." She sat back. "Is there anything else outside the scope of the binder?"

Piq read the paragraph. *Self-test pulse: ≤2 s. Interval: adaptive.* It was a good design. A coil in cold soak wouldn't need it. She ticked item 119 as *noted; to witness*, and moved on to the cryo bypass, where the arguing was easier.

Twice more Hana answered a question by repeating it back with a page number attached, as though stating where something was written settled whether it was true. Once she was right in a way that made Piq feel clumsy, about the heater firing sequence, and Piq conceded it out loud, and Hana looked briefly startled. Once Piq was right, about a missing calibration date on the voltage tap amplifiers, and Hana wrote it on a slip of paper, flagged the page with a fresh orange tab and said, without looking up, that it would be addressed in writing.

"By when?" Piq said.

"By when it's addressed."

The power lead took his hands off the table, which seemed to be how he adjourned meetings. The woman with the straw left with him, glancing back once at Piq as if checking the auditor was still the same shape.

Hana closed the binder and squared it against the table edge. "I'm going up," she said, meaning the hub. "If you're going to the shelter, it's the same car. I'd rather you didn't take notes in it."

"I'll hold them in," Piq said.

The spin-transfer elevator was a padded cylinder with two fold-down seats, a strap for each, and a ring of handholds round the ceiling, which would become the floor if anyone lost track of things. A strip display by the door showed the car's distance from the spin axis in metres, and beside it the local acceleration in hundredths of a g, for anyone who liked to watch their weight leave them.

Piq liked watching it. At 120 metres the display read 0.38, and her knees and the small of her back read it too. The car started up the spoke with a soft hum. As it climbed, Coriolis took hold. The car was being dragged toward the axis but still carried the ring's sideways speed, so the wall leaned into her shoulder as though someone were sitting too close. Her weight began to drain away. At ninety metres she felt her hips unclench a little. At seventy she noticed her jaw.

At sixty-one metres the car stopped with a jolt that went up through the seat into her teeth.

The display held at 0.19. Half her weight was gone, which was still far too much.

"Car two," said the recorded voice, as unhurried as the one in the duct. "Brake interlock fault. Please remain seated. Maintenance has been notified."

Hana pressed the intercom stud. "Szelig in car two. How long?"

A live voice came through, a man chewing something. "Interlock's tripped on both sides. We'll try a jog to the upper landing, Doctor. Hold on to something."

The car lurched upward, stopped, lurched again. At each jog the wall shoved Piq sideways and released her, and the number on the display dropped as if someone were spending it: 0.14, 0.09, 0.06. At the last jog the brakes caught with a bang that rang through the spoke, and the display settled on 10 metres and 0.03.

"Holding there," said the man. "Brake's clamped and it's not coming off till we swap the solenoid. Hour, maybe. Don't touch the door."

Ten metres from the axis gave a thirty-sixth of the ring's pull... no. Piq redid it, because she was tired of being careless today. Ten over a hundred and twenty was a twelfth. A twelfth of 0.38 was 0.032. Ceres gave 0.029.

Home, near enough. Off by a rounding error.

Her body knew it before her arithmetic finished. The ache in her hips, which had been living there for four days like a lodger, packed up and left. Her spine lengthened by some fraction she could feel. Her hands lifted off her thighs by themselves and stayed lifted, light as cloth, and she let go of the strap and let her weight settle onto the seat the way it settled onto every seat she had ever known before the taxi. She took a breath to the bottom of her lungs without bracing. Then she realised she was smiling, and stopped, and then didn't stop.

Across the car, Hana had both hands on the ceiling ring and her eyes shut.

"Are you all right?"

"Fine." Hana's voice came from somewhere behind her teeth. "Don't move your head."

"I wasn't."

"You were. You turned to look at me. I felt it." She opened her eyes and immediately shut them again. "Your head is going round at one point six eight rpm, and so is mine, and at the ring my body has some weight to set that against, and in the hub I'm floating and it doesn't matter. Here it's neither. Every time anything turns, my inner ear thinks the car is falling over sideways."

Piq looked at her properly. Hana was pale under the light, with a fine sheen on her upper lip, and her knuckles had gone white on the ring. The woman who had answered every question for an hour with a page number did not, apparently, have a page for this.

"You're Mars-born," Piq said.

"Olympus Rise. Point three eight my whole life. I can do zero, zero's honest. This," Hana said, "is a lie."

"It's Ceres," Piq said.

Hana opened one eye.

"Point oh three. That's what it is out there, give or take. This is what I grew up in." Piq unhooked her boots from the seat rail, which was the first time in four days she'd trusted her own weight to do anything sensible, and leaned forward very slowly. "Look at the door seam. Not at me. Pick one rivet and keep it. Move your eyes before your head, always. If you have to turn, turn from the hips, slowly, and keep the rivet."

"That's your advice. Look at a rivet."

"That's what we tell children on the transit cars at Occator. And breathe out longer than you breathe in."

Hana found a rivet. Piq watched her breathe out, long, and in, short, and out again. Some colour came back into her face.

"You're good at this," Hana said, still looking at the rivet.

"I'm good at this," Piq agreed. "I'm bad at your ring. I've walked into the galley door twice. I can't put a cup down. It lands before I've finished deciding to let go of it."

"I saw you with the cup." Something in Hana's mouth moved. "On the first day. I thought you were doing it to make a point."

"What point?"

"That the ship was badly designed."

"Everything's badly designed," Piq said, "if you weren't born in it."

The man on the intercom said something about a solenoid to somebody else, then went quiet. The car ticked as it cooled. Down the spoke, very faintly, the ring's fans hummed, the sound of a whole world turning slowly somewhere beneath their feet.

"What's it like?" Hana asked. "Ceres. I've only seen it on the water-rate charts."

Piq could have given the cover answer. She had one: a pleasant, boring account of a hydrology technician's childhood, rehearsed until it was smooth. She opened her mouth for it, and what came out instead was the truth.

"Tunnels," she said. "Warrens. You live under three hundred metres of ice and rock, because that's the shielding. Everything's lit from the ceiling. The ceilings in the old warrens are painted blue, because somebody's great-grandmother remembered what a sky looked like, but it's paint, and you can see the brush marks. You walk by pushing off. Children fly down the main bores in a sort of long, slow fall, and you can hold a conversation with someone in midair across a whole plaza." She stopped. "I'd never been anywhere with weather until I was twenty-six. I'd never seen the sky."

"You've seen space."

"Space isn't sky. Space is out there. Sky is over you."

Hana was quiet for a while. When she spoke, her voice had changed and lost its edge.

"Mars has auroras," she said. "Did you know that?"

"I thought you needed a magnetic field."

"You need a field to steer the particles. Mars lost the global one a few billion years ago, but bits of it are frozen into the crust, old rock in the southern highlands that remembers which way north used to be. During a big storm the whole night sky goes faintly green, all over, and the first time you see it you think it's your eyes. You think you're tired. You wait, and the horizon sort of breathes." Her eyes stayed on the rivet. Her free hand came off the ceiling ring and shaped something in the air. "Then over the crustal fields you get curtains, real ones. Not big like Earth's, but they move. They hang down exactly where the field lines open and let the particles in, so you're watching the shape of the field drawn on the sky. Where the field opens, the sky lights. Tomas and I..." She stopped, and the hand went back to the ring. "My brother and I used to drive out to Sirenum on storm nights to watch. Three hours each way in a rover with bad heating, to see green."

"I would like to see that," Piq said, and heard that she meant it, which surprised her more than anything else that day.

"You'd have to come to Mars."

"I'd have to stand up on Mars."

"You'd be on the floor in a week." Hana smiled at the rivet. "I'd prop you up."

Neither of them said anything after that. In the long quiet Piq became aware that her hand had drifted, with nothing to hold it down, until it rested on the ceiling ring a few centimetres from Hana's. She could have moved it. At 0.03 g every movement was a decision. She left it where it was.

Hana's knuckle touched hers when the car jolted.

"Brake's off," said the man on the intercom. "Taking you to the hub. Hold on."

The car hummed upward. The display ran down to zero, and the last of Piq's weight lifted off her like a sheet being drawn back. Hana let go of the ring, pushed off the moment the doors opened, and floated out into the hub corridor with relief all over her.

"Thank you," Hana said, turning in the air with the ease of somebody whose body had remembered the rules. "For the rivet." Then her face closed, not unkindly, the way a hatch closes for pressure. "The calibration dates will be addressed in writing."

"I look forward to it."

Hana went one way and Piq went the other. Only at the shelter hatch did Piq realise she had not lied once in the hour. She couldn't remember the last time that had happened, and she did not like how good it had felt.

## Chapter 5: Casting Notes

The notebook was heavier than it looked. It had been bound by hand in board covers and filled in fountain pen, and Ambrose Tull's pen pressed so hard that every page had a ridged back, as if he were writing for the blind. Piq carried it under her tray from the ring galley. Ring gravity pulled the tray one way and her tea the other, so she held the cup low and tilted it two degrees spinward, the way the crewman had shown her on day two. She spilled only a little.

The notebook went into the drawer under her bunk in C-41. She looked at the drawer for a moment. She had found the notebook face-down on a prop crate in the rehearsal room, where nobody had any business being unless they were looking for something. Returning it at the read-through would be easy. Explaining why she had read it would not be, unless she planned the explanation now. So she planned one. The notebook would have been left on a bench in the ring galley at lunch, where anybody might leave anything and anybody might pick it up. It would have fallen open, and she would be a little sheepish about it. She liked that version. Then she stopped thinking about it, which was how she knew it would work.

She had four hours before the read-through. She spent them on water.

Checklist item 58 was *Consumables reconciliation: potable, process, and shielding water inventory against logged draws.* It was a dull item, which made it useful. A dull item never needed a reason. The auditor's terminal in the purser's annex gave her read access to the quartermaster's digital log, and the log came up at once with clean columns.

Teodor Vasz had kept it for six years. On most days the entries were a single line: tank levels by section, draws for the farm decks, draws for hygiene, and the occasional transfer between hub and ring reservoirs, each with a work-order number. The handwriting tic that marked his tally inside her locker could not show up in a digital file, but people show up in digital files anyway. They are terse on bad days. They abbreviate the same word three different ways. They put a space before a colon because of a keyboard they learned on forty years ago.

In the early years Teodor did all of that. His entries said *Hub sec 3 — 97.1. ok.* and *Hub sec 3 – 97.0 (gauge sticky, rechecked)*. He wrote *xfer* in one place, *trans.* in another and *XFR* in a third. Sometimes the space before the colon was there and sometimes it wasn't.

From long-loop day 190 to his death on day 231, the entries were perfect. Every line ran to the same length. Every abbreviation was the house standard from the protocol's appendix. There were no rechecks and no sticky gauges. Each hub section read between 96 and 98 percent, as the shelter gauges had, and drifted down by a believable hundredth of a percent per week.

Piq opened the file properties. The auditor's view showed the history field, because the Board insisted on it: *Created: TVASZ. Last modified: OPS-ADMIN, LL day 233, 04:12. Comment: format migration.*

Two days after he died, somebody had migrated his format.

She thought about that for the length of a breath and then wrote it into her audit notes in exactly the words a Board auditor would use: *QM log, LL190–231: entries show post-hoc reformatting by OPS-ADMIN, LL233. Original entries not retained in view. Request originals.* She marked it amber, not red. A red mark would get a phone call. An amber mark would get a reply in a week, and by then she might know what she was asking for.

Purser Halloran watched her from the doorway of his inner office, binders stacked behind him with their strings tied in neat bows. His moustache was combed into two grey awnings.

"Teodor's things are still here," he said, though she hadn't asked. "He had a sister on Luna, Plato side. They should have gone down with the Earth taxi, by rights. But Operations impounded the lot pending the incident ruling, and the ruling isn't closed. So they ride to Mars with us, and we ship from there if Mr Ferro's office signs the release." He looked at the screen, at her, and at the screen again. "He kept paper. Section forty-four, records redundancy, recommends a paper daybook for any officer holding a consumables account. Recommends, not requires. Nobody does it. He did it."

"I'd like to see it," Piq said. "For reconciliation."

Halloran's expression held the conflict of a man who loved procedure and also, perhaps, had been fond of the dead. "It's personal effects. Impounded."

"It's a consumables account record under section forty-four," she said gently. "Kept in his capacity as quartermaster. An impound holds effects against release. It doesn't bar inspection by the Board. I'll sign for it."

That settled it. Halloran's face cleared, the face of a man handed a form he understood. He went to his shelves and came back with a string-tied binder labelled *CHAIN OF CUSTODY — TEMPORARY*. He opened it to a fresh page, unscrewed a pen, and watched her write her name, her credential number, the date, the item, and the purpose of the loan. Then he had her initial the margin. Then he initialled under her initials, blotted both with a square of paper he kept for the purpose, tied the strings and patted the binder.

The daybook came out of a crate behind the shelves. The crate had an Operations seal on its hasp, and Halloran broke it and logged that too. The book was a small, square paper log with a cloth spine gone soft, the kind sold in Lunar stationers to people who liked to feel their lives were being recorded by hand. It smelled of machine oil, and of something else she placed after a moment: the faint mineral tang of hub water, which got into everything stored near the tanks.

"Back by Mars," Halloran said.

"Long before," she said, and believed it.

She did not open the daybook in the annex. She took it to her cabin and set it on the shelf beside the dark sleep tracker that was not a sleep tracker. Then she went back to the terminal and did the second thing, because the clean log had given her an idea.

If someone had smoothed Teodor's entries, they had done it for a reader. The reader was a Board auditor, and that meant her. So the question was not what the log said but what it had to agree with. A water log had to agree with water draws. Most draws were small: farms, hygiene, the galley. The large ones were propellant.

The Aubade's attitude and trim thrusters were electrothermal. They took water, cracked and heated it on an arc, and threw it out of a nozzle. Each firing drew power from the hub bus in a hard, square-shouldered pulse that looked like nothing else on the telemetry. Her auditor access let her read every bus. It did not let her read the thruster control logs, which sat behind Operations with everything else. But a thruster could not fire without drawing current, and current could not be drawn without the bus recording it.

She pulled Bus C from Earth flyby forward. Bus C carried the thruster power conditioners, the docking collar heaters and the axial pumps. She set the filter for rise times under a hundred milliseconds and plateaus over two seconds.

The published propellant schedule for the fast leg was a two-page table in the protocol's appendix F, and she had read it on the taxi because she read everything. It listed four planned trim firings in the first week after Earth flyby, all short and all attitude, to hold the spin axis on the Sun as the ship's line to the Sun swung. Each was a few seconds on the small trim heads at a few kilowatts. A firing like that spent less water than she had spilled from her tea.

Bus C showed eleven firings in five days.

Four of them sat where the schedule said they should, in the planned windows, and they were short and clean. Seven did not. Two were at night, and five were scattered across ship's afternoons in a way that suggested someone was avoiding a pattern. Three were long, a full minute each, and they weren't on the trim heads. The draw climbed to the conditioners' plate rating, which appendix C gave as three megawatts. That was the most the main array could spare them while the radiators held.

She ran the numbers in her head because she did not trust the terminal to keep a record of what she asked it. Three megawatts at the conditioners, and call it forty percent of that reaching the jet, so one point two. Exhaust at six kilometres a second on a good day. Mass flow was twice the jet power over the square of the exhaust velocity: two point four million over thirty-six million, about sixty-seven grams a second. That made four kilograms a minute. Three long burns came to twelve kilograms, perhaps fifteen with the stragglers.

It wasn't much in a hub that held eight thousand tonnes. It was a couple of parts in a million, a rounding error, and Ferro had called it "housekeeping" through the duct. *Nobody files attitude trim.*

But three long burns were not attitude trim. Attitude trim was a tap, and these were shoves. A shove at the right moment, along the right line, did not turn a ship. It moved one.

She closed the bus view. She typed *Bus C: power events inconsistent with published App. F schedule (11 vs 4). Three events at conditioner rated draw, ~60 s. Request thruster control logs* into her notes, and marked it amber as well. Then she sat back in the chair, which in 0.38 g did not quite hold her and did not quite let her go. She looked at the two amber lines one above the other and felt the faint prickle at the back of her neck that she had felt on Kerberos at nineteen. It was the feeling she got when a lock's last wafer was nearly in line.

She told herself it was nothing yet. She had two anomalies, fifteen kilograms of water and no theory, and the Ledger trained you to distrust the theory that came first, because it was usually the one you had brought with you.

The ring galley's schedule board was a long flat panel above the serving line. It showed the ship's day, the hours to the next shelter drill, and the countdown that every passenger looked at at least once a meal: MARS FLYBY, with a ship-date and a time to the second, and below that the days, hours and minutes remaining.

Piq had read it on the morning of day two while she was spilling her tea, and she had kept it because she kept numbers the way some people kept stones from beaches. Flyby closest approach was ship-day 146, at 11:42:09.

At 18:30 on day five, the board said 11:42:49.

She stood in the queue for the vegetable stew with her tray tilted spinward and looked at it for a while. Ahead of her a woman in farm-deck green was explaining to a small boy that the window was not a window but a picture of outside, and that outside was not really that colour. Behind her someone was arguing about whether the play would have real costumes.

Forty seconds was not a difference anyone would notice. Nobody would miss the Mars flyby by forty seconds. The taxis would compensate. But the number on the board was not a timetable someone had chosen and typed in. The protocol's appendix A said so, proudly. The board took its feed straight from the navigation solution and refreshed at every fix, so that the passengers would always see the true figure. Nobody had to sign off on it, which meant nobody could hold it back. Someone who could reformat a dead man's log could not keep this from moving.

And it could only move for one reason. Closest approach came out of the ship's orbit, and the only way to change that number was to change the orbit, or to find out at a new fix that the old orbit had been wrong.

She did not know which. She did not know whether the shoves on Bus C had pushed the number or chased it. She knew only two things, and she set them side by side the way she had set the amber lines. The orbit was not the orbit it had been on day two, and the burns were not attitude trim.

"You're blocking the bread," said the man behind her.

"Sorry," she said, and took a roll, and did not think about it again until she was in the elevator.

The elevator was car three this time. She caught herself bracing at sixty-one metres all the same, where the brake interlock had failed in car two, and she felt her own stupidity at bracing and then relief when nothing happened. As the car rose, ring gravity bled out of her joints. The ache in her hips and knees, the low steady complaint of Ceres bones under nearly thirteen times the weight they were made for, loosened at forty metres, softened at twenty, and was gone by the hub. She came out of the car the way a fish comes out of a net into water.

The observation blister sat at the antisunward end of the hub, on Bus B. It was a hemisphere of laminated glass four metres across with a ring of foot loops around its rim and a stack of folded chairs no one would ever use, clipped to the wall for regulation. Beyond the glass the stars were steady. The hub did not spin, so there was no sweep and no wheeling. Earth was a bright blue-white point low to one side, and a smaller grey spark sat at its shoulder.

Ambrose was already there. He had looped one foot into a rim strap and was floating in his plum waistcoat with a sheaf of paper scripts fanned in one hand like a hand of cards. His lens hung on its chain and turned slowly in the air in front of him. He was giving notes to the glass.

"—and *pause*, and then the reveal, and *then*—Ah." He turned and saw her. "Ms Ansari-Holm. The face. Come in, come in. Mind the scripts, they're the only copies. Paper doesn't crash."

She pushed off the hatch rim with two fingers, drifted across, and caught a loop beside him without looking. She held out the notebook.

"You left this on the bench in the galley," she said. "At lunch. I'm afraid I read it." That was the plan: a small, specific lie wrapped around a small confession, offered before anyone could think to ask about the rehearsal room. "It fell open. I saw my name."

Ambrose took it. He did not ask which bench. He did not look embarrassed. He looked thrilled.

"Did you? Did you really? Which page?" He thumbed it open. His knuckles were swollen, the hands of a Lunar man who had worked with his hands in a sixth of a gee for decades. "Ah, yes. My suspects." He beamed at her. "Casting notes, dear. One casts the spy last, because the spy must be right. The spy *carries* the play. Everyone else is furniture with lines."

Piq felt the shape of the room shift. She knew it was a fair surprise, and it annoyed her that she hadn't seen it.

"Casting notes," she said.

"I watch for type. Mr Brandt, now." He tapped the first entry. "Far too obvious. A man who watches the hatch like that would be spotted in the first act. The rigger, wonderful presence, always there, but she won't speak to me. I've tried four times. She climbs away. One cannot cast a woman who climbs away." He sighed. "Ferro I considered for the villain, but he's management, and management never comes to rehearsal. And then—" He looked up at Piq with real affection. "Then I saw you at that door."

"The lab door."

"At one in the morning, in the dark, with a little case, absolutely *intent*. You thought no one was watching. That's the quality. It can't be taught." He shut the notebook. "And the towels. I nearly applauded. I followed you to the laundry to see if you'd break, and you came out with towels and a regulation. A *regulation*." He shook his head in wonder. "You are an auditor playing a spy who is playing an auditor. It's the whole play in one woman."

"Mr Tull, I am an auditor," Piq said, and it was true, and the truth sat oddly in her mouth.

"Of course you are, dear. That's why it works."

The cast drifted in over the next quarter hour. Halloran came, carrying his own binder. He was to play the Ambassador, a man of few lines, all of them about protocol, which he had already annotated. Dagny Wirth came as stage manager. She had a hub-chief's way of moving, all small corrections and no wasted thrust, and she took the scripts from Ambrose and redistributed them in order without being asked. Two postdocs from Hana's team came giggling. A farm-deck couple came, and a boy of about twelve who was to play a messenger and had been told he could carry a real envelope.

Osei Brandt came a little after the others, headset round his neck and cardigan buttoned wrong. He took the loop opposite Piq and fastened his foot in it as though it were a workstation.

"Telegraphist," Ambrose announced. "Small part. Pivotal. You decode the message in act two, Mr Brandt."

"I'd be decoding it from a store-and-forward relay," Osei said. "Realistically. There'd be a queue."

"There's no queue in my play."

"There's always a queue," Osei said mildly, and Piq marked the way he watched the hatch.

Hana Szelig arrived last. She did not drift in. She came through the hatch hand over hand on the rim rail like someone climbing a ladder she disliked, in the same dark jacket she had worn at the briefing, with a cardboard tube under one arm. She stopped when she saw the room was full.

"I was told," she said to Ambrose, in a voice built to carry to the back of a lecture hall, "that this was a hazard review for stage rigging."

"And so it is!" Ambrose spread his arms and turned a slow quarter-turn in the air. "Dr Szelig, you will play Professor Ilse Vantongeren, inventor of the Heliograph, a device that can turn the Sun itself against the inner worlds. You have four scenes, a cape, and a death."

"No."

"I'm not—"

Hana looked at Dagny and then at the room. Last of all she looked at Piq, who was holding the rim loop with one foot and floating at a slight angle, entirely at ease, with a script in her hand that said *AUDITOR VESNA CRANE (SECRETLY AGENT NIGHTJAR)*.

Something happened in Hana's face that Piq did not know how to read. It wasn't quite amusement or surrender. Then she hooked a foot into the loop nearest the hatch, as far from Ambrose as the blister allowed, and held out her hand for a script.

*Dead Drop at Perihelion* was very bad. It was bad in a way Piq came, over the next hour, to admire, the way she admired well-made locks. Every scene was built to be reached through the wrong door. An Ambassador mistook a cipher for a dinner menu. A messenger carried a vital envelope to the wrong ship. The Heliograph's plans were hidden in a crate of eggs, and Ambrose noted that this would need "livestock liaison". The villain, Baron Stroud, a water magnate, was played by Ambrose himself, because, he explained, no one else could do the laugh.

"'Your water,'" Ambrose read, in the voice Piq had heard at a bulkhead at one in the morning, "'is as *hollow* as your heart, Ambassador.'"

"'I must consult the protocol,'" read Halloran, with feeling.

The postdocs snorted. The farm-deck woman laughed so hard she drifted out of her loop and Dagny reeled her back by the ankle. The boy read his messenger lines as if they were a threat.

Then they reached Crane.

Auditor Vesna Crane entered in act one, scene three, to inspect the Ambassador's embassy, and her first speech was a lie. She said she was there about the plumbing. Piq read it the way she thought a spy in a farce would read it, with a dry edge and a knowing glance at the audience, slightly too smooth.

"'I'm only here about the plumbing, Ambassador. Purely routine.'"

"Stop," said Ambrose.

She stopped.

"Again. Less."

She read it again with less.

"No, no." He drifted toward her with one hand out, as if the line were a bird he wanted to stop flying. "You're *lying*, dear. One can see it from Luna. You're telling us she's lying."

"She is lying."

"Of course she is. But you're too obviously lying." He put his face close to hers with his lens swinging. "Real liars don't sound like that. Real liars sound bored. They sound like a woman reading out the plumbing schedule. The best liar I ever knew was my second husband, and he lied exactly the way he ordered breakfast." He snapped his fingers. "Breakfast. Again."

Across the blister, Hana laughed.

It was short, surprised out of her, and quickly put away, but Piq heard it. Hana looked down at her script as if it had said something to her, and colour came up under the dark of her jaw.

Piq read the line again. She read it the way she had read *IHB-A-4471, active since 2191* to an immigration officer on Earth's elevator, the way she had told a rigger that old Lunar haulers had zero-g hubs, the way she had told Ambrose about a bench in the galley ten minutes ago. It was flat, a little tired, and faintly put-upon.

"'I'm only here about the plumbing, Ambassador. Purely routine.'"

The blister went quiet.

"*Yes*," said Ambrose softly. "There she is."

Nobody was laughing. Osei was looking at her with his head tilted, the way he looked at panel edges. Halloran had lowered his binder. Hana was not looking at her script any more. She was looking at Piq, and her expression had nothing to do with the play.

Piq made herself smile, the smile of a woman embarrassed to be good at something silly, and turned the page.

They broke at 20:45. Ambrose collected the scripts, because paper did not crash and also could not be trusted out of his sight, and gave notes to everyone. Halloran was to find a better protocol voice. The boy was to stop threatening people. Osei was to stop correcting the transmission scene. Piq was to be less brilliant at the start of act two "or they'll guess." He left in a slow tumble of plum velvet, still talking. The postdocs went with him. Dagny stowed the folded chairs that had never been unfolded, gave Piq a look Piq could not read, and went back to her hub.

Osei stayed a moment, looking out at the Earth-point.

"It's a decent signal, the Earth," he said, to no one in particular. "This close. Ten seconds out. Lovely clean link. Gets worse every day from here." He unhooked his foot. "Night watch. Ten o'clock."

"You're on tonight?"

"I'm always on." He said it without self-pity, as a fact about a queue. He pushed off for the hatch.

Hana was still there, by the hatch, holding her tube.

"You're very good at that," she said.

"Reading menus."

"Lying." She did not make it a joke. She pushed off the rim and drifted across the blister, not gracefully. She let her shoulder take the stop against a strut and winced, then hooked an elbow through the loop beside Piq. Up close she smelled of coolant and cold metal and something like oranges. "Is it the job? Auditors. Do you train it? People say yes to you in rooms and you need to know which yes is real."

"Something like that."

"Hm." Hana looked out at the stars. She was clearly working herself up to something, and Piq waited, because waiting got more from people than asking did. "Tab seven," Hana said finally. "Orange. Your note on the controls review. You asked for stray field at the shelter."

Piq had asked for it on the first day, in passing. It was item 140 on the checklist, *personnel field exposure*, and she had not expected an answer before the lab inspection.

"I did."

"I don't like answering questions in writing when people misunderstand pictures." Hana pulled the cap off the tube with her teeth and shook out a roll of heavy plotter paper. In the hub it didn't fall. It hung open between them in a long slow curl until Piq caught the far edge and held it flat against the curve of the glass.

The lines on it were beautiful. Piq hadn't expected that.

It was a cross-section through the hub, drawn to scale. The long cylinder of the hub ran up the page with its tanks shaded blue and the shelter a pale rectangle at its heart. The solar array at the sunward cap was a thick bar at the top, labelled with a small sun symbol. Around the waist of the hub, a dark ring was cut through in section: the Umbrella's coil, two black ovals at either side of the cylinder, like eyes. Inboard of them, tucked against the tank walls above and below the shelter, were four smaller ovals hatched the other way, each labelled *BUCK* in pencil. From the big ovals the field lines poured out in great nested loops, dense near the coil and wider and wider outward, curving up over the sunward cap and down under the antisunward cap and back into the coil from the other side. They had been plotted by a machine and labelled by hand in Hana's narrow upright figures: *10 T*, *1 T*, *0.1 T*, a bundle of lines marked *design rigidity cut-off*, and a broad zone outside the coil drawn in light hatching labelled *PROTECTED VOLUME (equatorial)*.

"Here." Hana's finger, nail bitten short, touched the pale rectangle of the shelter. "That's where people are. Which is the problem, because a ring coil is strongest dead in its own middle. Left alone, the main winding would put several tesla straight through the shelter. So we cheat." She tapped the hatched ovals. "Bucking windings, two pairs, run counter to the main coil and tuned so their field cancels its field over the shelter volume. It costs us a little outside, and it costs a lot of conductor. But inside the bore it's under a millitesla at the shelter. You could put a pacemaker in there and it wouldn't notice. That's your number." She tapped a small table in the corner, three significant figures, signed and dated. "And here—" her finger swept out across the hatched zone along the equator, "is where the field turns a proton round. That's the shield. Anything coming in sideways at storm energies gets bent back out. The tanks catch what gets through. Together, a sliver of what water alone would let in."

"It's lovely," Piq said, and meant it, and saw Hana hear that she meant it.

But she was looking at the top of the page.

At the sunward cap, above the array, the field lines from both sides of the coil swept up, bent in, and met. They did not cross, because field lines never did. They crowded. They ran close together along the axis and dived down toward the hub in a narrowing throat, a funnel with its mouth open to the Sun. The same thing happened at the bottom of the page, under the antisunward cap. The bucking windings flattened the field inside the bore and did nothing to the throats beyond it. Two throats, top and bottom. Two places where the shield's lines did not wall the hub off but pointed into it, straight down the axis of the ship, toward the pale rectangle where everyone would go when the sky went bad.

She did not need to know the plasma physics. She was Ceres-born, and every shed hand at Occator did a dosimetry course before the first shift topside. The first slide was always the same one: a single field line with a proton corkscrewing along it. The instructor's voice was always the same too, bored and certain: *They follow the lines. You want to know where your dose comes from, look where the lines go.* The lines here went straight in.

Hana was talking about the cryostat. Piq let her talk, held the edge of the paper, and kept her face exactly as it had been. That took no effort. It never did.

She did not say *what about the poles*. She did not say it because Sabine's letter said *assess capability* and a weakness was the most useful thing an assessment could find. She did not say it because something the Ledger had built in her said a fact kept was worth more than a fact given away. She did not say it because Hana might already know, and asking would show what Piq knew. She did not say it, and the not saying sat in her like a swallowed stone.

"You're quiet," Hana said.

"I'm reading." Piq let the paper roll back toward her. "Can I have a copy of this?"

Hana hesitated. Then, briskly: "It's in the binder. Green, p11. Low resolution." She rolled the plot and slid it back into its tube. "This one's mine." She capped it. Then, as if it cost her, and without looking at Piq: "The rivet trick. It works on the transfer elevator. I used it this morning."

"Eyes before head."

"Breathe out longer." The ghost of the laugh again, faster this time. Then she was pushing for the hatch, hand over hand, not gracefully, and gone down the antisunward corridor toward the dark end where her lab door was. Piq watched the corridor lights for a while after she had gone.

At 22:40 in C-41, with the ache back in her hips and the bunk's restraint strap unfastened because she could never sleep with it on, Piq opened Teodor Vasz's daybook.

His hand was small and cramped and leaned hard to the right. He used a fine pencil he must have sharpened with a knife, because the line varied. Most of the book was what the digital log should have been: tank levels by section, draws, rechecks, gauge complaints. He wrote *sticky again, bastard* and *xfer* and *trans.* He had a tic of drawing a small box around a number he had checked twice.

After day 150 of the long loop the boxes started to multiply.

By day 190 the tank levels in the paper log stopped. The digital log, the clean one, carried on happily with its 96s and 97s and 98s. The paper book had no level readings at all, just dates and short notes: *gauges as panel*, *gauges as panel*, *gauges as panel*, as if he had stopped believing the panel and had not yet decided what to believe instead. Here and there was a small stroke in the margin. She took out her own notes and laid them beside the book and compared the strokes to the dates of the scratched tally behind her locker hinge. Several matched. The last stroke was on day 231.

She found it on the page for day 214. Under *gauges as panel* there was a smudged arithmetic of figures she could not quite read, crossed out, and in the margin, in that cramped right-leaning pencil, boxed twice:

*D/H?*

She looked at it for a long time.

Dagny-Halloran? Two names, two people. It might be someone to ask, or someone to suspect. Dump-heater, a coil term she had heard at the controls review. Deck/hub, a quartermaster's shorthand for the two reservoirs he moved water between. Or the ratio, the oldest one, the number every Compact assay ticket printed in its top right corner beside the purity grade. She had seen it on ten thousand tickets in the Occator export sheds. Deuterium to hydrogen.

But why would a cycler's quartermaster question the D/H of his own water? It was Compact water, certified at the shed and certified again at the tank. Its isotope ratio was the one fact about it that never changed. Water could be lost, stolen, spilled, boiled off, burned. It didn't change what it was made of.

She wrote all four readings in her notes and ranked none of them. She didn't like that, so she closed the daybook and set it on the shelf beside the sleep tracker.

She had three bursts. The first came at 02:07 on day two, short at four hundred milliseconds and broadband, as if a door had been opened and shut. The second came at 02:00:07, longer at 1.7 seconds, with the lights sagging on Bus B. The third came at 02:13:41, 1.8 seconds, Bus B dipping three percent and recovering inside two seconds, Buses A and C clean. All three fell in the hour after midnight, all three were inside the ship, and the last two were on the same bus as the comms office, the blister, the lab and the console room. Osei Brandt was on watch from 22:00 to 04:00 on every one of those nights. *I'm always on.*

The lab was on Bus B too. She made herself look at that, because the Ledger also trained you to look at the thing you didn't want to. The morning after 02:13 Hana had come into the controls review yawning, a real jaw-cracking yawn that she had covered with the back of her wrist and not apologised for. Piq turned it over and set it down. Hana's team kept the cryostat on a watch rota. A postdoc had complained about it at lunch: four-hour checks, alarms that woke whoever had the pager, a helium boil-off curve that didn't care what time it was. A physicist yawning was not a signal. Half the science deck yawned. And the bursts were encrypted-looking, timed and patient, built to be missed. Hana Szelig, who corrected strangers' soup, did not strike her as a woman who did anything that could be missed.

Then there was Teodor, who had kept a paper book because he did not trust the screens. He had stopped writing down levels because he did not believe them, and he had gone out at night without a spotter to look at something he couldn't trust any panel to show him. Someone had migrated his format two days after his death, and someone had impounded his effects so they could not go home.

Whatever was talking from inside the ship spoke between two and a quarter past two. It spoke from Bus B.

Piq took the receiver from the shelf and checked its charge. She clipped it into her sleeve pocket. She set the cabin chime for 01:20, which gave her the elevator, the hub, and twenty minutes to find somewhere dark on Bus B with a sightline to the comms office hatch and the console room door.

She lay down on the bunk without the strap, with the ring's 0.38 g pressing her into the mattress like a hand. Above her, the forty-page protocol lay on its shelf, initials page signed. Somewhere outside the hull, the Sun was behind her and Mars was ahead. The board in the galley said 11:42:49, and it had said something different on day two, and it would go on saying whatever the orbit told it, whoever wished otherwise.

She did not sleep. She lay there and counted the seconds of the ring's slow turn, thirty-five and a bit to every revolution, and waited for 01:20.

## Chapter 6: Heater Test

The chime went at 01:20. Piq was already awake to hear it.

She had spent two hours lying on top of the bunk in C-41, unstrapped, feeling the ring hold her down. At 0.38 g the weight never let go. It lay along her hips and the backs of her knees, and it sat behind her sternum like a hand that had forgotten to lift. When she swung her legs off the bunk her heels met the deck harder than she'd judged, and the ache climbed her shins into both knees. Six months, the Vesta clinicians had said. With exercise, with supplements, with sense.

She dressed in the dark. She put on the grey coverall with the IHB patch, because an auditor caught wandering at night ought to look like an auditor. The receiver went into her sleeve pocket, its shell printed with a cartoon moon and the words SLEEP BETTER. The stylus case went into her thigh pocket. The daybook stayed on the shelf with Teodor's pencil in its spine.

The ring corridor was in night cycle, lit by amber strips at ankle height. She walked a little to spinward the way the crew did, and twice corrected the Coriolis late. Spoke three's elevator stood open on standby. She set her feet in the floor loops and pressed HUB.

The weight drained out of her as the car climbed. At sixty metres her stomach began its old suspicion. By ninety she was barely touching the deck, and her shoulders came down from somewhere near her ears. The car shuddered through the spin-transfer collar, and the floor stopped being a floor and became a direction she happened to be facing.

The doors opened. Piq let go and went.

This was the part she couldn't hide, and at night she had stopped trying. One push on the frame carried her eleven metres down the axial corridor without touching a wall. She caught a rung with two fingers, swung, and cleared the junction where the spoke-head conduits crossed. The hub smelled of cold air and scrubber graphite, and under that it smelled of water. Eight thousand tonnes of it, or whatever was really in the tanks behind the walls.

Bus B began where the corridor necked down past the shelter hatches. The light changed there, very slightly, from Bus A's cool white to the yellower cast of a panel set somebody had bought cheap. She had learned to feel the seam the way you feel a change in water temperature. Twice now, at two in the morning, the yellow side had dimmed and recovered. It was the first thing in this ship she had been sure meant something.

She passed the shelter's antisunward hatch, sealed and green-lit, and then the comms office, which had a little round window in its hatch. Inside, a single screen scrolled columns of numbers. In front of it, strapped loosely to a chair, a cup of tea tethered to his wrist by a lanyard, sat Osei Brandt.

He looked up before she could drift past. The hatch slid open.

"Auditor Ansari-Holm." He said it the way some people said *weather*. "Is this an audit?"

"Insomnia," she said. It came out easily. "The ring sits on my chest. I come up here to float."

"Ah. Ceres." He nodded as though she had named a chronic condition with a known protocol. "Come in, if you're floating. Don't touch the second screen. It has opinions."

She hooked a toe under the hatch rail and hung in the doorway. She wanted the corridor, the console bay, the dark. But leaving straight away would be noticed. Sitting with Osei for three minutes would only be forgotten.

"You're on late watch."

"I'm always on late watch in conjunction season. Nobody else will take it, because nothing happens." He turned the screen toward her with real pride. "Except this happens. Relay Trailing-Four is holding three hundred and eleven packets for onward forwarding. Do you know how it orders them?"

"Priority?"

"*Size.*" He let it sit, as if it were a scandal. "Smallest first. A forty-kilobyte lunch roster from Deimos goes ahead of anything with an attachment. If you ever need something to arrive, auditor, write it short. Brevity is the only priority store-and-forward respects."

"I'll remember."

"Nobody remembers." He scrolled with one finger. "Queue depth on Four is up nine percent on the week. Dwell's up too. The headers are stripped, so I can't tell you who's waiting, only how many and how fat. It's like counting a crowd by the weight of the floor." He tapped another column without much interest. "Long-range set's been picking something up again. Steady line, well outboard. Too warm for a rock, no transponder. The classifier has three bins: natural body, registered vessel, vessel in distress. It isn't any of them, so it goes in the fourth bin."

"What's the fourth bin?"

"Debris." He sipped from the tethered cup. "Debris is a very accommodating category."

*Too warm for a rock.* Warm meant power, and power meant somebody. The thought surfaced and went. A warm thing a long way off had no bearing on what was happening forty metres down this corridor in the next quarter-hour.

"And Bus B sighed at me again on Tuesday," Osei went on. "Two in the morning, a little brownout, every light on the yellow side. That's my fourth ticket. Engineering thinks I'm describing a feeling."

"Maybe you are."

"I'm describing a voltage." He looked faintly hurt. "Feelings don't come with a timestamp."

"Mine do," Piq said, and pushed off before he could ask what she meant.

Past comms the corridor narrowed again and turned antisunward toward the far cap, where the Umbrella's control bay sat behind its own hatch. She had stood at that hatch four nights ago with a pick in her fingers and a rehearsing pensioner coming round the bend. Tonight she didn't plan to get that far. She had picked a recess two bays short, behind the junction box for the cryoplant feed. It was dark, with a sightline to the bay, and it smelled of nothing.

The cryoplant line ran along the corridor ceiling, a fat insulated pipe as thick as her thigh, and it was white.

She stopped herself with one hand on a rung and looked up. In daylight shift that pipe was matte grey and dry. Tonight frost had feathered along its underside in delicate combs where the lagging thinned at the brackets. Her breath fogged near it. She put a fingertip to the rime and felt the cold sting through the skin, a cold that came from something on the other side working very hard.

Cryoplants ran harder when there was heat to pull. A coil sitting cold and idle needed only enough cooling to hold it there. A coil carrying current, and taking ripple, needed more.

She followed the line hand over hand along the ceiling rungs, the frost a pale road ahead of her. It turned once at a bracket and dropped down a bulkhead. It went through a penetration into the control bay wall beside a hatch that was not locked. The hatch stood open a hand's width, and light came through the gap.

Piq checked her chrono. 01:57.

She went in without making a sound, because the hub let you do that if you had been born to it.

The bay was smaller than she had pictured from the floor plans: a crescent of consoles, a viewport onto nothing, and a lot of pipework sweating and crackling with frost. Hana Szelig hung at the centre console with one foot hooked under the rail and her hair tied up badly in a pencil and a twist. She wore a thermal jacket over sleep clothes. On the main display a single bar climbed in slow green increments, with a label Piq could read from the hatch: FIELD 0.38 / NOMINAL.

Thirty-eight percent. The ring's gravity, it occurred to her stupidly, and then the thought was gone.

In her sleeve pocket the receiver clicked.

It was the sound she had heard four times now: a dry, rising chatter like a fingernail run fast down a comb. Then the short haptic buzz against her forearm that meant *capture complete*. The bay lights dimmed a few percent and came back.

Hana turned. She saw Piq. Her face did nothing at all for one full second.

"Get out," Hana said.

"No."

"This bay is closed outside day shift."

"I'm an IHB auditor. Nothing on this ship is closed to me." She came off the hatch frame and stopped herself against a console, close enough to read the display properly, close enough that Hana's hand moved to cover a key. "What are you sending?"

"What?"

"The burst. It went out as I came in. It went out at 02:00 on the ninth and at 02:13 on the fourteenth. Who's receiving it?"

Hana stared at her. The fury on her face was real, but there was something else in it that Piq couldn't read yet. "You think I'm *transmitting*."

"I think somebody on this ship is putting out encrypted traffic at two in the morning, and I think you're the only person awake in the room it's coming from."

The receiver had slid half out of her sleeve pocket with the movement. Hana's eyes went to it. Piq felt the moment she was seen, and couldn't stop it.

"What," Hana said, "is that."

Piq had the lie ready, as she always did. "A white-noise unit. I don't sleep."

Hana reached out, quite slowly, and turned it face-up between two fingers. The cartoon moon smiled at them both. SLEEP BETTER.

"It's a broadband scanner," Hana said, "in a children's shell." She let go of it and it drifted. "If you're going to accuse me of espionage, the least you could do is not carry the equipment in a nightlight."

There was nothing to laugh at, and neither of them did.

---

Hana could have called security. Piq watched her think about it. She saw Hana's eyes go to the wall handset and then to the display, and she saw the arithmetic arrive. A security call meant a log entry, and a log entry meant someone asking why the coil was at thirty-eight percent at two in the morning. Piq knew that look. She had worn it in mirrors.

"Give me that," Hana said.

Piq didn't move.

"You think it's catching a transmitter. Fine. Put it on the console where we can both see it. Don't watch me. Watch the readout."

After a moment Piq caught the drifting receiver and pressed it to the console's grip pad. Its small screen showed the last capture as a waterfall of hot pixels, broad across the band and hard-edged in time.

Hana pulled up a panel Piq hadn't seen before: QUENCH PROTECTION / DIAGNOSTIC. There was a row of channel indicators and a single key under a hinged guard labelled HTR TEST.

"Superconducting coil," Hana said. Her voice was flat, the voice of someone lecturing a person she disliked. "If any piece of the winding goes normal, it starts dumping stored energy as heat right there, in a spot the size of your thumb, and it burns through. So we watch the voltage across every segment. If one starts to rise, we fire heaters along the whole winding so it all goes normal at once and the energy spreads out instead of punching a hole. That's quench protection. You lectured me on it, as I recall, the day you were going to write it up."

"I remember."

"The heaters are useless if they've failed. So the system test-fires them. A short current pulse through every heater circuit, a few hundred amps for a few milliseconds, to prove they're alive." She flipped up the guard. "A few hundred amps switched in a few milliseconds through metres of cable makes a very good antenna. You've been listening to my heaters clear their throat."

She pressed the key.

The receiver chattered on the console, the same fingernail down the same comb, and buzzed. A fresh stripe of hot pixels scrolled up its screen. The bay lights dipped by a hair.

Piq did not believe it. Believing things on first demonstration was how officers ended up in Ceres's memorial niches.

"Again," she said. "When I say."

Hana took her hand off the key and folded her arms. Piq watched the second hand of her own chrono. She waited a count of seven, which nobody would guess, and then a count of eleven.

"Now."

Hana pressed. The receiver chattered.

"Now."

Chatter.

Piq let the silence run for nineteen seconds, watching Hana's hands the whole time. They stayed folded. The receiver stayed silent.

"Now."

Chatter. Buzz. Another hot stripe.

There is a particular feeling when a structure you have built in your head loses a load-bearing member. It isn't a collapse. It's a sway. Piq felt it go through her now, and in microgravity there wasn't even a floor to blame.

"That's a pulse you fired by hand," she said. She could hear how steady her voice was, and how much that cost. "The bursts I recorded weren't regular. My decoder flagged them as high-entropy. They didn't come at fixed intervals. Interval and shape both varied. That's what made them look like traffic."

"Yes," Hana said. "Because they *aren't* regular when the system fires them itself." She opened another panel, and her hands were faster now, the hands of someone showing her work despite herself. "Automatic test pulses aren't on a clock. They're gated by a ripple discriminator. The heater test only fires when the coil current is quiet enough that the test won't be mistaken for a real quench signature. And the coil current isn't quiet. Not out here."

On the display she dragged up two traces and laid one over the other. The top one was labelled SUNWARD PLASMA MONITOR — DENSITY / VELOCITY. It was a ragged, fine-grained line that rose and fell without pattern. The lower trace was a column of tick marks: HTR TEST — AUTO, with timestamps.

"The field pushes on the solar wind," Hana said, "and the solar wind pushes back. Every gust of plasma against the field induces a little ripple in the coil current. When the wind is gusty, the discriminator waits. When it calms, the test fires. The pulse shape changes too, because the ripple is riding on the current when the heaters switch." She tapped the top trace. "That's your cipher. The Sun breathing on my magnet."

Piq looked at the overlay. Every tick sat in a trough. The spacing between the ticks followed the spacing between the troughs, which was no spacing at all. It was weather.

"Show me the ninth," she said. "02:00."

Hana's mouth tightened. She scrolled back. The log was local, kept on a drive that was not, Piq noted, the ship's maintenance server.

*09 — 01:59:40 RAMP START. 02:00:02 HTR TEST AUTO. 02:00:02 BUS B DRAW 344 kW (PEAK).*

Piq took the stylus case out of her thigh pocket and thumbed it open. Her own entries sat in her own cramped shorthand: *09 / 02:00 burst + hub lights dim (Bus B side).* She had written it standing in the shelter corridor with a hen loose somewhere overhead, and she had underlined *lights*.

Three hundred and forty-four kilowatts. She ran it without wanting to. Bus B fed the comms office, the shelter auxiliaries, the yellow-lit half of the hub corridor and, apparently, this bay. A cheap panel set, a ramp supply pulling a third of a megawatt to push current into the winding, and a cable run that sagged under it for a second and a half. Lights dip. Osei files a ticket about a feeling.

"The fourteenth," she said. "02:13."

Hana scrolled. *14 — 02:13:07 QUENCH DET — SEG 11 — TRANSIENT (FALSE) — HTR FIRE ABORTED — TEST PULSE.* Under it, in Hana's own hand-typed note: *wind spike, 640 km/s, nearly lost it, went to bed 04:10.*

Piq remembered the next morning: the 08:00 coil-status briefing in the hub wardroom, with Hana behind the colour-tabbed binder. She had yawned so hard on the word *margins* that her jaw cracked, and she had glared at Piq as if Piq had caused it. Piq had written in the daybook: *Szelig — tired. Why?* She'd assumed a lover, or a conscience.

She did the last piece of arithmetic because it was the one that should have stopped her a week ago. A mole talking to anyone off the ship needed to reach a relay, and in conjunction season that meant a Mars-trailing station tens of millions of kilometres out. That took a dish, gain and pointing. It took a beam narrow enough that you'd never catch its sidelobes with a novelty scanner in a cabin on the ring. Her receiver had picked up every burst at roughly the same faint strength from C-41 and loud in the hub, and loudest now, here, a metre from the heater bus. Inverse square from a source on the axis. Not an antenna aimed at anyone. A radiator, spraying in every direction, aimed at no one.

There was no mole. There had never been a mole. There was a physicist who wasn't sleeping, and a magnet, and a wind.

Piq closed the stylus case. She noticed that her hand was not quite steady, and she put it flat on the console until it was.

---

"Who authorised this?" she said.

Hana laughed. It was an unpleasant sound and she seemed to know it. "Nobody. That's rather the point."

"An energised prototype on a crewed ship. Unlogged, at night, with the ship asleep. Past the safety review, past the Board, past—"

"Past you," Hana said. "Yes. Specifically past you. You and people like you."

"Then *why*?"

Hana held her gaze for a long moment. Then something in her shoulders gave. She didn't soften. She was simply too tired to hold the posture. She hooked her foot deeper under the rail and spoke to the display, not to Piq.

"Day six after Earth flyby. Ferro's office on the ring, the one with the real chair. He'd had my test authorisation pulled up. Nine months, the Board's estimate, for a live flight-test approval on a crewed ship. He said Tharsis didn't have nine months. He said the Directorate would review the second-coil money at the Mars flyby. If he didn't have a full-field flight log to show them, a real one, a certifiable one, the money went, and then the company went, and then the Umbrella went into a warehouse on Deimos for the next twenty years." She said the next part as though reading it off a page. "He asked for twelve nights of ramps under forty percent, and he said he'd handle the logs. He wants a readiness certificate by day one hundred and twenty. Before solar maximum peaks."

"And you said yes."

"He started asking on the long loop," Hana said. "I started saying yes then." She turned her head and looked at Piq directly for the first time since the key. "He keeps asking how soon. Never how safe. How soon. I assumed that was just what management sounds like."

Piq filed it the way she filed everything, in the cold drawer behind her eyes. *How soon. Day 120. Before maximum. Handle the logs.* Something about the schedule nagged at her, something she'd seen on the board in the ring commissary and hadn't finished thinking about, and it wouldn't come.

"I know what you're going to write," Hana said. "Unauthorised energisation. Unlogged test. Quench-protection system operated outside certification. I can probably give you the article numbers. And then the Board seals the bay, and Ferro says he never asked, which he'll say very well, and I go home on the next taxi, and the only working active shield anyone has ever flown goes cold on a ship that's about to fly through the worst sky in eleven years." She stopped. "So write it. You came up here for something to write."

The stylus case was still in Piq's hand. She opened it.

The finding form was already in there. It was always in there. IHB Form 7, Nonconformance — Critical. Her real credential would sign it with a real key, and the signature would travel out through Osei's beloved queue to the Board and be forwarded, smallest first, ahead of the lunch rosters. Her thumb rested on the field marked DESCRIPTION.

She thought about it properly, because thinking about it properly was the only thing she trusted.

Disposition A. *Observe.* Sabine's words had come through with their headers stripped, as stark as a tide table. Observe what? An energised coil was exactly what Ceres wanted to know about. A live test meant the Umbrella was further along than anyone in the Compact had modelled. But the moment Form 7 went out, the coil went under Board seal, and Piq stopped observing anything. She would be reading about it secondhand in someone else's inquiry.

And the auditor who wrote that finding would become the most visible woman on the ship. Ferro would want to know how she'd been in a locked bay at two in the morning. He would want to know what was in her sleeve. Ferro, who handled logs, would start looking at hers.

Those were the reasons. They were good reasons. She could have defended them to Sabine at the hearing table on Ceres.

There was another reason, and she noticed it and did not look at it straight. It was the way Hana's hands had moved over the console when she stopped being angry and started explaining. *That's your cipher. The Sun breathing on my magnet.* It was the yawn at the briefing, and the frost on the pipe, and twelve nights of this alone while the ship slept. Some part of Piq that she rarely consulted had registered all of it. It had decided, without asking her, that she didn't want to be the person who turned that green bar off.

She closed the form without saving. She closed the case and put it back in her thigh pocket, and the small sound of the clasp was very loud.

That was the moment, she understood later. Not the lock four nights ago, and not the lies. A Board auditor had watched a crewed ship run an uncertified prototype in the dark and had chosen to say nothing. It was the first thing she had done aboard the *Aubade* that was a crime under her cover as well as under her name.

"I'm not going to log it," she said.

Hana didn't relax. If anything she went stiller. "Why?"

"Because I can't defend it." The lie was smooth and well made, and it came out before she had decided to tell it. "I don't have a calibrated reference for your field strength, and I don't have chain of custody on that drive. A finding I can't defend is a finding that gets thrown out, and then you're cleared, and I'm the auditor who cried wolf. I don't write findings that fall over."

Hana looked at her for a long time. The frost on the pipework crackled faintly as the cryoplant cycled. On the display the green bar sat at thirty-eight and did not move.

"That's very procedural," Hana said.

"I'm a very procedural person."

"You're a person who breaks into locked bays at two in the morning." Hana reached out past her and plucked the receiver off the grip pad. The cartoon moon grinned up between her fingers. "With a broadband scanner, built to look like a toy, sitting in your sleeve. You recorded my heaters on the ninth and the fourteenth and you underlined the lights. And you came up here not to *audit* a transmitter but to *catch* one." She turned it over and read the back, where there was no serial number, only a smooth blank where one should have been. "Auditors don't hunt moles, Piq."

Piq said nothing. Nothing true came to her.

Hana held the receiver out. When Piq took it, Hana didn't let go at once. They hung there with it between them, both holding on, a metre from a magnet carrying enough stored energy to tear itself apart.

"I've just shown you mine," Hana said quietly. "On a log I could go to prison for. That's my crime." She let go. "What's yours?"
