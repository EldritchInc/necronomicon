# Small Forces (Book One of The Drift Trilogy)

> Partial: 1 chapter written of 18 planned. This book is unfinished.

## Chapter 1: Before You Begin

The taxi that brought Piq Adebayo-Lund out from Mars smelled of hot plastic and other people's sleep. It had smelled that way for nine days. When the docking collar finally kissed the cycler's hub and the pressure equalized with a sigh like a disappointed relative, the first breath of *Patience* came through the hatch. Recycled air, faintly metallic. Something green under it. Tomato vines, she realized, warm and peppery, a smell she had not met since a hydroponics tour as a schoolgirl in the Ceres warrens. It made her absurdly happy for about four seconds.

Then the taxi's pilot unclipped her duffel from the cargo net and said, "That's yours, isn't it," in the voice of a man glad to be rid of it.

It was hers. It was a dense, overstuffed grey thing the size of a sleeping child, and it hung in the air of the airlock with enough inertia to dislocate a shoulder if she let it build up speed. She took it by both handles, braced a foot on the coaming and pushed off into the hub of the ship she would live on for the next five months.

The hub of *Patience* was a long cylinder of handrails and hatches, lit in the flat white of a ship that liked to see what it was doing. Cargo nets bellied along one wall. Small objects drifted in the eddies of the air returns: a stylus, a twist of foil, a single orange bead. Far down the cylinder, through a viewport, the blunt nose of the ship's own taxi sat in its cradle. A stencil on its flank read GANNET, and beneath it, in smaller letters, PLEASE DO NOT LEAN ON THE GANNET.

A placard bolted beside the airlock read ALL ARRIVALS TO MASS OFFICE. NO EXCEPTIONS. THIS MEANS YOU. Someone had added, in neat marker, *Yes, you.*

Piq had been briefed on cyclers. She had read the regulations twice, which was once more than she read most things. She had been told *Patience* rarely burned, that the ship swung between Mars and the Belt on a fixed loop the sun kept for it, and that every gram aboard was accounted for, because the only way to change course was to throw water out the back. She had understood all of it, the way you understand a fact about someone you have not met.

She had also packed for five months on a ship with no shops.

The mass office was a cylindrical room off the hub's forward end, padded on every surface, with a desk bolted at an angle that only made sense in zero gravity. The odd thing in it was a chair. It sat in a frame of polished rails on four heavy springs, with a lap bar, a footrest and the general air of a fairground ride whose license had lapsed. A woman floated beside it, one foot hooked under a rail, reading something on a wrist slate. She looked up when Piq came in, saw the duffel, and her eyebrows rose a fraction of a millimetre.

"Welcome aboard *Patience*," she said. "Teodora Anselm, trajectory and mass. Is that all one bag?"

"It's one bag," Piq said.

"Mm." Anselm pushed off the rail, caught the duffel with a practiced hand on its centre of mass, and stopped it dead without seeming to try. She was a few years older than Piq, maybe forty, with close-cropped dark hair going silver at the temples and the build of someone raised in real gravity: shorter than Piq, solider in the shoulders. She wore a ship's jumpsuit with the sleeves pushed up. A faded embroidered patch on the left breast showed a tug pulling a stylized rock, and the thread had gone soft with age. "We'll do you first, then the bag. Chair, please."

"I've seen these in training," Piq said, pulling herself into the seat. "It's a spring scale."

"It's an inertial mass measurement device," Anselm said. "A spring scale weighs you. Up here you don't weigh anything, so we bounce you instead." She lowered the lap bar across Piq's thighs and checked the latch with two fingers. "Heavier things bounce slower. I time how fast you bob and the chair tells me how much of you there is. Keep your arms in. Don't help."

"Don't help?"

"People try to help. They tense up. Some of them hum." Anselm drew back a release lever. "Ready?"

The chair dropped and caught and rose again, a long smooth oscillation, and Piq's stomach went with it. Up, down, up, down. It was the stupidest sensation she had ever had in her body. She fixed her eyes on the opposite wall and kept her face composed while Anselm watched a timer on her slate with total, unhurried attention.

"Twenty cycles," Anselm said at last, and stopped the chair with her palm. "You're sixty-eight point four kilos. Your registration said sixty-nine."

"I lost weight on the taxi."

"Everybody loses weight on the taxi." She tapped something into her slate. "Now the bag."

The bag had its own smaller rig, a padded basket on a spring arm. Piq watched it bob with the sinking feeling of someone watching a jury return. It moved slowly. It moved like a planet.

"Thirty-four point one," Anselm said. She turned her slate so Piq could see, though Piq did not need to. "Your allowance is twenty. You're fourteen point one kilograms over."

"Is there a tolerance?"

"There's a tolerance of one hundred grams." Anselm's face did not move, but something at the corner of her mouth did. "You're outside it by a hundred and forty-one tolerances."

Piq had spent eleven years reading faces for a living, and she could read this one perfectly well. The officer was enjoying herself, quietly and without malice, the way a cat enjoys a sunbeam. That was infuriating. It was also, in a way Piq did not care to examine just then, rather attractive.

"What are my options?"

"Two." Anselm held up a finger. "You pay the excess. Cycler freight rate, door to door, Mars flyby to Ceres approach." She named a figure, and Piq did the arithmetic. It came to roughly a month's salary. Her real salary, not her cover's. The Compact would reimburse it eventually, after a form and a query and a meeting, and at that meeting someone in Accounts would ask why its field assessor had not packed like a professional.

"And two?"

"You wear it."

Piq looked at her.

"The passenger tariff budgets every soul aboard at ninety-five kilos," Anselm said. "It's an envelope. Body plus whatever's on the body. We set it high because the ship has to plan for the heaviest person who might walk through the door, and the heaviest person is almost never the one who does." She nodded at the chair. "You're sixty-eight. That leaves you twenty-six kilos of headroom inside your envelope. The regulations don't care what's in your bag. They care whether it's in your bag or on your person when the manifest closes."

"When does the manifest close?"

"Twenty-two hundred, ship's time." Anselm checked her slate. "Eight hours and fifty-one minutes from now. Anything worn at manifest close gets logged as personal effects, and after that it's your business what you do with it. Hang it in your locker. Burn it. I don't care." She paused. "Well. Don't burn it. Fire's a mass problem too."

"You're telling me," Piq said slowly, "that the ship's mass budget can be satisfied by my wearing fourteen kilos of my own luggage for nine hours."

"The ship's mass budget is satisfied either way. The ship doesn't care where the kilos are, it's the same ship. The *tariff* cares." Anselm shrugged, a small, economical movement. "I didn't write the tariff. I just enforce it."

"That's absurd."

"Yes," Anselm agreed. "Would you like to pay?"

Piq had been called stubborn in three performance reviews and once, memorably, in a wedding toast. She was also proud, in the particular way of someone who has made a career of never being seen to struggle. She looked at the bag. She thought about the meeting with Accounts.

"Give me the bag," she said.

---

It took twenty minutes, in a curtained alcove off the mass office that Anselm said was "for exactly this, you'd be surprised," with Piq's belongings drifting around her like a slow explosion.

Fourteen kilograms is a great deal of clothing, and not all of what she had was clothing. There were the boots: two pairs, one magnetic for the hub, one soft-soled for the ring. There was the notarial kit, a leather case of stamps and wax and the Registry seal itself, and that went into the inner pocket of the second jacket, where it rested against her ribs with a familiar weight. There was a bound book of the Ceres Registry's Celebrant's Forms and Liturgies, which she had been told she was legally required to carry and had never opened. There were three sets of formal blacks for the cover, because a notary who looked shabby was a notary nobody trusted with their deeds. There were a coat, a fleece and a ship's sweater bought at the Mars spaceport for a price she had refused to think about since.

She put it all on. Undershirt. Thermal. The first formal blouse over the second formal blouse, because blouses were light but counted. The fleece. The good black jacket. The bad black jacket. The ship's sweater, which went over everything and stopped just above her navel. The coat on top of that. Two pairs of trousers. Three pairs of socks inside the magnetic boots, the soft boots tied together by their laces and slung around her neck like a garland. The liturgy book went in the coat's left pocket, where it banged against her hip, and a dozen smaller things went in a dozen smaller pockets. When she was finished she felt like a mattress that had been stood upright and asked to walk.

She pushed the curtain aside. Anselm looked up from her desk and held her face very still for exactly two seconds.

"Back in the chair," she said.

The chair bobbed slower this time. Piq could feel it, the extra mass dragging every swing out longer. Anselm watched her timer with the same careful attention as before. She did not smile. She had not smiled once since Piq came in, and Piq was beginning to suspect it was a point of professional pride.

"Bag first," Anselm said, and set the basket going. The duffel, limp now, bobbed quickly. "Nineteen point eight. Inside allowance." She made a note, then looked back at the chair readout and frowned very slightly. "Body and effects, eighty-two point six."

"Is that a problem?"

"It's inside envelope by a wide margin." Anselm tilted her slate as if the number might improve from another angle. "But sixty-eight four plus the fourteen point three you took out of that bag is eighty-two seven. You've lost a hundred grams in twenty minutes."

"Where would I have put a hundred grams?"

"Into the air, mostly." Anselm's gaze rested for a moment on Piq's forehead. "That's sweat. The recyclers will have it back by tonight. It'll probably end up in a tomato." She made another note. "I'm logging it as evaporative. Congratulations. You're legal."

"Is that sarcasm?"

"I don't do sarcasm on duty. Mass is a sincere subject." She tapped her slate and Piq's own slate chimed as a manifest slip landed on it. "You'll want to keep that until twenty-two hundred. If anybody asks, show it. Nobody will ask. Orientation's in the ring, sector four, in thirty minutes. Follow the green line down the spoke." She paused. "A word of advice."

"Please."

"In the ring you'll weigh something again. One-third standard. Stand up slowly." Her eyes flicked over the layers. "Especially today. You'll be slower to catch yourself."

"I'm Ceres-born," Piq said. "I've been in a third of a g before."

"Then you'll know," Anselm said, and turned back to her desk.

---

She did know, in principle. Ceres's own gravity was a whisper, three percent of the standard, enough to let dust settle eventually and not much more. Belters grew up drifting through the warrens with a hand always half-reaching for a rail. The Compact had put her through eight weeks of conditioning in the Mars Academy's centrifuge before this posting, and she had walked, run and even done a very bad pull-up in a full Martian gravity. She had thought herself prepared.

She had not done it wearing a wardrobe.

The spoke down to the ring was a long tube with a ladder. Halfway along, the ladder stopped being something you pulled yourself along and became something you climbed down, as the ship's spin began laying a hand on her and pressing. By the bottom she was breathing hard. By the time she stepped off into the ring corridor the weight had settled on her fully, a third of a g on her own body and on fourteen kilos of wool and leather, and sweat was already gathering under the fourth layer and starting to wonder where it might go.

The ring corridor curved up and away from her in both directions, so that the floor ahead rose gently into a wall and then a ceiling, which made her stomach lurch until she stopped looking. The light was warmer here, almost golden, and the tomato smell was stronger. Somewhere ahead, behind a long glass panel, plants were growing in tiered racks under violet lamps, thick with vines and the small hard green shapes of fruit not yet ripe. Water beaded on the outer surface of the glass and ran down it in slow, crooked lines.

She followed the green line along the floor. A passenger couple, a pair of elderly men in matching sweaters, passed her going the other way. They looked at her, looked at each other, and very politely said nothing.

Orientation was held in a low room in sector four with rows of padded benches. About twenty other new arrivals had come up from the Mars taxi, and none of them was wearing more than two layers. A cheerful steward with a shaved head and a laser pointer stood at the front beside a diagram of the ship.

"Welcome aboard!" he said. "My name's Okafor. I'm your purser, your steward, your friend, and if you lose your cabin key, your locksmith, which costs extra. Sit anywhere. You. Yes. Are you all right?"

"I'm fine," said Piq, who was not fine. She lowered herself onto a bench at the back. The bench creaked.

"Mass office?" Okafor asked, with sympathy.

"Mass office."

"She's very fair," he said, as if Piq had complained. "Right! *Patience.*"

The diagram lit up. A long central spine, a docking hub at one end, a wheel turning slowly around the middle with three spokes. A blunt shield at the sunward end. A cluster of dishes and sensor masts. Okafor's pointer danced over it.

"*Patience* is one point two kilometres from nose to tail, and she is the most patient ship in the system. She doesn't burn. She doesn't need to. The sun does the work. She swings out past Mars, comes in through the Belt, swings out again, round and round, same loop every time, a bit like my uncle. Anybody who wants to go anywhere else gets off in a taxi, and anybody who wants to come aboard gets on the same way. Our own taxi is the *Gannet*. Please don't lean on it."

Polite laughter. Piq unzipped the coat a few centimetres. Steam did not quite rise, but she felt it wanted to.

"Here in the ring we've got a third of a gee. That's your cabins, hydroponics, the galley, the gym, and the chapel." The pointer circled a small room. "Chapel's also the courtroom, and on Thursdays it's quiz night, so please don't get married or tried on a Thursday. Up in the hub you've got the docks, the mass office, the telescope club, and the shelter, which I'll come to.

"Now, physics." He clapped his hands. "Everyone stand up."

Everyone stood up. Piq stood up, quickly, because she had been told to and because she did not want to be the last one.

The room slid sideways.

It did not, of course. The room stayed where it was. Her inner ear disagreed. The rising motion of standing carried her body along a slightly different arc than the floor beneath it, and the ship's spin took hold of that difference and tugged her firmly to the left. It tugged everyone. All along the benches people swayed, grabbed, laughed and straightened. Piq swayed too, and went to straighten, and discovered that her body had become a very tall stack of laundry with its centre of gravity somewhere up around the third jacket. The lean took her further than she meant it to. Her correction came late and slow, dragging a great deal of wool behind it, and by the time it arrived she had already overbalanced. She staggered, caught the back of the bench in front of her, and sat back down hard enough that the liturgy book in her pocket smacked her hip with the flat crack of a schoolmistress's ruler.

"And that," said Okafor, beaming, "is Coriolis! Thank you, madam. Perfect demonstration. Didn't plan that. Couldn't have planned it better."

There was applause. It was, Piq would think later, the first applause of her career in intelligence, and she had earned it by falling down.

"The ring's spinning," Okafor went on. "When you move up or down, which here means standing or sitting or climbing the spokes, you're moving to a part of the ring that's going a slightly different speed, and the ship nudges you sideways for it. Same nudge for everybody, big or small. What matters is how fast you catch it. Stand slow. Sit slow. Pour your drinks slow. Do not throw anything you're fond of. If you play catch, you'll find it's a game for one."

Piq sat very still for the rest of orientation, and sweated.

She learned that cabins were allocated by deck and spoke, and that the cabin numbering had been designed, as far as she could tell, by someone with a grudge. She learned that showers were timed and water-metered, and that every litre of grey water went back to the hydroponics bays, so passengers were asked not to use scented soaps because, in Okafor's words, "the tomatoes don't like lavender and neither do I." She learned that the storm shelter, a cramped space in the hub with water tanks for walls, was where everyone went if the sun had a bad day, and that drills would be announced, and that real ones would not.

"The water's the thing," Okafor said, and for a moment the cheerfulness left his voice and something plainer came through. "On a ship like this, the water is everything. You drink it, the plants drink it, and it's what keeps the sun's bad days off your skin. If you see a leak, you tell someone. If you see someone wasting it, you tell someone. If you have to choose between telling someone and finishing your soup, you tell someone." Then he grinned again. "Supper's in the galley at nineteen hundred. Welcome home."

---

Her cabin was on the outer deck of spoke two. It measured two metres by three, with a bunk that folded up into the wall, a desk that folded down out of it, a sink smaller than her two hands cupped together, and a locker with exactly enough room for one person's clothing, provided the person was not currently wearing all of it.

She shut the hatch and leaned against it, then very carefully took off the coat, the sweater and the boots around her neck, and stood in the remaining layers, steaming gently in the third of a gravity, while the sweat cooled on her back.

She would not take the rest off. She had been told twenty-two hundred. A rule was a rule, and the woman in the mass office had looked like someone who would check.

She sat at the desk instead, slowly, feeling the sideways tug and leaning against it, and took the notarial seal out of her inner pocket.

It was a heavy little thing, brass and black glass, about the size of the round of her palm, of the kind the Ceres Registry had issued its notaries since before the warrens had pressure doors. It had a ring of letters around its face, CERES REGISTRY—NOTARIUS PUBLICUS—PER FIDEM, and a tiny window at its centre where a chip of glass caught the light. When she pressed it to a document it embossed the paper and printed a timestamp and her commission number in fine black dots. That was what everyone saw: a notary with a seal, swearing that you were who you said you were and that your signature on a deed of salvage or a mining claim or a marriage certificate was honest and witnessed.

That was the cover. Everything about it was true. Piq Adebayo-Lund held a valid commission from the Ceres Registry as a traveling notary and celebrant. She had studied for it at twenty-three, in the months before her first posting, sat an exam she had passed by a margin she preferred not to think about, and been sworn in by a registrar who called her *my dear* throughout. Her name was in the directory. Her license number could be checked. The best covers, Marisol Quah liked to say, were the ones that did not have to lie.

The other part was the glass at the centre of the seal. If you tilted the seal so the chip of glass caught a light at the right angle, you could see that it was not quite flat. Under the glass sat an optical key, a pattern of light written and rewritten by a chip in the brass, which shifted every few seconds according to a clock nobody but the Compact's own engineers could read. Pressed to a Compact reader, it said: *this is Field Assessor Adebayo-Lund, and she is entitled to know.*

She held the seal to the reader on her slate. The slate thought for a moment, then flashed a soft green, and a file that had been nothing but noise on its storage opened itself to her.

Marisol's voice filled the small cabin, dry and clipped. It had been recorded before Piq left Ceres, months ago now, and something in the compression made it sound, as Marisol always sounded, like a woman reading out a recipe she did not personally approve of.

"P. Brief follows. Text appended.

"*Patience* passes Vesta at a hundred and forty days from your boarding. Closest approach forty thousand kilometres. Vesta Works has a rail. Our estimate, nine kilometres. Electromagnetic. Mars has said nothing about it, which is itself a statement.

"Task. Image the rail. Characterize. Confirm or deny a threat to Ceres. That's the whole of it.

"Use what the ship has. Use your cover. Don't spend the Office's money on anything the cover can buy you. Don't send what you can't stand behind. Don't be clever.

"Clean result and the Ceres desk is yours. I'll sign it myself. M."

The file closed, and a few lines of text scrolled up beneath the voice log. Vesta's orbit. The cycler's orbit. A grainy, fuzzy image, almost useless, of a dark line along a dark surface that might have been a mass driver, or might have been a shadow, or might have been a scratch on the lens. The relevant clauses of the Compact's standing instructions on threats to insured habitats. A note on Mars Republic personnel likely aboard.

Piq read the text twice. Then she sat back in the chair and let herself feel it.

*The Ceres desk is yours.*

She was thirty-four. She had spent eleven years under covers for the Assessment Office, which did not officially exist, of the Ceres Mutual Assurance Compact, which officially insured nearly everything in the Belt. Policies on hulls and habitats, cargo and crops, the air in a hundred thousand pairs of lungs. In practice the Office was the closest thing the Belt had to an intelligence service, because an insurer who knew what was coming could price it, and an insurer who could price it could, quietly, prevent it. She had been a freight clerk at Pallas, a hydroponics inspector at Hygiea, a buyer of secondhand pressure suits at a scrapyard at Juno whose name she had never been able to spell. Under every one of those, the notary's commission had travelled with her, renewed every three years, a standing credential that explained why a freight clerk might be trusted to witness a cargo deed or why a scrapyard buyer carried a seal. The covers changed. The commission was the one thing that never did. She had been good at all of it. She read rooms. She read people. She could tell in a single conversation who was lying and who was frightened and who wanted something and would trade for it, and she had been right often enough that her reviews used words like *decisive* and *instinctive*.

They had also, every one of them, used the word *impatient*.

She had never stayed anywhere longer than a posting lasted. She had never had a desk, or a window, or a front door that was hers under her own name. She had had a mother on Ceres who sent her long messages about other people's grandchildren, and a series of lodgings that smelled of other people's dinners, and no one, for a very long time now, who knew which of her selves was the one underneath.

One hundred and forty days. One rail. Confirm or deny. Then home.

She pulled up the ship's telescope schedule on her slate and found a listing for an amateur club, meeting in the hub observatory, *all welcome, ask for Juniper*. She added it to her calendar. Then she pulled up the passenger directory, out of habit, to see who she was sharing five months with.

The directory listed everyone aboard by deck and cabin, with an optional line for occupation. She found herself: *Adebayo-Lund, P. — Ring 2 / 4-17. Notary Public & Marriage Celebrant, Ceres Registry. Commissions accepted.*

She had not written *commissions accepted*. The Registry's template must have added it. She frowned at it for a moment, thought about changing it, and decided that a notary who changed her listing on her first day was a notary with something to hide.

She scrolled on. Mars Republic attaché, travelling to Hygiea. Three ice-prospectors. A family with five children, which explained a crayon drawing she had seen taped to a hatch in the spoke. A student from Pallas. A man who listed his occupation as *retired, and enjoying it*. A woman who listed hers as *please don't ask*. Near the end she found *Fellowship of the Long Orbit: Br. L. Ostlund, Celebrant*, with a note that the Fellowship's ceremonies required eight weeks' notice and the completion of preparatory forms. She frowned at that for a moment too, and then let it go. It was a big ship. Surely it had room for two people who knew how to read a vow.

She dimmed the slate and sat in the warm small cabin, under five layers, a third of her own weight pressing her into the chair, and listened to the ship. The low hum of the air. The faint creak of the ring as it turned. Somewhere far down the corridor, children laughing, and a tinny, cheerful music she couldn't place. Under it all, she thought, if she listened very carefully, the long silence outside the hull, where the light from home took half an hour to arrive.

She was asleep in the chair when the chime went at the hatch.

---

It was eighteen thirty-one, by the clock on her slate. She had been aboard for five hours and thirty-one minutes. She sat up, too fast, and the chair and the cabin and most of her internal organs slid to the left. She clutched the desk, waited for the world to stop arguing with her, and then stood more carefully and went to the hatch.

Two people stood outside. The man was enormous, two metres tall and broad with it, with a short red beard and the stooped, apologetic posture of someone who had spent his life clearing doorways by a finger's width. The woman beside him came up to roughly his elbow. She was small and neat and composed, with black hair pinned up and an expression of fierce, organized hope. She held a slate. He held, inexplicably, a small potted tomato plant.

"Hello," the woman said. "Are you P. Adebayo-Lund? Notary and celebrant?"

There was a short pause in which Piq registered several things: that she was standing in a doorway wearing two blouses, two jackets, a fleece, an undershirt, a thermal and two pairs of trousers, and that she was visibly damp; that the woman's slate showed the passenger directory, open at her listing; and that the man was looking at her with the open, astonished courtesy of someone who had seen her fall over at orientation, or heard about it, and very badly wanted not to mention it.

"Yes," she said.

"Oh, wonderful." The woman let out a breath. "I'm Mei-Lin Okoro. This is Halvard Brekke. We're getting married."

"Congratulations," said Piq.

"Thank you," said Halvard. He held out the tomato plant. "This is for you. They had them in the hydroponics shop. We didn't know what notaries like."

Piq took it. It was warm from his hands and it smelled exactly like the corridor. "I," she began, and found she had nothing to add, so she said, "Thank you."

"We'd like you to do it," Mei-Lin said. "The ceremony. Before Vesta, if that's all right. In the hub, in free fall, if that's allowed. We met in free fall. On a tug. It's a long story."

"It's not that long," said Halvard.

"It's a little long," said Mei-Lin. "We'll tell you." She turned her slate around and held it up. "We know there's another celebrant aboard. The Fellowship one. We went to see him." Something passed between the two of them, a brief and eloquent look. "He gave us a booklet."

"Eleven forms," said Halvard. "And an eight-week course in orbital humility. With an exam."

"We've been engaged for four years," said Mei-Lin. "We are not, at this point, very humble."

Piq looked at them: the giant and his small, determined bride, on her doorstep, nearly six hours after she had come aboard a ship with no way off it for five months. She thought about the leather-bound book of Forms and Liturgies banging against her hip, which she had never opened. She thought about her exam, and the registrar who had called her *my dear*, and the fact that in eleven years of carrying this commission across the Belt, under one cover after another, she had notarized four hundred and twelve documents and never once, not one single time, married anybody.

She thought, very clearly, *A notary who turns down her first commission on her first day is a notary with something to hide.*

She thought, less clearly, under that, that they were both looking at her as though she were the answer to something.

"I'd be honoured," said Piq Adebayo-Lund, and somewhere inside the fourth layer the sweat ran down her spine.

Mei-Lin's face broke into a smile so sudden and wide that it changed the shape of the corridor. Halvard made a small noise, a sort of happy exhale, and reached as if to hug Piq, remembered she was a stranger, and patted her shoulder instead. She swayed and he caught her elbow. The tomato plant survived.

"Thank you," Mei-Lin said. "Thank you. We'll come back tomorrow with the paperwork. Is there paperwork?"

"There is always paperwork," said Piq, with total authority, and no idea whatsoever what it was.

When the hatch closed she stood in the middle of the tiny cabin and looked at the tomato plant, and then at the book in her pocket, and then at the clock on her slate. Three hours and twenty-nine minutes until the manifest closed and she could take off her trousers.

She sat down slowly, against the sideways pull of the turning ship, put the plant on the desk beside the brass seal, and opened the Celebrant's Forms and Liturgies of the Ceres Registry to the first page.

*Chapter One,* it read. *Before You Begin.*
