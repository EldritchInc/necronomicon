# Chapter 17: The Persons Column

At 1000 the crawler crested the first ridge back toward K4. For a moment the cab tilted nose-high on a slab the colour of old ivory, and everything loose in it slid half a centimetre aft and stopped. Seven people were packed into a space built for four, so nobody slid further than the knees of the person behind.

"Eighteen point four," Teo said from the rear pod. "Lucky, bear left of the dark seam. Your last pass bent the tread guard."

"I saw it."

"Then see it again."

Piq sat with her back against the forward bulkhead. Oduya's boots were braced against her shins, and the notebook rested on her thigh where the jolts couldn't reach it. She had written the shortfall twice already, once in pencil and once in the ledger column. Seventy metres per second. One thousand one hundred and forty kilograms. She had drawn a square beside it, the mark for *later*, and the later was coming fast. Every metre of tread brought it closer.

Rhee lay on the litter along the centre line, strapped at chest and thigh. Each time the crawler pitched he coughed, wet and short, and turned his head to put it into the cloth Oduya held. The cloth had been grey when they left the Grace. It had a darker grey in it now. Teo had wedged herself between the litter and the starboard jacket wall, one gloved hand on Rhee's sternum and one on her slate. Every few minutes she leaned close to his face and said something Piq couldn't hear over the drive whine. Each time he nodded once.

Amaya was beside Teo, small inside a suit that had been sized for someone larger. The suit's chest was distorted, as if she'd swallowed a length of pipe. The slate was in there, and so was the wrapped cylinder. She hadn't touched it since the Grace, and she hadn't stopped knowing it was there. Piq had watched her hand drift toward it twice and come back.

Wierzbicki sat across from Piq with a pack on his knees. He had been ready when they arrived, and as far as Piq could tell he had been ready for fourteen months. It was a stained Ninefold Grace stowage sack with its drawstring knotted three times. He held it the way people held things they had already decided to lose.

Piq leaned forward until her helmet nearly touched his. "I'll need a manifest of everything that came aboard," she said. "Everything you carry, everything in that bag. I'm sorry. It's the number."

He looked at her for a while. His face had the stretched clarity of someone who had been underfed for a year. Then he loosened the first of the three knots.

---

The ridges came back in reverse order. Piq counted them against the inertial log, and the inertial log agreed with the stars. At 1041 the Mule rose over the last plate. It stood on its three legs on R-K4 under its blanket of hand-cut blocks, frost-white with a faint pink cast where Jupiter's light lay across it from the horizon. Seen from outside like this, it looked like a building someone had wrapped for shipping.

"Twenty-four point nine," Teo said. "Mate us up, Lucky. Gently."

The soft collar found the Mule's lower lock on the second try. The latches cycled and the pressure equalised with a sigh that Piq felt in her ears. Lucky went through first, as Teo had written it, and Teo went second. Then came the litter. Oduya and Wierzbicki fed Rhee up through the collar while Teo took his weight from above. In Europa's gravity a man and a litter together weighed about as much as a full grocery sack, but they did not move like one. They kept their momentum and swung on the straps, so Teo had to catch the head end and hold it while the foot end finished its arc.

Amaya went next. Piq went last. She sealed the crawler behind her and heard it settle on its tracks with the faint creak of cooling metal. It had always been a one-way machine in the ledger, written off since Callisto and never carried in the ascent mass. She noted the time and gave it no further thought, because she couldn't afford to.

Inside the Mule, the dosimeter at the couch read 3.8 millisieverts per hour, and the cabin read 2.6. Teo checked both, wrote them down, and turned to the cabin full of people with the pencil stub already back behind her ear.

"Window?" she said.

Lucky had his head in the flight deck. "Margin's line comes over at 1528. Ascent burn at 1511 to meet her low. Four and a half hours on the ground."

"That's twelve to eighteen millisieverts for everyone, sitting still." Teo looked at the tables on the bulkhead, then at the survivors, and something in her face reorganised. "Right. Rhee on the couch. No, not the couch, the couch has the window. Rhee in the dark grey, aft, against the tank."

That was where Piq had slept on the way down. Piq said nothing. She unclipped her bedroll from the wall and moved it.

---

The audit began at 1104.

She did it aloud, because there was no time to do it twice and nobody could check a ledger they hadn't heard. She wedged herself in the hatch between cabin and flight deck, notebook on the fold-down shelf, and gave the procedure to the room in the same voice she had used on the dock crew in Bay Three on her second day at Asgard. It felt like a hundred years ago.

"Gross is the ship as it sits," she said. "Tare is the ship we have to keep. Contents are everything else. I'm going to name every line. If it isn't named, it stays. If you want something to stay, tell me what it weighs and why, and I'll write it in. Nobody throws anything out until I've written it down."

"How short?" said Rhee from the dark grey. His voice was a scrape.

Piq looked at Teo. Teo nodded.

"Seventy metres a second on the match leg," Piq said. "That's one thousand one hundred and forty kilograms. Every kilo we drop now buys about six centimetres per second at the end. Sixteen kilos makes one metre."

Nobody spoke. Somewhere aft the reactor made the small ticking noise of a drum held cold.

Consumables were arithmetic, and she let the arithmetic carry them. They kept one day of water for seven and a day and a half of food. Everything else went: rations, the second scrubber canister, the spare filters, one of two medical kits after Teo had gutted it into the other. ENERGY (SAVOURY) went into the lock brick by brick, and Piq caught herself counting the bricks the way her father had counted crates. She didn't stop.

Tools hurt more. Lucky held onto the ice saw and a pry bar for the EVA. Piq let him, and took everything else. "Two things can break on the way up," she said. "The reactor, and you can't fix that with a bonding gun. The hull, and if the hull goes we're dead before the gun warms up. I'm keeping the patch kit. Three kilos." Lucky looked at her, then put the bonding gun on the pile. The spare suit parts followed, and the second litter, and the crawler's charging umbilical, which no longer had a crawler to charge.

After forty minutes the pile reached the inner hatch. Piq ran the column. It came to two hundred and nine kilograms.

Thirteen metres per second. Fifty-seven still to find.

"Personal," Piq said.

She started with herself, because that was how you did it.

The F2 comms unit hung where it had hung all the way from Callisto, strapped to the frame beside her sleeping place, which was now Rhee's. It was dull grey and the size of a lunchbox. Its housing still bore the Ibis Metrology stencil that had got it through the mass intake: AUDIT REFERENCE MASSES, CERTIFIED, DO NOT OPEN. The receiver inside was the only reason any of them had known Amaya was alive. For two weeks it had been the only thing joining Callisto to Europa. Before that, for longer than Piq liked to count, it had been her only line to Augustin Pell, and the place where she put everything she couldn't say out loud on Asgard.

The relay was dark and would stay dark for another fifteen days. Margin had its own radios. They were standing on the moon the receiver had been built to listen to, and there was nothing left on this moon to listen for.

She unclipped the straps. The unit came free with a small rip of hook-and-loop. She weighed it on the hanging scale: two point zero four kilograms, a little over the stencil. The housing held ten years of grit, and nobody had ever been allowed to open it to clean it.

"Two point oh four," she said, and wrote it down. Twelve centimetres per second.

"That's your..." Lucky began.

"It's two kilograms."

"That's your phone home." He said it without heat, as if he were reading a label.

"I don't have one," Piq said, and she put the unit on the pile.

She didn't look at Teo, but she knew where Teo was the way she knew where the cabin's centre of mass was, with her whole body. Teo stood by the litter with her hand flat on Rhee's chest, and she had stopped moving.

Lucky reached past Piq and tapped the housing three times with his knuckle: once, then twice quickly. It was the same pattern he used on the Mule's hull before every burn. Then he went forward without a word.

Piq turned the page. "My notebook stays," she said. "Seventy grams. It has the nav residuals in it, and the tile map, and the seal logs. It's evidence."

Teo emptied her pockets without being asked: a roll of medical tape, the tar-tea sachets, and her reading glasses, which she kept. The tea went on the pile, all eleven sachets, thirty-three grams.

Lucky gave up a sweater, two meal bars he'd hidden in the flight deck, and a small brass weight shaped like a fish. Piq knew it was the tie-weight for his wedding knot, and she knew because she had certified it.

"Keep it," she said. "Fourteen grams. It's under the noise."

"Nothing's under the noise. You told everybody."

"I'll write it in. It goes under exceptions."

He put it back in his pocket. A muscle moved in his jaw.

Oduya had almost nothing: a comb, a photograph laminated in plastic gone yellow, and a cut-down spoon. She put the spoon on the pile and kept the photograph. Piq wrote down nine grams and didn't ask.

Rhee said, "Me."

Every one of them stopped.

"Sixty-one kilos," Rhee said. He had his eyes closed. "I weighed myself on the cargo scale in the Grace last month. Bones and a cough. Four metres a second. More than anyone's pile."

"No," Amaya said.

"I'm not asking you, Anand. I'm asking her." He opened his eyes and found Piq in the hatch. "You're the one who counts. Count it."

Piq had done it before he finished the sentence. Her mind did that without permission: sixty-one kilos, three point seven five metres per second against fifty-seven needed. It wouldn't come close. That was beside the point, and she was aware, as she framed the answer, that she had reached for the arithmetic first because the arithmetic was easier than the other thing.

"I don't take people," she said. "Not into the ledger and not off it. You aren't contents, Captain. You're why there's a ship."

"That's a pretty line."

"It's a procedural one. I'll show you the page." She held it up. At the top of the column, in her small block capitals, before any of the masses: PERSONS ABOARD: 7. FIXED.

Rhee looked at it for a long time. Then he coughed into the cloth, and Teo pressed him gently back.

"Four point six seconds," Teo said to nobody, and wrote something on her slate.

---

Wierzbicki had the third knot open. He was taking things out one at a time and laying them on his knees in a row, like a merchant showing goods to a buyer he didn't like.

First came a knitted cap, then a slate with a cracked face, then a packet of letters bound in medical tape and a small wooden box that rattled. Then a jacket folded very flat and four Ninefold Grace crew patches. Then three short steel chains, and on each chain a pair of stamped tags, worn bright at the edges where they had rubbed together for fourteen months.

"Sefton," Wierzbicki said, touching the first chain. "Bui. Lachance."

Piq knew the first name. She had read it in a Lagrange claim file in the dark of her quarters on Asgard: *Pilot K. Sefton deviated to a low-energy trajectory contrary to filed plan, constituting operator error under Section 4.* The file said nothing else about Sefton. It mentioned no knitted cap and no wooden box.

"Where are they?" Teo asked quietly.

"In the ice, up the fissure. Captain brought them down off the surface the first week, so they wouldn't be in the sky." Wierzbicki didn't look at Rhee. "He went up twice. That's where his dose came from, mostly. I packed their things the day the pulses started getting answers. I thought, when somebody comes."

Each body in its suit would have been ninety kilos or more. They had never been in any ledger anyone had drawn, and Piq had not drawn them into hers. She had understood before the crawler left Callisto that the dead of the Ninefold Grace would stay on Europa. She had understood it as a number. Here they were as a cap and some letters, and she understood it again.

She weighed the tags together: forty-one grams. "They come. I'm writing them in the persons column."

Wierzbicki's eyebrows moved.

"They were aboard," Piq said. "The Grace manifest lists seven persons. I'm reconciling it."

She weighed the rest because she had to, and read the total aloud: three point one kilograms, about nineteen centimetres per second.

Wierzbicki looked at the letters a long time. Then he picked up the knitted cap and put it on, pulling it down over his cropped hair. It was too big for him.

"That's on my head," he said. "My head's in the persons column."

Piq wrote *cap, 80 g, worn*, and drew no line through it.

He put the letters, the box, the jacket and the patches back into the sack and pulled the drawstring. Then he took the sack to the lock himself and laid it on top of the rations and the comms unit and the bonding gun, very square, the way someone sets a thing on an altar.

---

The core was last.

Amaya unsealed her chest panel and took out the slate first, then the cylinder, and held both. It was wrapped in silvered blanket and taped at both ends, about forearm length and three fingers thick. Frost had started to form on the tape where the cabin's moisture met the cold inside.

"I know," Amaya said, before Piq could open her mouth. "It's ice. It's an ice core from the bore. It's water."

"I haven't said anything."

"It's the sample from where the hydrophone was. From the bottom, where the brine starts." She was speaking fast, and her voice was steady in a way that was costing her. "If anything's in the water it's in that. Or nothing is. I haven't looked, I didn't have anything to look with. But if we leave it, nobody gets to know. Not until somebody else crashes here."

"What does it weigh?"

Amaya blinked. "Point eight. Point eight one, on the Grace scale. I knew you'd ask."

Amaya clipped the cylinder to the scale with hands that trembled a little.

"Point seven nine," Piq said.

"It's been sublimating."

"It has." Piq handed it back. "Five centimetres per second. I'm writing it in."

Amaya didn't take it. "You just made Wierzbicki throw out three kilos of..."

"I didn't make him do anything. He weighed it and decided." Piq turned the notebook so Amaya could see the bottom of the page. There, below the totals and below the line her father had taught her to draw before you were allowed to say *done*, was a short column with its own heading: EXCEPTIONS. *Wedding weight (Mbeki), 14 g. Cap, 80 g, worn. Core sample (Anand A.), 0.79 kg.*

"Exceptions go last," Piq said. "In their own place, where everyone can see them. They count. I don't fold them in."

From the side Teo said, very quietly, "Take it, Amaya."

Amaya took it.

---

It came to two hundred and fourteen kilograms. Thirteen point one metres per second, fifty-six point nine still to find. It was 1151.

"Everything else we can drop is water," Piq said.

"Crust or jacket," said Teo.

The jacket meant Tank C, wardrobe-sized, sitting aft of the cabin where its shadow and the crust's and the reactor bulkhead's overlapped into the dark grey. It could be vented from inside with a valve and a hiss. The crust meant cutting blocks off the hull from outside, in vacuum, under two hundred and nine millisieverts an hour.

Teo had the laminated tables spread over her knees and the Mule sketched in grease pencil, crust shaded, tank boxed.

"Venting Tank C costs nobody surface dose and costs everyone in the transit. Pull nine hundred kilos out of it and the dark grey goes light grey and the light grey goes white. Through the cuff, that's..." Her pencil went down a column. "Rhee doubles. Everyone else up forty, fifty percent."

"And crust?"

"Crust is uneven. There are faces we don't use." The pencil tip found the dorsal-aft corner where the hull curved into the reactor bulkhead. "In ascent attitude this face points down-track and outboard. The reactor shield already covers that angle. These three blocks shadow nothing except the bulkhead. The fourth's partial, and its partial lands on the litter."

Piq had the block log. She had built it herself on Callisto, through the nights when the Brigade was cutting and stacking. Block 22: 702 kg. Block 23: 688. Block 24: 731. Block 25, the partial: 694.

"Any one closes most of it," Piq said. "Seven hundred kilos is forty-three metres. Two close it with margin."

"Twenty-two and twenty-four. Fourteen hundred and thirty-three kilos." Teo wrote it. "That's more than you need. You'd be over by..."

"Thirty-one metres. A cushion."

"Cushion's fine. That's an EVA. Strap cut, lever off, push clear of the legs. In Europa gravity a seven-hundred-kilo block is ninety kilos of weight and seven hundred of momentum. One person can lever it if it's free. If it's frozen to the hull it needs two."

"Who goes?"

"I go." Teo had already written it at the bottom of the sheet: *EVA (A): ANAND T.* "Lucky flies. He doesn't get one microsievert we can avoid. Oduya is holding Rhee's line. Amaya's lifetime is one point two one sieverts. She doesn't go outside again until she's in a clinic. Wierzbicki has nine hundred and forty millisieverts and two broken fingers he hasn't mentioned. That leaves you and me. I've got the medic waiver. The rest of you are on statute."

"Statute's fifty a year," Piq said. "I passed it on the way down."

"And I'm on my waiver. Which I wrote. Which I will log." Teo's eyes were very clear. "Twenty-two is mine, twelve minutes by the table. Twenty-four is yours, twelve minutes. Then we're in."

"Fine," said Piq.

She said it so easily that Teo looked at her a second longer than the word deserved.

---

They suited up in the lower lock among the jettison pile. Lucky checked their seals with the flat economy of a man who had checked a thousand. Then he put a hand flat on each of their chest plates, Teo's first and then Piq's, once and then twice.

"Twelve minutes," he said. "I'm counting."

"Count out loud," said Teo.

The pump took the lock down. The hatch opened onto the floor of Europa and the light came in.

It was not like Callisto. Piq had already filed it as a second Callisto with a worse number on it, and it wasn't. The plain of R-K4 was smooth and pale, glazed in places like the inside of a cup. Far off stood the shattered towers of Conamara, tilted plates catching Jupiter's banded light on their upper faces and black on their lower. Jupiter sat on the horizon, cut off by it, an enormous half-disc in amber and cream and rust, and it did not move. Above the ice hung a faint mist of light, the sputter glow, where the planet's magnetosphere tore atoms off the surface. Piq had thought on the way down that it was the colour of a wound. It was the colour of the thing that was killing everyone she could see.

Her suit dosimeter began to chatter. The display climbed the way a tank level rises: three millisieverts a minute, then a little more.

"Go," said Teo.

They went up the leg ladder in long low hops. Each block on the crust bore its Brigade grease-pencil number. Twenty-two was on Teo's side, twenty-four on Piq's. Piq clipped to the hull rail and put the saw to the first strap. It parted and sprang away in slow motion like a ribbon. The second parted. She set the pry bar under the block's lower edge and leaned.

The block didn't move.

She bent close. Along the lower edge a seam of clear ice ran half a metre long, glassy, bonding block to hull. This was the one the Brigade had called Juno's drip block. It had dripped all through loading at Asgard, and Piq had logged it twice because it kept losing mass. Somewhere in the descent heat or the hour of reactor soak on the surface, its wet face had sweated and frozen into the skin.

"Twenty-four's frozen down," she said. "Seam along the base. I'll have to chip it."

"How long?"

"Ten minutes. More."

"Copy. I'm nearly free on twenty-two." A grunt, and then the scrape of something heavy moving. "It's going."

Teo's block came away in a single slow piece. It tipped past vertical, past forty-five degrees, a slab of Callisto ice the size of a coffin turning over in Europa's gentle gravity. For a long moment it hung with its number on its face, and then it came down onto the plain without a sound. A fan of white crystal sprayed out from beneath it. Some of the crystals didn't fall. They rose into the glow, subliming, and were gone.

"Twenty-two's off," Teo said. "Lucky?"

"Eight minutes," said Lucky. "Nine. You're at twenty-seven millisieverts out there, Teo."

"I know what I'm at. Piq, I'll come round and take the bar on twenty-four. Two of us is faster."

The clear ice came away under the saw in thin glittering curls, and the seam underneath was exactly as long as before. There was no fast way through it. There was only through it.

"No," she said.

"Two people. That's the procedure."

"Two people for eight minutes is sixteen person-minutes. One person for sixteen is sixteen person-minutes. Same dose total." She shifted the saw two centimetres and cut again. "The difference is who it lands on. You're the medic. There's a man inside coughing blood and a tanker crew coming into the field and a moon of dose ahead of us. Your hands are in that ledger. Mine aren't. It's my block, and I don't need anyone on it to cut a seam. Go in, Teo."

"Piq."

"I'm not lying to you. I'm telling you. Go in."

The radio hissed, and Piq counted three seconds by habit. Three seconds had been the wedding delay, Callisto to the tanker, vows colliding in the gap. Margin was so close now that Ziva's voice came back faster than a breath. Nothing out here took three seconds to cross. The silence was Teo's.

"Ten," said Lucky. Then, quieter: "Teo. Rhee's coughing again. Oduya says it's worse."

Piq heard Teo breathe in once, deeply. Then came the scrape of a boot on the ladder.

"Fourteen minutes," Teo said. "Not one more. I'll be at the lock with the clock in my hand."

"Copy."

She worked.

She would remember it later only as numbers, because she had no other way to hold it. Thirteen minutes, thirty-nine millisieverts. Seven hundred and two kilos gone, seven hundred and thirty-one still on the hull. Fourteen, and Lucky said "fourteen" and nothing after it. Fifteen. The seam two-thirds through.

At seventeen minutes it gave. A crack ran along it with a vibration she felt through her gloves, and the block shifted a centimetre and stopped. She put her whole weight on the bar, and in Europa's gravity her whole weight was nine kilos of push. Nine kilos against seven hundred and thirty-one did nothing at all. Then she set her boots against the hull rail and pushed with her legs, and the block began, very slowly, to tip.

It went over the edge of the crust and down past the landing leg, closer than she liked. It struck the plain beside twenty-two, threw its own fan of crystal into the glow, and lay there number up.

"Twenty-four's off. Coming in."

"Nineteen minutes," said Lucky. "Teo says come down the rail, not the ladder."

She came down the rail.

---

Teo was in the lock with her helmet off and the clock in her hand, as promised. She cycled the pressure up before Piq's boots had settled, unsealed Piq's helmet, and found the suit badge on its clip at the collar. Her hands were not quite steady.

"Fifty-eight," Teo said. "Out there. Fifty-eight."

"Rhee?"

"Coughing. Holding. He's on fluids." Teo didn't take her eyes off the badge. "Nineteen minutes for you. Ten for me. I'm at thirty-one. You're at fifty-eight. I'm logging both. I'm logging that I came in at ten with a block still on the hull, and that you stayed five past the limit I set. I'm logging why."

"Log it."

Teo pulled Piq's glove off and pressed two fingers flat against the inside of her wrist, and counted. Her lips moved slightly. Piq felt the light pressure and the warmth, and she knew the number was high, and she knew it wasn't high from the dose.

It was the first time Teo had touched her since the badge in the dose office, weeks ago.

"Ninety-four," Teo said. "Fast."

"I've been working."

"You've been working." Teo kept her fingers there one breath longer than the count needed. Then she folded the glove flat and square and put it in the suit locker. "Don't do that again," she said, in the voice she used for tables. "You don't get to pick whose number goes up. That's mine."

"That's procedure."

"It's mine." For a moment something showed in her face that wasn't anger and wasn't tables, as naked as Piq had ever seen it. "I'll know you were in that column," Teo said. "I'll know you were there."

---

They pushed the jettison pile off the lower step in one shove, and it spread over a few metres of R-K4 under the glow. The comms unit landed face up beside Wierzbicki's sack, stencil visible: AUDIT REFERENCE MASSES. CERTIFIED. DO NOT OPEN.

Piq sealed the hatch and wrote in the time: 1243.

She rebuilt the ledger from the top, every line again, aloud. Blocks and pile came to one thousand six hundred and forty-seven kilograms off the ascent mass, against eleven hundred and forty needed. On paper they were thirty-one metres per second over.

She fed the new mass into the nav stack and watched the profile come back, and for a moment she didn't understand it.

"Lucky. Run the burn again."

He ran it. "That's short."

Twenty metres per second short.

The masses were right. She could feel it the way she had felt the block start to tip. She ran the legs backward from the tanker line, and found the change in her own handwriting. On Callisto the ascent leg had been three point five nine kilometres per second. The computer now gave three point six three, forty metres more. The match geometry had also slewed, and that added another eleven for the plane.

"The window's later," she said slowly. "We're hours behind the Callisto plan. Forty-one minutes in the hover. The hour on one seam instead of two people on two. Margin's line moved while we sat here. The ascent grew forty, the plane grew eleven. Fifty-one against a cushion of thirty-one." She looked up. "The phasing ate the margin."

"Another block?" said Teo.

"Twenty-five. The partial. It shades the litter."

Teo looked at Rhee, in the dark grey with the cloth in his hand. Piq watched her do the arithmetic and come to the end of it.

"No. The tank kills everyone a little. Twenty-five kills him a lot. We go with what we have."

"Can Margin make it up?" Amaya asked from the aft rail.

Piq didn't know. Only one person did. She went forward and keyed the link.

"Margin, this is Mule."

Ziva answered before Piq had finished letting go of the key. "Mule, Margin. We see you on the ground. You are late."

"We're late. We shed sixteen hundred kilos, and the phasing moved on us. We can make the ascent, but we're twenty metres per second short on our half of the match. We'll be below your line and we can't close."

"Then we come down to meet you."

"How far?"

This time the silence was long, five seconds, six, and none of it was light-lag. Piq heard the tanker's flight deck behind it, low voices, an alarm someone silenced. She thought of a crew she had never met, with waivered allowances of a hundred and fifty millisieverts each, holding above the cuff where the field was still thin.

"Below the cuff," Ziva said. "To the floor of it. You know what our numbers become."

"I know."

"Then we come down. Tell Dov to fly well. Tell him he does not get to make me wait in there."

Behind Piq, Lucky closed his eyes for exactly one second.

"Copy. Ascent 1511. We'll meet you on the floor."

She closed the link. The cabin read 3.9 millisieverts an hour, and the couch read 4.1 where the crust was gone. It was 1258. She wrote it: *M. to dive below cuff. Our short: 20.* She drew a square beside it, then looked at the square and drew an equals sign instead. There was nothing left to take off the ship.

---

At 1511 the Mule lifted.

It went up on a column of white that blew the jettison pile flat across the plain. Piq watched through the couch port as the comms unit tumbled once and lay still, and then the ice dropped away and it was too small to see. Thrust pressed her into the bulkhead padding at a little over one g. After weeks at a tenth of that, it felt like a hand on her chest.

Nobody talked. Teo called numbers.

"Cabin six. Couch nine." A minute. "Cabin eight. We're into the cuff."

The cuff was where the field thickened around Europa's orbit, and they were climbing straight into it. The dosimeter on the bulkhead stopped chattering and began to hum. Rhee's breathing went shallow under the thrust, and Oduya held the cloth and Teo held his wrist.

"Main engine cutoff in ten," Lucky said. "Five. Cut."

Weightlessness came. A pencil drifted off the shelf, and Piq caught it without looking.

"Short as plotted," Lucky said. "Nineteen point six under the line. Margin's diving."

On the flight deck plot Margin was a green chevron descending toward them, its trail bending into the field. Below it the Mule's yellow arc ran lower and faster, overtaking.

"Cabin eleven," Teo said. "Couch fifteen."

Ziva's voice: "Mule, Margin. Burning down. Our cabin reads twenty-two. We will be at your altitude in nine minutes. Do not be clever. Hold attitude and let us do the work."

"Holding," said Lucky.

Piq ran the closure. The tanker carried four hundred tonnes and could not turn on a coin. The Mule had perhaps eight metres per second of trim left in her tanks, and every one of them was already promised to the docking. If Ziva came in fast, there would be no second pass. If she came in slow, the field would take its time with all of them.

"Cabin fourteen," Teo said. "Rhee's at four on his badge since liftoff. Wierzbicki, put your hand in your armpit and stop looking at the port."

Margin grew from a chevron into a shape, and then into a ship: a long grey drum of tankage with a crew section at one end, pitted and frosted, its docking cone lit amber. It came down across Jupiter's face, and the bands slid behind it.

"Range two kilometres," Lucky said. "Closing at nine."

"Too fast," said Piq.

"She knows."

Margin's thrusters fired in a ring of small white flowers. The closure dropped to six, then four, then two.

"Cabin nineteen," Teo said. "Couch twenty-eight. I need this done."

"Range three hundred. One point one closing."

Lucky had his hands on the trim controls and his lips moving without sound. Piq realised he was counting the taps, once and then twice, over and over.

"Mule, Margin," said Ziva. "Our capture cone is offset two degrees to your plus-Y. Correct."

Lucky corrected. The docking target swam into the camera reticle and centred, drifted, and centred again.

"Fifty metres."

"Twenty."

"Ten. Zero point three."

The cone came in over the port and filled it. There was a sound through the hull like a door closed hard in another room, then the clatter of capture latches, then a long grinding as the hard latches pulled the two ships together.

"Hard dock," said Ziva. "Hard dock. Equalising."

"Cabin twenty-one," Teo said, and her voice broke on the last syllable. She put it back together and said it again. "Twenty-one. Get Rhee through first."

Margin fired its main engine before the hatch was open. Piq felt the deck come up under her feet as the tanker began climbing out of the floor of the field with the Mule hanging from its nose. The tunnel pressure went green.

---

They passed Rhee through first, then Oduya and Wierzbicki, then Amaya with the core held against her chest in both arms. Teo went with the litter. Piq was taking the evidence pouch off the shelf when she heard Lucky stop in the tunnel ahead of her and stay stopped.

She came up behind him.

Ziva Kalnins was at the far end of the tunnel, holding a grab bar under the tanker's light thrust. She was taller than she had looked on any screen. She had cropped pale hair and a flight suit with a tear at the elbow mended in red thread. Her face was entirely without expression.

Lucky opened his mouth. Then he closed it and waited, visibly, for three seconds.

Ziva waited too.

They both began at once. "I..."

They both stopped. They both waited for three seconds.

"There is no delay, Dov," said Ziva.

"I know there's no delay."

"You are waiting for one."

"You're waiting for one too."

"I am waiting for you to finish," Ziva said. "Last time you did not finish for eleven minutes. Your officiant had to stop you."

"She was very professional about it," Lucky said.

Piq, behind him with the pouch, said nothing professional at all.

Lucky pushed off the bar. In a tenth of a g he overshot badly. Ziva caught him by the collar and the belt, the way a deckhand catches a loose line, and held him at arm's length.

"You are shorter," she said.

"You're standing on a step."

"I am not on a step."

Lucky reached into his pocket and took out the brass fish and held it up between them. It caught the amber light. "Fourteen grams," he said. "I got it through an audit. Do you know how hard that is?"

Ziva looked at it, and then at him, and her face did something small and complete that Piq would not have been able to describe and was glad she didn't have to. She took the fish. Then she pulled him in by the collar and kissed him. In the low thrust they rotated very slowly against the tunnel wall, in no hurry to stop.

Piq waited for an opening to get past them. It took some time. When she finally squeezed by, Ziva said into Lucky's ear, without letting go of him, "Your officiant may proceed."

"Thank you," said Piq.

---

Margin's medical bay had two couches, a cabinet, and a bulkhead dosimeter that someone had decorated with a sticker of a cartoon sun wearing sunglasses. Margin's medic, a broad silent man named Teodorescu, had Rhee on one couch with oxygen and a line and Wierzbicki's fingers splinted within twenty minutes. Amaya went to a crew bunk under orders. Oduya refused to leave Rhee and was allowed to sit on the floor.

Teo did everyone before herself. She went down the line twice. Then she sat on the edge of the second couch and was sick into a bag with great neatness, sealed it, labelled it, and passed it to Teodorescu.

Piq had felt it coming for half an hour, a slow greasy turning low in her stomach and a headache settling behind her eyes like grit in a seal. She took the next bag. Teodorescu handed it over without comment, as if he gave out bags all day, which on a Jovian tanker he probably did.

"Prodromal," Teo said when Piq had finished. Her face was the colour of the depot frost. "Onset under three hours. That's consistent with..." She found Piq's badge on her collar, then her own, and read them. "Point seven one sieverts for the trip. You. Point six four, me. Bloods tomorrow and every day after. Lymphocytes will tell us what the number doesn't."

"It's consistent," Piq said.

"It's consistent." Teo lay back on the couch because she had no choice, and Piq sat on the deck beside it with her back against the cabinet, because there was nowhere else to sit. The tanker hummed. On the bulkhead the sunglasses-sun stood over a readout that said 6.3 millisieverts per hour and rising, because Margin was still climbing out through the cuff and would be for another two hours.

"I'm logging mine as an exceedance," Teo said to the ceiling. "Under my own waiver. My licence goes to review. I wrote that rule too."

"I know."

"You don't get to make that sound reasonable."

"I'm not trying to. It's just in the column."

Teo laughed, which turned into a cough, which turned into nothing. Her hand came over the edge of the couch and found Piq's wrist, two fingers on the inside, where she had pressed them in the lock. She didn't count this time. She just left them there.

"You took five minutes that were mine," Teo said.

"Fifteen millisieverts. Thereabouts."

"Fifteen." Teo's eyes were closed. "I've hated insurers my whole adult life. I thought you were one with better manners. Then I thought you were one with worse. Then you put your phone home on a pile of ration bricks and didn't look at me, and I thought, she's going to make me do the arithmetic on this too."

Piq looked at the hand on her wrist. All her life she had made feelings into lines so she could find them again: cost and benefit, exposure and likelihood. She reached for that now and nothing came. There was no column for it.

"I love you," Piq said. "I haven't weighed it. I'm not going to."

The fingers on her wrist tightened.

"Good," said Teo. "Because I'd have to check your work." She turned her head on the couch and opened her eyes, and they were wet and steady. "I love you. I've been trying not to since you lied to me about a dosimeter waiver at intake."

"I didn't lie. I asked for one."

"You asked for one with a face." Teo swallowed against the nausea. "Stay where I can read you."

"I'm on the floor. I'm not going anywhere."

The intercom clicked. "All crew, Margin," said Ziva. "Clear of the cuff at 1802. Course laid for Ur Station, thirty-nine hours. We have informed Ur that we carry seven aboard Mule. Ur has informed the Authority." A pause, flat as ice. "The Authority has not answered yet."

On the bulkhead the readout ticked from 6.3 to 6.4. Teo's fingers stayed on Piq's wrist, and Piq felt her own pulse under them, fast, still climbing.
