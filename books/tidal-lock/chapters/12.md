# Chapter 12: Chain of Custody

At 0730 the badge on Piq's collar read 0.191 Sv lifetime. She knew this because Teo read it aloud.

"Zero point one nine one," Teo said, and wrote it in the column. "Ring C background twelve point four microsieverts a day. Dome excursion this morning, forty-one minutes, negligible. You were in the dome until six-ten."

"Six-twelve."

Teo didn't look up. "Six-ten. The door logs it." She wrote *6:10* and initialled it. Grey cable-insulation tie, reading glasses low, the cracked brass dosimeter turned face-in against her sternum. There was no flask on the desk. Piq had noticed that before she sat down and had spent the first four minutes trying not to look for it.

The office was the one behind the blue door at the end of Ring A. It was as cold as it always was. The card was still on the shelf, NEITHER ARE DOSES, in Teo's square capitals, and the requisition pad sat beside it with the top sheet torn off. Piq had the matching sheet in a plastic sleeve in her quarters. It had Teo's reactor geometry on the back, and its contents had gone into report two.

"Any symptoms," Teo said.

"No."

"Nausea, fatigue outside sleep debt, petechiae."

"No."

"Sleep debt."

Piq thought about it. "About nine hours across three days."

Teo wrote *9h* in the margin with no inflection at all. "You'll need a weekly blood draw if you go above zero point two. You're not above it. I'm noting it because you will be." She capped the pen. "That's all."

That was the whole consultation, and it was correct in every line. Piq stood up. Low gravity drew it out the way it drew out everything, the rise taking longer than any decision should. She stood with one hand on the chair back while the room settled around her.

"The Commissioner," she said.

"I sent it at ten past seven." Teo turned to her terminal and typed with two fingers. "Chaos terrain dose tables for trailing hemisphere, mean and shadowed. Management of persons of unknown prior exposure, intake protocol rev. nine. And a standing offer under Framework 2201 to exchange real-time dosimetry with Ur Medical on any surface operation. I copied the Authority liaison as the Framework requires. The Authority liaison is Prew. Prew reports to two offices. You know all this."

"I put Haddad's request in last night's batch to L4," Piq said. "The twenty-three hundred upload. I thought you should hear that from me."

"Before I'd answered it."

"Yes."

"You delivered it first." Teo looked at her over the glasses for exactly as long as it took to say it. "I noticed."

Piq had nothing to put in that column. Haddad's letter had come to the dose warden, in the dose warden's name, about the dose warden's work. Piq had read it over Teo's shoulder, gone to her quarters and filed it to her employer six hours before Teo replied. She had told herself it was a matter of transit time and the relay's batching. That was true, and it was also the kind of true she had built a career on.

She went to the door. The seal there was cheap and gritty and hissed when it broke. Behind her the pen scratched once more, and she didn't know what Teo had written.

It would have been easier if Teo had shouted. An accusation could be answered, contested, partially conceded and closed out with a note. Teo had made no accusation. She had recorded the dose to the minute and corrected the timestamp. She had listed the symptoms and capped the pen, and she had treated Piq exactly as she treated every other badge on the station, which was scrupulously and from a great distance. There was no line in the ledger where Piq could post a reply. The account was simply closed to her, and every figure in it was correct.

---

The day ran on the station's arithmetic regardless. At 0900 Ring B's potable reserve came up six litres short. The cause was a cracked galley fitting and a cook with a soup pot, and Piq logged it as informal return-to-stock rather than fine anyone.

At 1015 Fitch came down the Ring B corridor at a low-g jog with the spring-trap under one arm. It was a mesh box with a load cell bolted underneath and a tablet taped to the lid.

"Four point zero nine eight kilograms," he said, and held the tablet up to her face. The trace showed a flat line, a soft-edged spike, then a slow decaying settle. "Junction fourteen, oh-three-twelve. It ate the bait and left through the bars. I want it certified."

"I can certify that your cell registered a four point zero nine eight kilogram transient," Piq said. "I can't certify what caused it."

"It was the cat."

"Was the cat on the manifest?"

His mouth opened and shut. She sealed it as a calibration event, and he went back up the corridor looking into every louvre he passed.

When she turned toward the ramp there were two people in grey Authority coveralls between her and it.

---

The taller one was Officer Danso. Piq had his name from a dock manifest and had never heard him speak. The other one she didn't know. Danso held a hand terminal out flat with a document on the screen, the way a man offers a dog something to sniff.

"Piq Varga. Under Authority Ordinance Six you're detained for interview. Maximum six hours, extendable on the Security Chief's signature. You'll surrender personal electronics for the duration."

Piq read the document. She read the whole of it, the citation and the hold period and the blank where a reason ought to be, because the act of reading was the only thing in the corridor she controlled. The ordinance number was correct. The signature block said *M. Saar* in a hand that sloped forward.

"My audit terminal is under notarial seal," she said. "It goes with me, sealed. You can hold it. You can't open it."

Danso looked at the other officer. The other officer looked at nothing.

"Bring it sealed," Danso said.

They walked her down the ramp to Ring D and through the pressure door that separated Authority offices from everything else. She noted the turns. She noted that Danso walked on her left and kept half a step back, and that the other one carried her terminal in both hands like something that might spill. Nobody touched her. The corridor lights in Ring D were a degree whiter than the residential rings and colder on the skin.

The room was three metres by three. It had a table bolted to the floor, two chairs bolted to the floor, and a clock set into the wall above the door, so the detainee had to lift her eyes to read it and the interviewer never did. 1031. The air was dry, scrubbed of the station's usual ozone, and her own breath was the loudest thing in it. Danso set her terminal on the table between the chairs with its seal tape facing up, red and unbroken, and left.

She sat. In 0.126 g she sank into the chair slowly, and once she was down she kept her hands flat on the table where they could be seen.

At 1047 Saar came in carrying nothing.

He took the other chair the way a man takes a chair he owns. Grey coveralls like the others, but pressed, with the creases standing. He looked at the seal on her terminal for a long moment and then at her.

"Ms Varga."

"Chief Saar."

"You've had a busy month." He folded his hands. "I'd like to go through it with you. Not the audit. The audit I've read. Your reconciliations are filed to the Authority registry every evening, and they're very good. I've learned more about where this station keeps its water from your filings than from eleven years of my own inspections."

She said nothing. A compliment opened an account, and she would not be the one to post the first entry.

"The station runs a spectrum monitor," he said. "It isn't secret. It's in the safety annex, page forty. It exists so that nobody keys a transmitter near the Sling's capacitor banks and puts a slug into the regolith three kilometres short. It logs every emission inside the pressure boundary. Band, duration, origin to within about four metres." He recited it the way she recited clauses. "Since your arrival it has logged thirty-four emissions from your quarters. Six from the observation dome. Two from a maintenance alcove in Ring C, behind the scrubber stack, where there is no reason for anyone to transmit anything. Burst traffic. Compressed and wrapped in a commercial carrier the monitor can't open. Last night at twenty-three hundred you sent eleven point six megabytes."

"Eleven point six two," Piq said.

"Thank you."

"It wasn't a correction. It's the size of the file. If you tell me the size of every file, I'll tell you what was in each one." She kept her hands where they were. "Ibis Metrology requires a daily evidentiary upload from any certified auditor working in the field. Mass reconciliations. Seal logs. Photographs of every weighing, with the load cell's serial in frame. Ibis certifies my figures to the underwriters, and the underwriters set Asgard's rates. When the upload is late, Ibis assumes the auditor has been bribed. I've never been late."

"From the scrubber alcove."

"The signal in my quarters is poor. The alcove has a line to the main antenna trunk. You can test that yourself."

"I have," Saar said. "It does." He let that sit. "And the reception. The monitor logs reception too, where it can. It has you listening for long periods on a band nobody on this station is licensed to use."

The clock read 1052. She did not look at it.

"Ask Ibis," she said.

"I intend to."

"Then you should know what you're asking for. Earth-Luna is forty-nine light-minutes today. Your query reaches Ibis's L4 office in forty-nine minutes if it goes direct, and it doesn't go direct. It goes through the Mars relay. The relay is batching commercial queries at thirty-one hours now and rising, because conjunction is close and everything without a priority flag waits behind everything with one. Someone at Ibis has to read it, find my supervising auditor and pull my upload certificates. Then the answer comes back through the same queue. You'd be lucky to have it in four days." She paused exactly long enough to be fair. "The relay goes dark in eleven."

"I can flag it priority."

"You can. Authority priority traffic is read by the Authority's L4 desk before anything is forwarded. They'll want to know why the Security Chief on Asgard needs an Ibis auditor's upload certificates confirmed ahead of the conjunction freight manifests. You'd have to put that in writing to the people who review your budget." She had worked this out at four in the morning a week ago, sitting in the dark of her quarters. It sounded worse said aloud in a dry room. "Your answer comes back after the window closes either way. Until then, what you have is an auditor sending audit files."

Saar unfolded his hands and laid them flat on the table, a mirror of hers. It might have been deliberate. It looked like a man getting comfortable.

"Then I'll open the terminal," he said. "Here. Now. And we'll both know."

"It's sealed under the Compact Notarial Act, section nine. I'm a sworn notary, registered with the Compact registry at Luna. The seal on that terminal is the chain of custody for every mass figure I've certified since I stepped off the shuttle." She turned her right hand over and touched the tape with one finger, the only movement she had made since sitting down. "If you break it, the chain breaks. Every certification behind it becomes uncertified evidence. That includes the depot reconciliation the Director signed, the Ring B water figures and the tank farm transfer."
