# Chapter 13: Backlog

Ring D let her go at 1131 by its mechanical clock. Piq had come to resent that clock. She took the long corridor back toward the spoke and did not let herself hurry. On her right hip the cover terminal sat sealed under the security tape Saar had decided not to break. Under her sternum the dose badge kept recording whatever Ring D's private scrubber loop had been doing to the background. She would read it later, the way she would read everything later.

Before the interrogation began he had made her sit for forty minutes with nothing but the sweep hand for company. His hands were long and uncalloused, the nails cut square. They were hands that had never hauled a crate or cracked a frozen valve. His coveralls had been pressed to a shine, which meant an iron, and an iron meant a power draw that appeared on no laundry allocation she had ever audited. Halfway through, the tape had nearly come off the terminal. She had stopped that by pointing out that her seal sat on the Authority's last regret notice to Ur Station. That was the one she had attested two weeks ago at 0452, because the escalating exchange of courtesies between the JDA and Ur Station had grown formal enough that both sides wanted a neutral notary. She was the only one within six hundred thousand kilometres. Saar had wanted the record uncontestable. Then he had discovered what uncontestable cost him.

At the end, with the clock reading 1129, he had said it plainly, as though reading her a fee schedule. He had signed the ruling that called the Ninefold Grace pulses an automated echo. He could not let four people exist who would make that signature a lie. Then he had told her she could go.

She had gone. Now, in the spoke lift, rising through the slow pull of a tenth of a gravity, she did the accounting he had not done. He could not verify her uploads before the window closed. Every packet she had sent through the station uplink looked like routine audit traffic, padded out to standard block length with filler so that no one counting bytes could tell a mass reconciliation from a report to Special Losses. Fifty minutes out to Earth-Luna and fifty back. He would need two full rounds to confirm anything with her nominal employers at Ibis Metrology. He did not have two rounds. The Mars relay would go dark in a little over thirty hours.

That was the alibi, and it had held. Somewhere between Ring D and the spoke lift she understood it was also a problem. She had not thought it through, because it had always worked in her favour.

Her reports were hard to verify because they were hard to find. She had made them hard to find on purpose.

The lift doors opened onto Ring B and its ozone. The electrolysis stacks were running high for the end of the shift, and the air tasted faintly of struck flint. A maintenance tech was chasing a dropped torque driver along the deck. It turned end over end at the speed of a thought you were trying not to have. Piq stepped around them both and went to find Ruairi Danso.

---

The comms shack was a converted pump room on the outer curve of Ring B. Its walls still carried pipe brackets that no one had found worth the mass to remove. Danso had hung his jacket on one and his headset on another. He was eating something from a pouch that had been labelled lentil before the label wore away.

He had explained the Mars relay to her on her second day, cheerfully and at length, the way people on Asgard explained things they suspected no one else cared about. She had cared. She had written it down. Then she had treated it as background, the way you treat the floor.

"You look like you've been in Ring D," he said.

"Is that a look?"

"Flat. Everyone comes out of Ring D looking ironed." He swallowed. "What do you need? I've got about thirty hours of relay left, and half the station wants to send its mother a birthday message before the dark."

"I want to talk to the relay operator. Not the queue. The person."

Danso put the pouch down. "Nobody talks to the operator."

"There is an operator."

"There's a person at Deimos who signs the logs, yes. But you don't talk to her. You send into the queue, the queue sorts you, and the relay forwards what it forwards. Talking to the operator means a priority-class header, and nothing on this station has one except the Director's emergency channel and the Authority's diplomatic traffic." He stopped, then looked at her the way people on Asgard looked at a mass anomaly in a duct. "Which you've notarized."

"Which I've notarized," she said. "The relay has my seal on file, because every regret notice the Authority sent Ur in the last fortnight went out carrying it. Under the Correspondence Act a notarial instrument that carries a statutory deadline gets Class A handling. Conjunction is a statutory deadline for any claim under an inner-system policy. I'm a notary. I can make an instrument."

"About what?"

"About my own outbound traffic. A notarized protest of non-delivery." She set her cover terminal on his bench and peeled back the corner of Saar's tape just far enough to free the data port. It left a faint sticky residue on her thumb. She did not intend to explain the tape. "I am attesting that the following sixteen packets were lodged with the station uplink on the following dates. I am requesting the relay certify their status. The certification has to come from the operator. Instruments need a human signature on both ends."

Danso stared at her for one long second. Then he laughed, short and unwilling. "That's the most auditor thing I've ever heard."

"Thank you."

"It wasn't a compliment. But it'll work." He pulled his headset off its bracket. "Callisto to Mars is about thirty-four minutes one way right now. Call it seventy-plus for a round trip if she answers straight away, and she won't. She'll be drowning. Everyone in the system is shoving traffic at her before the dark."

"Then I'll wait."

"You'll wait in here?"

"I'll wait in here," Piq said. She sat on an upturned spool of fibre that someone had logged as furniture, and began composing the instrument.

It took her nine minutes. She kept it short, because the relay judged messages by length as well as class, and that thought had begun to sit in her stomach like a swallowed bolt. She listed her sixteen outbound packets by date and size. She attested under seal that each had been lodged. She requested certification of delivery or non-delivery. At the bottom she added a sentence that was not strictly instrument language: *If any of these remain in your queue, I request their release under this seal before conjunction hold.*

Danso read it over her shoulder. He could not help it, and she did not stop him.

"Sixteen," he said. "All padded."

"All padded to standard block."

"Every one of them a fat padded packet." He rubbed his eyes. "I told you, the first week. The relay deprioritizes big padded packets as conjunction comes on. They look like bulk. Somebody's backup. Somebody's ten thousand photographs of a load cell."

"You told me," she agreed.

She pressed the seal into the terminal's notarial field. It was a physical die in a physical housing, which the Act required. Old law, written for people who did not trust anything they could not hold. The die gave its small mechanical click, the instrument closed, and Danso sent it.

---

For the first forty minutes she worked, because she did not know what else to do with her hands.

She opened her own outbound log, the true one. It lived inside the hollow comms unit she had declared at the shuttle port as audit reference masses, and she laid it out in two columns. In the left she put what she had sent and when. In the right she put what Pell had said back and when it had arrived. She had never done this. She had kept the two streams in separate folders as though they were separate conversations. Each of his messages answered something. She had simply assumed that it answered her latest.

The left column went like this.

Day 3. A short report, padded. Pulses on a retired dosimetry band, gaps of about thirty hours every four and a half days, consistent with a shift roster. *Assess as coordinated activity, possibly throw cabal.*

Day 6. Short, padded. A question about how to log a contraband cat. She had written it at two in the morning, when it had seemed both reasonable and funny.

Day 8. Long, padded. The Mule. The hangar. The Brigade.

Day 14. Very long, padded until it was as fat as a slug of ice. *The transmitter is on Europa. Four alive. Reactor drum sixty days. This is not a cabal. Recommend immediate revision of all prior assessments.*

After that came a dozen more, each longer than the last, each full of delta-v and dose figures and the flight recorder file. Each carried the quiet, rising panic of a woman who could not understand why her handler kept writing as though she had said nothing.

The right column went like this.

Day 12. *Identify throw cabal. Treat depot crew as hostile.* She had read that order on Day 12, two days before she knew about Europa, and it had seemed reasonable.

Day 16. *Ur has bought war-risk cover over the claimed Europa zone. Stop any unlicensed launch by any means.* On Day 16 she already knew. Her Day 14 report had said, in so many words, that the launch was a rescue. She had read *any means* and felt the floor tilt.

Day 21. *Report status of throw cabal.* As though Europa had never been mentioned.

Day 22. At the wedding, with Lucky and Ziva's vows colliding across three seconds of delay, Pell's earnest answer to her two-in-the-morning cat question had arrived. *Re: feline. Recommend Schedule F, livestock, non-consumable.* She had laughed. She remembered laughing. She remembered thinking that the cat question must have jumped the queue. She had not asked herself why the cat question could jump the queue when Europa could not.

The cat question had been small. That was why.

Her messages had been sitting in the outbound queue this whole time, and every one carried a small grey tag, ACK PENDING. She had looked at those tags for a week and filed them as the normal sullenness of a relay approaching conjunction. ACK pending meant the relay had not yet confirmed delivery downstream. It had been telling her, packet by packet, exactly what was wrong, and she had decided the instrument was miscalibrated because the reading was inconvenient.

She sat on the fibre spool and looked at the two columns. A low-g cough of air from the vent stirred the hair at her temple. The arithmetic had already closed. She just had not let herself read the total.

"You've gone grey," Danso said.

"I'm reconciling."

"Does it usually go grey?"

"Only when it balances," Piq said.

Something heavy and furred landed on the pipe bracket above Danso's jacket, sending a slow puff of regolith dust drifting down through the shaft of the lamp. Ballast looked at them both with the patience of an animal that had never once been declared. Then the cat reached out one paw, considered Danso's lentil pouch, and withdrew the paw again, unimpressed.

"Four point one," Piq said, mostly to herself.

"What?"

"Nothing. Schedule F." She looked back at her columns. "Livestock. Non-consumable."

---

The operator answered at seventy-eight minutes. That was faster than Danso had predicted and slower than Piq could stand.

The message came in Class A, so it arrived first in its frame and alone, a brief stack of plain text under a Deimos relay header. It was signed CELESTINE AHN-MBATHA, OPERATOR, DEIMOS STORE-AND-FORWARD, and it read like a woman who had been awake for two days and had not expected anyone to address her by job title.

> Notary Varga. Instrument received and certified under your seal. Answering in human because you asked in human, which nobody does.
>
> Your sixteen packets. Four delivered: your Day 3 and Day 6 shorts, plus two small housekeeping uploads. Twelve remain in queue as of this writing. They are your large padded uploads, oldest first, and they never cleared bulk-class sorting.
>
> I want you to understand why, because you're going to be angry with someone, and it shouldn't be me and it shouldn't be the machine.
>
> As we approach conjunction the relay's bandwidth to inner system drops. Priority traffic goes first: navigation, medical, diplomatic, legal, anything with a class header. Then small packets, because small packets are cheap and usually human. Then bulk. Bulk is identified by size and by entropy profile. Padded packets look like bulk. That is what padding is for. You padded your uploads to standard block so that nobody counting bytes could tell them apart from backup traffic. Nobody could. Including us.
>
> You disguised your reports as unimportant, and the relay agreed with you. Every time a priority frame came through, your packets dropped a place in line. Two of your largest have been in this queue for eleven days.
>
> Under your seal I am releasing all twelve now, at Class A, to their downstream addressee. I am also holding the inbound frame for your addressee's responses so that it goes to you whole before hold, because inbound to Callisto from that addressee is backed up for the same reason. Expect it in one burst. It will not arrive in order. Nothing arrives in order this close to the sun.
>
> Hold begins at 1704 Deimos time tomorrow. That's 1612 yours, approx. After that, nineteen days of nothing.
>
> I hope whatever it is was worth padding.
>
> C. A-M.

Piq read it twice. She did not reread things. She read it twice.

*Including us.*

There was a word for a control that works exactly as designed and still produces a loss: an operational loss. She had written that phrase into claim files twenty times. She had never once written it about herself.

Danso was quiet for a long moment. "Your boss," he said at last, "has been getting the short ones."

"My boss has been getting the Day 3 report and a question about a cat."

"Your Day 3 said cabal."

"My Day 3 said cabal." She heard her own voice, level and dry, the voice she used for tug crews disputing a manifest. "Every order he's sent me in two weeks was written to a woman who thinks there's a weapon on Callisto. He's been answering the last thing I told him. The last thing I told him was wrong."

"So he's not—"

"I don't know what he is. I've been arguing with him as though he could see my ledger." She closed her mouth, then opened it again, because the next part had to be said or it would sit in her like the bolt. "He couldn't. He was looking at a page I'd torn out."

---

The inbound burst arrived at 1940. Twelve of her packets had gone out at 1702, so it had been enough time for them to cross to Deimos, cross again to L4, reach Pell, and for Pell to do whatever Pell did, which apparently took less time than she would have guessed. Then the operator had sealed the frame and sent it back across thirty-four minutes of dark.

Danso had gone off shift and come back with two bulbs of tea. He had a strong feeling, he said, that something was about to happen in his pump room that he would want to tell people about later, and he wanted to be able to say he had watched.

Nine messages, one frame. The relay had sorted them by size and class, smallest first, exactly as it always did. Piq did not read them in that order.

She stripped the headers and laid them out by L4 timestamp. She did not open a single body until she had them sequenced. It was the discipline her father had taught her on the cycler: weigh the crates before you open them, or what's inside will tell you how heavy it is.

Then she read them in order.

**1.** *Day 13, L4 time. Re: feline. Further to my earlier response, Schedule F requires vaccination certificate or veterinary attestation in lieu. If neither is available, notary may self-attest. Pell.*

She was the notary. Pell had told her, eleven days ago, that she could certify a cat on her own authority. She looked up. Ballast had relocated to the top of the comms rack, where the warm exhaust came out, and was washing one paw with enormous care.

"Absolutely not," she told the cat. Danso coughed tea onto the bench.

**2.** *Day 14, L4. Report status throw cabal. No update received since your Day 3. Pell.*

This was the same message she had received on Day 21, the one that had made her want to throw the terminal at the bulkhead. This was its first send. A Day 21 resend would have meant someone at L4 who knew nothing. A Day 14 send meant only someone at L4 who had not heard anything yet.

**3.** *Day 15, L4. Your silence is noted. Assume communications compromised. Do not attempt priority channels, which are monitored. Continue standard uploads. Pell.*

He had told her to keep padding. He had told her to make her reports look exactly like the thing the relay would bury. He had done it to protect her.

**4.** *Day 16, L4. Ur war-risk purchase confirmed over JDA-claimed Europa zone. Assess as preparation for armed incident. Stop any unlicensed launch by any means. Pell.*

This was the order that had made her want to burn him. She held it now next to her left column: a man at L4 with a week-old report of a cabal on Callisto, a confirmed war-risk policy over Europa, and an adjuster who had gone silent. *Any means* was a terrible sentence. It was not a cruel one. It was a man telling her to keep her head down in a world he could see only one week of.

**5.** *Day 17, L4. Disregard previous re feline vaccination. Schedule F amended. Cats now Schedule G, companion organism, effective last quarter. Apologies. Pell.*

Danso put his head down on the bench. His shoulders shook.

"It's not funny," Piq said.

"It's a little funny."

"It's a little funny," she conceded. Ballast, on the rack, did not consider it funny at all and turned to face the wall.

**6.** *Day 18, L4. Expense query. Your intake fine for 1.3 kg overage has posted to Special Losses as "reference masses, audit." Accounts asks what was referenced. Do not answer this. Pell.*

**7.** *Day 20, L4. No ACK from you on my 3 through 6. Relay shows your end pending. Concerned. If you are able to send anything, send it short. Pell.*

*Send it short.* He had worked it out. On Day 20 he had looked at his own queue, seen the same grey tags she had been ignoring, and told her the one thing that would have fixed it. Like everything else, the instruction had been small enough to go through only once she had already stopped listening for it.

She looked at that one for a long time. Her own ACK pending flags had been his flags. They had both been watching the same instrument from opposite ends of the solar system. Each had read it as the other's silence.

**8.** *Day 26, L4, received 1758 L4. Your Day 8 report arrived this hour. Mule, Brigade, hangar. Revise cabal assessment pending further. Holding all orders. Pell.*

Her Day 8 upload had reached him today. It was nineteen days old. He had read about the Mule as though it were news, because to him it was.

**9.** *Day 26, L4, sent 1839 L4. Twelve of your uploads have arrived in the last forty minutes under notarial release. I have read the Europa report and the flight recorder summary. I have read all of them.*

*The existence of four survivors of Ninefold Grace has now reached me and I have logged it with the Society.*

*Lagrange Article 31, the salvage clause, can fund recovery of insured crew as salvage of a covered asset. I have invoked it. You have funding authority. That is all I can give you from here.*

*All prior orders are rescinded. I wrote them to someone you were not anymore. I am sorry for that.*

*Help them. You'll be alone for 19 days.*

*Pell.*

She read it a second time, and then a third. That made two things today she had read more than once. It was getting to be a habit.

Then she went back to the top of her own column and looked at it again.

Day 3: *Assess as coordinated activity, possibly throw cabal.*

That was her sentence. For two weeks she had been fighting a man who disagreed with her about whether four people were alive on Europa. She had built him in her head as cold, as actuarial, as the kind of handler who would see four survivors as a liability on the Society's balance sheet. She had carried him that way through every delayed order. All that time he had never disagreed with her about Europa, because he had never heard of Europa. He had only ever argued with her Day 3 report. She had been arguing with him on its behalf. Her opponent had been a ghost of her own earlier thinking, written down, padded, and sent out into the queue to wait.

That was her flaw, and she knew its exact shape, because she had been told it in performance reviews in language softer than this. She trusted her own numbers so completely that she assumed everyone was reading from the same page. She had built a ledger and then lived as though it were the world. She had believed the world had a copy. The world did not have a copy. The world had nineteen-day-old fragments of a copy, sorted by size.

"So," Danso said gently. "Is he the bad guy?"

"No," she said. "He's the one who's been reading my mail late."

---

She found Teo in the dose office on Ring C, as she had known she would. The dose warden did not leave the dose office the night before a relay hold. Hold meant nineteen days with no outside medical consults, no Earth-side oncology reads on anyone's blood panel, no second opinions. Teo would sit up and recheck every resident's running total, because the rules were the only thing that had never lied to her.

The door was open. It was always open. A warden who closed her door was a warden people stopped bringing their badges to.

Teo did not look up. "If it's your badge, leave it on the tray. Ring D runs on a separate scrubber loop and its regolith cover's deeper than ours. You'll read low. I'll log it in the morning."

Since the dome, it had been like this between them. Teo had not reported Piq to anyone and had not burned her, exactly as she had said she would not. She had been scrupulously, coldly professional. She had cleared Piq for the hangar, logged her doses without comment, and looked at her the way one looks at a reading on a badge one does not trust.

Piq unclipped the badge and put it on the tray. Then she put the terminal beside it, open to message nine.

"I'm not asking you to forgive anything," she said. "I'm asking you to read this. It came in an hour ago."

Teo looked at the terminal for a long moment without touching it, as she might regard a sealed source. Then she pulled it across the desk and read.

Piq watched her read and did not try to translate it. She knew Teo's face well enough by now to read its micro-movements, though not well enough to know what they added up to. Teo's mouth went still on the third line. At *I have invoked it* she put one fingertip on the screen, the way she would touch a dose figure she was about to correct. At *I wrote them to someone you were not anymore* her eyes went up to Piq's and then back down.

She read the rest of the nine too, all of them, in the order Piq had sorted them. She read the cat messages without smiling. She read the expense query without smiling. Piq had expected nothing else. At message seven, *send it short*, she stopped for a while.

"Your reports were buried," Teo said finally. "Because you padded them."

"Because I padded them to look like nothing."

"So for two weeks he's been telling you to shut down a cabal."

"There was never a cabal. I told him there was. On Day 3. Before I knew." Piq kept her hands still at her sides. "Every order you heard me quote came from that."

Teo closed the terminal. She did not slide it back.

"I want to be careful," she said, "about what I say next. Because I'm angry, and I've been angry for a long time, and I don't trust it."

"All right."

"You lied to me. For weeks. About what you were and who you were sending things to." Teo's voice was low and level, warden's register. "That's still true. Your handler turning out to be a person doesn't make it less true."

"No."

"And it was your employer's clause that put my sister in the ice."

"Yes."

Teo looked at the tray, at the badge, at the small orange figure on its readout that she had not yet logged. "But you came to me with the pulses," she said. "Before you knew they were her. Before you knew they were anyone. You had a handler telling you to treat the depot crew as hostile, and you broke cover far enough to bring a dose warden an unknown signal on her own old band. You didn't know what I'd do with it. I could have walked straight to Saar." She paused. "That cost you something. I've been pretending it didn't, because it was easier to be angry at one thing."

Piq said nothing. Saying anything would have been asking for more than had been offered.

"It doesn't fix it," Teo said.

"I know."

"I'm not—" Teo stopped and restarted, precise as a recalibration. "I'm going to log your badge now. I'll want your Ring D exposure separated from your hangar time, because you've been near that reactor again and I'm not combining those."

"I'll get you the timestamps."

"You'll get me the timestamps." Teo pulled the badge toward her, slotted it into the reader, and watched the number come up. Eleven point four microsieverts for the room. Piq had guessed eleven. For one second something moved across Teo's face that was almost the beginning of the thing it used to be. Then it was gone, filed, logged.

"Article 31," Teo said, without looking up from the reader. "It's real? He can actually do that?"

"He has done it. It's logged with the Society."

"Then Lagrange is paying to bring her home." It was not quite a question. Teo's hand had gone still on the reader.

"Lagrange is paying to bring her home," Piq said. "If we can get there."

Teo finished logging the badge. She held it out across the desk, and Piq took it. For half a second their fingers were on the same small square of plastic. Neither of them moved. Then Teo let go, and Piq clipped it back to her sternum, face out, as the rules required.

"Nineteen days," Teo said.

"From tomorrow at 1612."

"And the drum."

"Thirty-nine days on the reactor drum, from Amaya's last count."

Teo nodded slowly. "Then I'm going to need your delta-v ledger by morning," she said. "All of it. If Lagrange is paying for a rescue, I need to know how much dose it costs, per person, per minute, before anybody gets near the Mule." She turned back to her screen. "Goodnight, Varga."

It was the first time in eleven days she had used a name.

---

Piq did not sleep. Sleep had begun to seem like something other people could afford.

She went back to the true terminal. Under Pell's message nine she wrote a reply of four lines, short, unpadded, under her notarial seal: *Received. Understood. Working. Short from now on.* She sent it at 0210 and watched the tag. Within the hour it changed from ACK PENDING to DELIVERED, DEIMOS. It was the first of her messages to make that journey cleanly in two weeks. It would reach L4 before the dark. He would know she had heard him. That was something. It was the one thing she could give him from here.

At 0340 her covert receiver caught Amaya's pulse on the old dosimetry band, faint, on schedule, in the keying Teo had taught her sister when they were girls. Europa was still clear of Jupiter's limb for another eleven hours. Piq decoded it at her bench with the receiver warm in her hands. It was the only one left on Asgard, now that Saar had the Brigade's.

DRUM 39. DOSE HOLDING. ICE LOUD TONIGHT. TELL HER.

She sat with that until the stacks cycled again and the ozone came up through the vents.

At 0800 she gave Teo the ledger. At 1100 she gave it to Hesper and Lucky in the hangar. It was still one hundred and forty metres per second short of returning to Callisto with four extra bodies aboard. The figure was the same as it had been for a week, except that now there was money behind it and nowhere to send a complaint.

At 1611 she was back in Danso's pump room, because it seemed like the place to be.

The relay status line on Danso's display had been green for as long as she had lived on Callisto. At 1612:00 it went amber. A plain-text notice from Deimos came through, the operator's signature on it one last time:

> DEIMOS STORE-AND-FORWARD ENTERING CONJUNCTION HOLD. ALL INNER-SYSTEM TRAFFIC SUSPENDED. NEXT SCHEDULED CONTACT: T+19 DAYS. — C. A-M.

Then it went grey.

Danso let out a breath he seemed to have been holding since lunch. "Well," he said. "That's us. On our own."

Piq did the sum without wanting to. Nineteen days of silence. Thirty-eight days on the reactor drum as of this morning's count. The relay would come back with nineteen days of drum still burning under the ice, if nothing in the ice went wrong, and a great many things in the ice could go wrong. Europa's next clean window from Callisto opened in sixty-one hours. After that came another, every four and a half days, and each one cost the people on the surface another slice of the drum.

The station address system chimed: three ascending tones, the security register. Every speaker in every ring carried it at once. Danso's headset buzzed faintly against its bracket. Up on the comms rack, Ballast lifted its head.

"This is Marek Saar, Jovian Development Authority Security, Asgard." The voice was level, unhurried, pressed flat. "Effective immediately and for the duration of solar conjunction, all launches from Asgard Yard are suspended under Authority emergency statute. This includes the Sling, all surface pads, all hangar egress, and all vessel movements, crewed or uncrewed. Ice transfers require a security countersignature. Unlicensed departure will be treated as hostile action against Authority infrastructure. This order remains in force until relay contact is restored." A pause, precise to the second. "Thank you for your cooperation."

The tones descended. The ring went quiet. Somewhere below them the electrolysis stacks went on splitting water into breath.

Nineteen days of dark. Thirty-eight days of drum. Sixty-one hours to the next window. And the only man on Callisto who could sign a launch had just made sure no one would.

Piq looked at the grey status line and then down at the notarial die in its housing on her belt. It was physical and old, the kind of thing an older law had trusted because you could hold it.

"Danso," she said. "Who else on this station has to obey a seal?"
