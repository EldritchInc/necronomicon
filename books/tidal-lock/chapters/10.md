# Chapter 10: Interlock

The hangar had a particular silence after Saar left. Piq would have weighed it if she could: eleven people standing very still in a tenth of a gravity, and the air handlers suddenly loud. The empty bench where the receiver had sat still held its outline in dust. Koskinen had carried it out in the grey evidence case with both hands, like a cake he was afraid of dropping, and somebody had already set a coffee bulb in the clean rectangle as if to cover a wound.

Lucky knocked three times on the Mule's skid. Nobody laughed.

Juno said, "Five months," and that was all she said. Five months of archive was gone into a JDA locker. That was every frame the Brigade had ever logged, including the ones from before anyone knew what they were.

Hesper had not moved. She stood with her hands in the thigh pockets of her coverall and her eyes on the bench, and Piq could see her running a calculation with no good terms in it. Behind her the wall chart still read *Europa's down* in somebody's careful block capitals. Under it was the next window: 2241 tonight. Amaya keyed her long frame at 2241 every night the geometry allowed it, the way other people set an alarm, and the Brigade's set had answered with a single carrier pulse. That was one bit, and it meant *we hear you*. It was the only thing they had ever been able to send back.

Tonight nothing would answer her.

Piq checked the time on the hangar's wall clock, which ran four seconds fast and which she had stopped correcting for in her head on her second day. 2201. Forty minutes.

Teo was looking at her. She had been looking at her since Koskinen's boots cleared the hatch. Her jaw had gone white along the hinge, and she was not looking at Saar's back or at the empty bench. She was looking at Piq with the patience of someone who has already done the arithmetic and is waiting to see whether the other party will present the same figure.

*She knows I have an ear.* Teo had seen Piq's logs in the cipher room before anyone in the hangar had shown her a frame. The timestamps had been Piq's and not the Brigade's. Teo corrected people's rounding for a living. She had known for days and had said nothing, and that was its own kind of entry.

There were several ways to do this. Piq listed them, because listing was what she did when she was frightened. She could say nothing and listen alone, and Amaya would hear silence from Callisto on the first night after five months of answers. She could tell the whole room, and in a week eleven people's enthusiasm would put her kit in Saar's evidence locker next to the hangar set. Or she could tell the one person who already knew, and the one person who would decide whether the rest needed to.

She crossed to Hesper. The low gravity made it a short, deliberate glide that looked calmer than she was. She said, quietly, "I have a receiver."

Hesper's eyes came off the bench.

"Survey grade. It's part of my reference set. Ibis certifies us to sweep the bands for interference before we calibrate a scale. Stray RF walks load cells." All of that was true, and none of it was the reason. "It's been on the retired band since my second night. It's how I found the pattern before I found you."

"Your second night," Hesper said.

"Yes."

"And you sat in my hangar and watched us lose ours."

"I sat in your hangar and watched Saar look at the case it's in," Piq said. "He's already seen that one of my masses is hollow. If I'd said anything with him here, there'd be two receivers in a locker and none in the basin."

Hesper considered this. Her face did the thing Piq had seen it do over the crust geometry: it took the problem and turned it until a flat side faced up. "Can it send?"

"Burst. Short. Not much."

"A heartbeat?"

"A heartbeat. More than that if it has to."

"Then go," Hesper said. "Don't walk there with a parade. Teo goes with you, because Teo's going to go with you whether I say so or not." She raised her voice for the room, flat and unhurried. "Shift's done. Juno, cover the floor cuts. Lucky, stop knocking on things, she'll answer when she answers."

---

Piq's quarters were three point one metres by two point four, with a fold-down bunk and a desk that was also the lid of her locker. With two people inside, every movement had to be negotiated in advance. Teo stood with her back to the hatch and her arms folded, so that she took up as little of the room as possible while somehow occupying all of it.

Piq lifted the reference case onto the desk. The five masses sat in their foam: four dense, and one that had read lighter under the densitometer that morning, the way a liar's pulse reads under a cuff. She took out the fourth mass and gave it a quarter-turn and a reverse quarter-turn. The cap came away. Inside, under a skin of tungsten shot bonded to the wall to make up the declared weight, the kit lay in its pads: burst transceiver, antenna lead, receiver, cell.

Teo looked at it for a long moment.

"That isn't survey kit," she said.

"It's very expensive survey kit."

"Varga."

"The antenna runs up the light-pipe," Piq said. She was clipping the lead to the coupler behind the louvre, where Ballast's grit had left a fine grey crescent on the sill. "The pipe's a waveguide for about forty centimetres if you're lucky and nobody's cleaned it. Which nobody has."

Teo did not unfold her arms. She said, very evenly, "Here's what I'm going to do. I'm going to write down that you have an instrument I can't identify, that you've carried it since intake, and that you told me tonight and not before. Then I'm going to stand here while it's useful. You can tell me the rest of what it is when you decide I've earned it, or when you decide you have. I don't much care which."

"That's fair."

"It isn't fair. It's a dose history." She glanced at the clock on Piq's slate. "Twenty-six minutes. Do you need me to do anything or just not breathe on it?"

"Read for me. Your hands are better on her grid than mine."

That was true too, and it was the first thing Piq had said in a week that was true all the way down. Teo heard it. Something in her shoulders gave a millimetre.

At 2241:06 the receiver chirped. It was the badge-alarm tone, so that anyone in the corridor would hear only a resident getting nagged about her dose. The frame came up on the slate in raw pulses, clipped shorts and lingering longs, the hand Piq had learned to read the way you learn a colleague's signature: by the corners it cut.

Teo read it off the bunk grid without paper.

"HANGAR QUIET. YOU OK. DRUM 54." A pause. The doubled final pulse came, Amaya's mark, the thing she'd done at fourteen to show her sister she'd meant it. "Then a question mark. She uses a long-long-short for it. I gave her that. I don't think I've seen it in nine years."

"She's asking why nobody answered."

"She's asking if *we're* all right." Teo's voice did not change, but her hand had come up flat against the wall. "Of course she is."

Piq had already built the burst: a single carrier pulse and then, because one bit seemed suddenly too small a thing to send to a woman under a kilometre of ice, the grid code for *HEAR YOU* and Amaya's own doubled final pulse back at her.

She made herself say the cost aloud. "Saar logs everything I transmit. He can't read it, but he'll have the azimuth. Until now everything's gone up toward Mars. This goes to Europa."

"Will he know the difference?"

"If he looks. He looks."

Teo's eyes were on the frame and the question mark. "Send it."

Piq sent it. It took four tenths of a second. On the meter it looked like nothing at all.

---

Directive 2214-31a went up on the station notice board at 0600 on Day 22. Piq read it in the queue for the Ring C galley, between a man with an illegal cardamom pod tucked behind one ear and a child carrying a water ration in both hands as if it were a sacrament.

*Further to 2214-31: for the purposes of this directive, "ice" includes any separated composite material of which frozen water forms a constituent part by mass, including but not limited to regolith-bonded deposits, crust, floor material and fill. Such material, once separated, shall be deemed to be at its point of origin wherever it lies at the time of this notice, and shall be held there pending countersignature. — M. Saar, JDA-SEC.*

She read it twice. It was good drafting. Saar had stayed up with the statute as promised, and he had not tried to undo the floor gambit. He had frozen it in place. Twelve of the thirty-one crust blocks had gone onto the Mule's frame before midnight. The other nineteen lay chalked and initialled on the hangar floor, three metres off the port skid, and as of 0600 that was legally where they had come from. They could not move the three metres. They could not move a millimetre.

She drew a square in the 2214-31 column of her ledger. *Later.* Squares were not answers. They were promises to look.

"Auditor."

Fitch was standing by the galley hatch with something under his arm. He was the environmental tech for Ring B, with mournful eyebrows and nine months of a 4.1 kg discrepancy in his scrubber residuals, and he had the air of a man who has been patient about a thing for longer than is healthy. The something was a trap: a wire box with a spring door and, inside it, a load cell and a sliver of reconstituted fish.

"I've been meaning to find you," he said. "You're certified."

"I am."

"Then you can certify a mass." He hefted the box. "It's in the ducts. Ring B east, around Junction Fourteen. Nine months I've been carrying four point one kilos of unexplained biological in my residuals, and the regional office thinks I'm cooking the filters. I'm not cooking the filters. Something is *eating* in my ducts and breathing in my ducts and leaving—" he lowered his voice, "—*deposits*. If I catch it and you weigh it, it's on the books. If it's on the books, I'm not a liar."

Piq thought of the 4.1 kg blip her sensor sweep had flagged in a ventilation duct on her second day, and of what she had found when she put her lamp to the grille: a notched ear, a white bib, and a tabby face regarding her without the least concern, with the end of her ration bar in its mouth. It had turned and gone off down the duct like a judgement.

"I can only certify a mass that's presented," she said. "A mass without a manifest isn't a mass. It's a rumour."

"So help me present it."

"Fitch, I've got nineteen tonnes of floor that's legally where it's lying. I can't take on a rumour this week."

He looked at her for a long, sorrowful moment. Then, with dignity, he said, "It took your ration bar, didn't it."

"I'm not at liberty to discuss my ration bar."

"It's taken everybody's ration bar," Fitch said, with something close to love, and went off toward Ring B with the trap held out in front of him like a lantern.

---

Teo's office at the end of Ring A smelled of centrifuge oil and her tea, which had gone to tar in its bulb sometime the previous evening and been neither drunk nor thrown away. The card on the shelf still said COUNTS ARE NOT GRADES. Under it, today, a second card had appeared in Teo's square hand: NEITHER ARE DOSES.

She had the triage on the wall screen when Piq came in at 1500. It was a grid. It would be.

"I need four histories," Teo said, without turning. "I don't have them. What I've got is Amaya's 'thirty-eight a day', which I don't believe."

"You think she's lying."

"I think she's rounding in the direction that keeps the thing from breaking." It was Hesper's phrase, and Teo used it like a diagnosis. "Thirty-eight millisieverts a day isn't a living dose under that much ice. If they'd taken thirty-eight a day for fourteen months they'd have sixteen sieverts each and they'd be keying from the afterlife. Under a few metres of overburden, in a cut, their living dose should be a fraction of that. A few mSv. Maybe four." She tapped a cell, and the column beneath it lit. "Thirty-eight is what you get if somebody goes up to the surface every night and stands at a mast for a few minutes to key."

Piq had already done that sum in the corridor and had not wanted to be the one to say it. Five sieverts a day was about three and a half millisieverts a minute in the open. A suit did very little against what the trailing hemisphere threw. Ten minutes at the mast, with the walk up and the walk down, cost a little over thirty.

"It's the transmitter," she said. "Someone's carrying it up."

"Someone." Teo turned around at last. She had her reading glasses on, low, and over them her eyes were steady and appalling. "It's her. Of course it's her. She was fourteen and I told her that if you have to spend dose, spend it fast and spend it once. She took that to mean you're allowed to spend it."

"Then tell her not to."

"That's the plan." Teo pulled up a second sheet. "Message one. Stop the mast runs except on alternate nights. Rotate the keyer. I want each of their cumulative doses, estimated, and a symptom list. Nausea, mouth, skin, bruising, hair. I want Rhee's cough described in five words, not one." Her finger moved down the grid. "Message two goes once I know where everyone stands. Surface sequence for the pickup. Who comes out first, in what order, how long each of them is exposed between crevasse and crawler. Heaviest dose last, because the last one waits in the most ice. Unless they can't walk, in which case they go first, because whoever carries them carries them fast."

"And the rescuers?"

"That's my column." She did not elaborate. Piq looked at the column anyway. In the cell for medic Teo had written a number that was not a dose but a ceiling, and next to it a second number that was higher, in pencil, the way you write a figure you have decided you will be allowed to exceed.

Piq did not mention it. She did not have the standing.

"Can you get all that into her grid?" she said instead. "It's long."

"It'll go in three nights if you can give me forty pulses a frame."

"I can give you forty. More, and the burst starts to look like a conversation."

"It *is* a conversation."

"Not on Saar's azimuth log."

Teo exhaled through her nose and changed the number to forty.

There was one more thing Piq had to put in, and she had been carrying it since the claim file. She put it in now as an auditor would, as a line item that needed verifying.

"Ask her for the flight recorder."

Teo's hand stopped on the grid.

"The Ninefold Grace logged state, mass and control inputs until it hit the ice," Piq said. "If the recorder survived, and if they still have power to read it, it exists. You've been appealing a denial for fourteen months on the strength of a dose log and your own certainty. A recorder isn't certainty. It's evidence."

"It'll cost her drum."

"It'll cost her drum," Piq agreed. "She can decide whether it's worth it. She's an engineer."

Teo looked at the wall and the long grid of other people's bodies. "She'll say yes. She'll say yes before I've finished asking."

"Then let her answer first. Nobody else got to."

Teo added a row.

Piq took the folded scrap out of the thigh pocket where it had been riding for two days. She had copied it from Amaya's frame on the night of Day 20, in her own hand, three letters and a word: *TELL T EAT*. She set it on the desk next to the dead tea and said nothing.

Teo read it. Then she read it again. Then she took her glasses off, which Piq had learned was what she did instead of anything else. She opened the drawer under the centrifuge, took out a ration bar, peeled it, and ate it standing up, mechanically and completely, looking at the wall the whole time.

"She always told on me," she said when she'd finished. "To our mother. *Teo didn't eat.* Every shift change."

Piq kept her eyes on the grid.

---

Amaya's answer came at 2241 on Day 22, through the light-pipe and the cheap old coupler, while Teo sat on the end of Piq's bunk with her boots drawn up so they wouldn't touch anything.

It was not long. Amaya had never been long.

*OK ALT NIGHTS. ODUYA KEYS TMRW. RECORDER YES. 4 NIGHTS. COSTS 1 DAY DRUM. WORTH. DRUM 53.*

Then the doubled pulse.

"She said yes before I asked," Teo said quietly. "We sent the request in the same frame as the triage. She answered the last line first."

"She had a tape of the frame running," Piq said. "She read the end."

"She read what she wanted."

Piq was looking at the number. *DRUM 53.*

The first clean frame she had ever decoded, on the night of Day 15, the one that had come up FOUR ALIVE, had said sixty. She had taken it on faith because she had wanted to. On Day 20 it had said fifty-five. Last night, fifty-four. Tonight, fifty-three. Seven days on the calendar, seven days off the drum, to the day, every time she had a reading.

Reactors did not do that. A drum burning under a habitat with a sick man in it, a mast run every night and somebody melting ice to hold a crevasse open did not lose exactly one day per day. A table did.

"Teo," she said. "What's she measuring the drum against?"

"Days at draw."

"At which draw?"

Teo turned her head.

Piq already had the reactor plate up. Ninefold Grace drew from the same TK-Δ series family as the Mule, an older lot, and the depletion curves were standard Compact public data, because anything with fission in it had to be. In the Day 15 frame, between the dose figures, Amaya had sent a thermal output number. Piq had logged it in a column headed *?* because she hadn't known what it was for. She knew now. She ran it.

"The drum curve is sixty days at hold draw," she said. "Hold is the lowest the reactor goes without the control drums seizing. It keeps the habitat around four degrees, scrubbers on half. Her count's been dropping one a day exactly, which means she isn't reading the drum at all. She looked up the hold-draw figure once and she's been subtracting the calendar from it. And I don't think they've been at hold. Not with Rhee coughing, and not with Oduya on a mast every alternate night, and not with somebody heating up ice to keep a crevasse open. Her thermal number says they've been above it the whole time."

"So the real count is lower."

"She's quoting the best case for the worst life. That's—" Piq stopped herself before she could say *her flaw*, because she had no right to the word. "That's optimism."

"Give me the number."

Piq gave it to her the way she would have given it to an underwriter, with the error bar. "At her reported thermal output, with the recorder transfer costed in as she says, the drum hits shutdown margin on Day 51. Plus or minus three."

Teo did the subtraction aloud, because she needed to hear a voice do it. "Blackout opens on thirty-eight. Closes on fifty-seven."

"So the rescue has to fly inside the blackout. Which we knew. But it can't fly at the end of it. It has to fly early."

"With nineteen blocks frozen to the floor."

"And a hundred and forty metres per second we don't have."

Teo looked at her for a long time. Then she said, "Write it in your book."

Piq wrote it. *DRUM: Amaya 53 (hold table minus calendar). Recalc 51 ± 3 (actual draw). Fly early.* She drew no square next to it. There was no *later* left in that line.

---

The recorder came in over four nights, and over four days the station went about its business as though nothing in it were dying.

On Day 23 Koskinen was posted to the hangar floor with a clipboard and a chalk line. He was to note any displacement of the nineteen frozen blocks greater than two centimetres, which was his best reading of *wherever it lies*. Lucky asked him whether thermal expansion counted. Koskinen said he'd ask. He asked. Saar's office sent back the word *no* and nothing else. Koskinen pinned it to his clipboard and looked relieved.

On Day 23 Juno's tithe block was still held in the lift and still dripping into Koskinen's cup. Piq tared it for him again on the way past, and he thanked her as if she'd done him a favour.

On Day 24 Fitch's trap caught a cleaning drone.

On Day 24 Piq wrote her report to Pell. It went out as 0941, padded in the usual way to look like a bulky routine upload: forty megabytes of depot calibration tables wrapped around four kilobytes of truth. *Survivors confirmed alive, see 0911, 0927. Drum recalculated: failure D51 ±3. Flight recorder of NFG in receipt, partial. Request urgent advice re: claim LM-C-2213-1146. Request urgent advice re: R7 order.* Her outbound queue now had seven items. Every one of them was labelled ACK PENDING in the same patient grey. She looked at the column and thought *the relay is batching* and *Pell never acks until he's decided*. She closed the queue the way you close the door on a room you'll clean later.

On Day 25 at 2244 the last packet cleared the coupler. Amaya had added her mark to it twice.

---

They worked it in Teo's office, because Teo's office had the larger screen and a door that her own override could lock. Teo, it turned out, could lock it against her own override too, a feature she had requested and been granted after an incident she declined to describe.

A flight recorder is not a story. It is a sequence of numbers and switch states, flattened to a few kilobytes a second and then, in this case, flattened again by a woman in a crevasse who had chosen which channels mattered and thrown the rest away. Amaya had chosen well. She had kept mass state, propellant state, tank valve states, the control log and the dose sensor, and nothing else.

Piq laid it out on the screen as a ledger. She could not have laid it out any other way.

"T zero," she said. "Departure burn from Callisto parking. Total mass 117.4 tonnes: dry about thirty-eight, propellant 61.4, shielding 18. It matches the filed plan to the kilogram. They weren't overloaded. Whoever loaded them was good."

"Hesper," Teo said. "Hesper loaded them."

Piq kept going. "T plus 1.2 hours, burn complete. Propellant state nominal. Shielding tanks show *locked*, which is—"

She stopped.

Teo leaned in. "Which is what?"

Piq scrolled back through the valve channels. Six shielding tank valves were listed, sorted A to F. Through the departure burn every one of them read open to the feed. At burn cutoff, all six changed state together and took on a flag in a field the filed telemetry hadn't included. The flag was a five-character code. She had seen it before on placards and on the faces of compliance boxes on tug decks. She had once seen it in a Lagrange training manual in a font so small it might have been a disclaimer.

*14C/I.*

"It's an interlock," she said.

"A what?"

"Clause 14(c). The dose clause. When I came, I recited it to the tug crew in Bay Three." She could hear herself in that bay twenty-odd days ago, word-perfect, a little pleased. "*Minimum shielding water, scaled by filed transit time, to be retained for the duration of the exposed transit.* Retained. The 2213 revision made any cover on a Europa run conditional on an interlock box that enforces it. It locks the shielding tanks out of the propellant feed until the vessel is on the surface or out of the torus. And it doesn't arm on the pad. It arms at departure cutoff, so you can still trim and dump on the ground."

Teo said nothing for a moment. "They're water tanks."

"They're water tanks."

"Then how did Hesper file a plan that counted them as fuel? She doesn't make that kind of mistake."

"She didn't make one." Piq pulled the filing header up beside the valve log. "She filed on the tug pool's standing Europa template. It's older than the revision. Its mass sheet still books shielding as *consumable on capture*, because before 2213 that's what it was. The Grace's box was a retrofit." The maintenance stamp was there in the header, in Lagrange's own format. "Fitted nineteen days before departure, to make the revision's compliance date. Fitted to the tanks, not written into the template. So every pre-flight check on Callisto ran against six unlocked tanks of water, because on the pad they *were* unlocked. Nothing in the system saw those tanks the way the box would until the box armed. By then they'd already burned."

"Every pilot on this station used to burn their blankets on capture," Teo said slowly. "I've heard them complain about it for years. You carried the shield in and you burned it in the last minutes, when you were already past the worst of the belt, and now they—" She stopped. "Lucky. Lucky said three hundred metres a second."

"Lucky said Lagrange's water blankets cost him three hundred metres per second on every Europa job," Piq said. Her mouth was dry. "Because they do, now. He has to carry them all the way down as dead mass. They're not reaction mass any more."

She pulled the next channel up.

"T plus 4.1 hours. The flight computer runs the capture-and-landing check against current state. The check fails." The log was there in plain text, because flight computers had no tact. *CAPTURE PROFILE FAST-3.9 — REQ 3.59 KM/S — AVAIL 3.22 KM/S — SHLD UNAVAIL (14C/I) — DEFICIT 0.37 KM/S.* "Here's the arithmetic. After the departure burn, with the shielding locked out as dead mass, they've got three point two two kilometres a second left in the tanks. That's the whole budget. The fast capture and landing needs three point five nine. Three hundred and seventy short. If the blankets had counted as propellant, the way the template said they would, they'd have had about three point seven and it closes with margin."

"Could they turn back?"

Piq had already run it, because she ran everything. "Capture back at Callisto costs about three point three. Against the same three point two two. Eighty short. That doesn't close either. They're past the turn."

"So what does close?"

"T plus 4.2 hours." The control log had it, and beside it, in a field the flight computer reserved for crew annotation, three words in a pilot's two-finger shorthand: *only one closes.* "Rhee switches to the low-energy profile. A long Ganymede-perturbed fall. Capture and landing on that path cost about two point nine, which fits inside three point two two with the blankets still locked aboard, because it pays in time instead of propellant. Nine point six days instead of three point nine. And it shelves in the plasma torus for eleven hours instead of four."

"The torus shelf," Teo said. She knew her sister's dose log by heart; Piq had watched her recite it. "Eleven hours against four predicted."

"Yes."

"And the spike."

Piq ran the dose channel against the control log and found it at once, because a thing that size could not hide. T plus 9.0 days. A forty-second burst, dense, high on the energy scale, exactly as Amaya had logged it. The adjusters had called it a *terminal artifact*, a sensor having its last fit as the ship died. Two seconds into that burst, the flight computer's state field read *SEU-LATCH*. Then it read nothing. For six minutes it read nothing, and the control inputs that followed were not automatic. They were jagged and human: Rhee flying a descent by hand from a dead computer's last solution, and nearly making it.

"Single-event upset," Piq said. Her voice had gone flat in a way she recognised from her father in the weigh-house, reading a scale that told him something he didn't want to know. "A heavy ion through the processor. It latched up and lost the descent solution, and Rhee hand-flew the rest. They came down hard, but they came down. Telemetry ended at T plus 9.1 because the antenna went into the ice, not because they died."

The room was quiet. The centrifuge ticked as it cooled.

"Say it," Teo said. "Say the whole thing in order. I want to hear it in one line."

Piq said it in one line, because she owed her that.

"Lagrange's dose clause made them carry eighteen tonnes of water they weren't allowed to burn. That put the fast transfer out of reach. The only path that closed was slow. The slow path held them in the torus nearly three times as long. The torus killed the flight computer. Then Lagrange denied the claim because the pilot had *deviated to a low-energy trajectory*, and called it pilot error."

Teo stood up.

It was not quick. In 0.126 g nothing was quick unless you meant it to be. She rose with the slow inevitability of a weight on a long arm, and when she was upright her hands were shaking. Piq had never seen them do that, not on the valve, and not in the cipher room when the first letters came out.

"Fourteen months," Teo said. "Fourteen months of dispute fees. Forty marks a filing, every time I wanted them to look again, and they looked. *They looked.* They had the interlock spec. It's their box. It's their clause. They wrote the rule that put my sister into the torus, and then they billed me to ask why she was there." Her voice did not rise, and that was the worst thing about it. "And they've still got her water. Did you know that? Her return reserve. Twenty-six tonnes the Grace staged here to fill up on for the trip home, and it's still sitting in Tank Seven in our own tug pool. Lagrange has it booked as an *offset* against the claim. She's alive under a kilometre of ice, and they're holding the water she was supposed to come home on as part of the settlement."

Piq knew. It was listed under OFFSETS on page eleven of LM-C-2213-1146, which she had read in her quarters by the light of her own slate on the night of Day 18. On page four of the same file, against *Shielding: 18.0 t per Clause 14(c)*, was a tick in her own hand. She had ticked it as compliant. She had ticked it as correct.

"They put her in the ice with a safety rule," Teo said, "and they called it her captain's fault, and they wrote her off, and they're still collecting on her. Fourteen months of her down there and they never once had to look. And people like you—"

She caught herself. Her eyes went, for half a second, to the thigh pocket where Piq's slate rode, and Piq knew exactly what she was seeing: the foam, the hollow mass, the kit in its pads. *That isn't survey kit.* Teo had said it two nights ago in a room three metres long. She had written it down.

Then, visibly, she set it aside. Her hand went flat to the wall, the way it had in Piq's quarters.

"No," she said. "Not you. I know what I saw in your case. I've decided what I'm going to believe about it, and I'm going to believe that tonight." She closed her eyes. "You're an auditor. You count other people's tonnes. You don't write their rules. I'm sorry." A breath. "Vultures. I said it the day you came in, and I didn't know how right I was."

Piq heard *not you*. It went into her the way the 14C/I code had: five small characters, and the whole structure behind them. Teo had not missed anything. She had looked straight at the hollow mass and chosen, deliberately, to extend credit against it. That was worse than if she had forgotten.

Piq looked at the screen. The latch-up stood there in plain text, and after it six minutes of Rhee's hands flying a dying ship onto Europa, and after that fourteen months of ice. She was not the one who had denied the claim. She had not written the clause or fitted the box. She had only recited it, ticked it, and sent seven reports to the people who had done all three. In every one of them she had said *Ibis Metrology* when what she meant was *Lagrange Mutual*.

Her own ledger was not balancing. She had kept it since the cycler. Its entries ran *what I said* against *what was so*, and every account she had ever run had been allowed a little float of omission, because omission was not a lie, only a delay. Tonight the float was larger than the account. The figure in the *owed* column had a name. The name was standing four feet away with shaking hands and her sister's water still on her tongue, and it had just apologised to her, knowingly, for something she had done.

Piq knew how this went. She had watched other adjusters do it. You let the anger burn itself out against the institution. You let the person you've been lying to keep thinking you're on their side, because in the way that matters you are. You find the moment, later, when the confession costs less, when the rescue's flown and the claim's reversed and the truth is a story about the past instead of a knife in the present. You do it in the right order.

She had always believed there was a right order. That was the lie under all the others.

"Teo," she said.

Teo opened her eyes.

Piq didn't say it. Not here, under the centrifuge light and the second card, with the flight log still open and the latch-up still on the screen. Not in the room where Teo ate ration bars because her sister told her to. If she was going to break this, she would break it somewhere it could be heard properly, somewhere with Jupiter in it, somewhere Teo had chosen herself the first time she had wanted someone to understand a thing.

"Come to the dome with me," she said. "Tomorrow. Before shift. There's something I have to put on your books, and I want to do it under the old man."

Teo looked at her for a long time. Her hands were still now, but only because she was holding them still.

"Is it going to make this worse?"

Piq thought of the tick in her own hand on page four. She thought of seven grey flags, ACK PENDING, in a queue she had not wanted to read.

"Yes," she said.

Teo nodded, once, slowly, the way she nodded at a count she didn't like but believed.

"Oh-six-hundred," she said. "Don't be late. I'll know if you are."

When Piq let herself out into Ring A, the corridor was empty except for a fine trail of grey grit running along the top of the cable tray toward Ring B and Fitch's patient trap. She stood there for a moment with her slate against her chest and opened her ledger to a clean page. At the head of it she wrote the date and the time, 0600, and under that one line, *Tell her.* She drew no square beside it, and no question mark either. She drew an equals sign, the place where you stop looking. Then she went to her quarters to spend the night failing to rehearse.
