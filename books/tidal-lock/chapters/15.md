# Chapter 15: Cheap Seats

At 0709 the depot feed came back. The hangar told Piq before the gauge did. A low note rose in the transfer line and went up through the deck, through the crate she was using as a desk, into the heels of her hands. Tank C had been frozen at sixty-one percent for fifty-one minutes. Now it began to climb again, at the pace of a woman at the far end of the station opening a valve with her own arm.

Piq wrote *0709, feed restored, audit custody* in the margin. The clock above the hangar door said she had nine hours and three minutes. Under it she wrote the number she had been writing since D26 and had not yet managed to cross out.

*Return CAL: −140 m/s.*

Beside it was the honest symbol, a question mark, and beside that the word *Ganymede*. All night it had read to her like a joke someone else had told.

She hadn't slept since D26. She noticed it the way you notice a scale reading high: the instrument might be drifting, but you still have to work with what it shows. Her handwriting was fine. Her arithmetic was fine. The trouble was the edges. Twice in the last hour she had looked up and found that a block of ice had moved across the hangar without her seeing it go.

Achterberg came back through the side hatch with his hands empty and the look of a man who had been insulted in a new way.

"Forty-one minutes," he said. "For a countersignature on one block. It's the same block I took to him yesterday. The one he already countersigned. He asked me whether the block had changed."

"Had it?"

"It had sublimated. Slightly. He made me reweigh it." Achterberg held up a slip. "Point three kilograms lighter. He wanted to know where the point three went."

"Into the air," Piq said. "Then the struts. Then the humidity complaints. I can give you a citation."

"I gave him yours." Achterberg's mouth twitched. "He signed. The gardener from Gallery Two was in the queue behind me with her irrigation bucket again, so I don't think I'm the one who's having the worst day."

"Did he ask if it was going to Europa?"

"He asked if it had been to Europa. She told him it had been to the lettuces, and that the lettuces had a better safety record than his office." He set the slip on the crate and lined it up square with her ledger, as if he had decided she was owed that much. "Nineteen blocks left. Thirteen through the queue by noon, if the tall one keeps his door open. Koskinen's cutting the rest on the skid. If we're cutting on the skid, nothing's moving, so it isn't a transfer."

"That's correct," Piq said. "Please never say it out loud near a terminal."

He went off. Above the cradle doors something shifted in the ductwork with a soft four-kilogram sound. A tabby face with a notched ear looked down at the cold ration bar on her crate with the patient interest of a creature that had never once been weighed. Piq moved the bar six centimetres closer to her elbow, gave the cat one look, and declined to log it.

---

Teo came in at 0724. She carried a tablet, her reading glasses and a mug of tea dark enough to have a mass of its own. She hadn't slept either. On her the lack of sleep showed as a tighter version of what she already was: hair cable-tied higher, the cracked brass dosimeter turned face-in against her collarbone.

"He wants sequencing," she said. She didn't say who, and didn't need to. "I told him tables by eight. He'll have them by eight." She set the tablet down on Piq's ledger and turned it around. "Read me his questions again. I want to answer the ones he actually asked, not the ones I think he meant."

Piq had read them four times since the message came in. Every time, she had read them as an auditor reads a buyer's due-diligence request, looking for what the buyer was really trying to find out. She had filed them under *threat* with the war-risk purchase and the tanker. It had been a reasonable place to put them. Her father would have called it a reasonable place to put a crate you hadn't opened yet.

She read them aloud.

"Cumulative dose for a surface party of three persons. Duration not to exceed four hours. Terrain type: Conamara chaos or equivalent. Shielding: Ur-pattern pressurized crawler, eleven centimetres borated composite. And guidance on the sequencing of non-ambulatory persons during transfer between a surface shelter and the crawler."

"Yes," Teo said.

"Three persons," Piq said. "Not four. He isn't asking how to bring four people out. He's asking how many to send in."

Teo looked at her over the glasses.

"Four hours," Piq said. "You don't put a four-hour cap on a reconnaissance. You cap reconnaissance at the minimum time to see what you came to see, and you don't ask about the dose of the people doing it. You set four hours when you've already decided you'll be carrying something. Conamara isn't a guess." She was talking faster now, the way she did when a column started to sum. "He's naming the terrain, so he has a bearing. Borated composite is neutron shielding, which means a reactor nearby that isn't the crawler's. And non-ambulatory." She stopped. "You told me yesterday. You said he was asking how not to kill people."

"I did say that."

"I put it in the wrong column."

"You put it in the column you were paid to keep," Teo said. She didn't say it to cut. She said it the way she'd read out a dose, as a fact about what had been absorbed. "Go on. You've got the face."

"What face?"

"The face you had in the alcove when the gaps lined up." Teo pulled the second stool across with her foot and sat. "Go on."

---

Piq laid it out on the hangar floor because the crate was too small. Gustav Varga had taught her to do that on the cargo deck of a ship she no longer named, even to herself. When the manifest won't close, take everything off the shelf and put it on the floor where you can walk around it. Gross, tare, contents. Exceptions last.

She had been doing exceptions first, and she knew it.

Four sheets. She weighed them down with whatever was to hand: a torque wrench, Teo's mug, the ration bar, and the thumb-sized impound stamp, which she set down carefully because it had already won her one argument. When she glanced back at the ration bar a minute later, one corner of its wrapper had been opened with surgical precision. The duct above was empty. She logged nothing.

The first sheet was the wedding feed. She had frozen it on the frame where Ziva Kalnins stood at the tanker's helm in a borrowed dress, saying *tanks full, and I come home* into a three-second delay. Over Ziva's shoulder, in the corner of the frame, was the Steadfast Margin's tank board. Piq had read it on the night of the wedding and written beside it: *≈3 km/s excess. Why?* Back then the answer had been: to throw something, to chase something, to reach something the JDA didn't want reached.

The second sheet was Pell's message about war-risk cover. Ur had bought war-risk over the JDA's claimed Europa zone. She was an insurer, or she had been until about three days ago, and she knew what war-risk was for. Nobody buys war-risk to start a war. Wars are excluded or priced so high that a sane buyer self-insures. You buy war-risk when you are about to do something you expect to be called an act of war. You buy it the way you'd pay a notary: in advance, so that afterward someone has to read the clause aloud.

The third sheet was Haddad's questions, and they were no longer the questions of an enemy.

The fourth was the tanker's orbit. JDA bulletins called it a loiter and a provocation, a Martian-aligned tanker parked on a Ganymede-Callisto transfer with nothing to do but be seen. Piq had logged it as a buyer waiting for delivery. Now she pulled the public ephemeris up on her slate and did what she should have done at the start: she stopped asking where the ship was and asked where the orbit went. She propagated it forward. It wasn't a loiter. It was a phasing orbit. Tomorrow, on D29, it reached a node where one burn would drop the Steadfast Margin onto a Ganymede-Europa transfer and park it high on the Europa end, just outside the worst of the torus. The size of that burn matched the excess on Ziva's tank board.

It sat there like a hand held out.

"Lucky," Piq said, without looking up.

He was on the port skid with Koskinen, holding a block of ice that was nominally the size of a person and, under 0.126 g, weighed about as much as a full duffel. He let it settle onto the skid and came over in long flat strides, still talking to Koskinen over his shoulder about whether cut faces should go hull-side or space-side for luck.

"Boss."

"Before the receiver was confiscated. In the hangar. You said something about Ganymede."

"I say a lot of things about Ganymede. Ziva lives there. I'm legally obliged to."

"The cheap seats."

"Oh." He grinned, then saw the floor and stopped grinning. "Ganymede's the cheap seats, but Ur would shoot us. It's a tug joke. Europa to Ganymede's downhill. Well, it isn't downhill, it's sideways, but sideways is cheaper than up. Everybody knows you'd come home cheaper to Ur if Ur weren't..." He looked at the four sheets and the orbit on her slate. "Piq. Why are you looking at me like I'm a receipt?"

"Because you were right, and I logged it as a joke." She tapped the phasing node. "Where will she be tomorrow?"

He crouched. A pilot's eyes went over the arc faster than hers had. She watched him find the burn, find the transfer, and stop where the orbit stopped, high on the Europa side, holding.

"That isn't a loiter," he said quietly.

"No."

"That's a tanker waiting for a lander. That's someone parked where they can come down and pick..." He stopped again, and his face did something complicated and quite young. "Tanks full, and I come home. She said it in the vows. She told me. She stood there in that dress and told me, and I cried, and I thought it was about..." He put a hand flat on the deck. "She was telling me where she'd be."

"She was careful about what she said on an open channel," Teo said.

"She's careful about what she says on a closed one." He laughed. It came out wet. "God. I married a spy."

"You married a tanker pilot," Piq said, and kept her eyes on the slate. "Get me the rendezvous numbers. I want the match at the highest point she could hold if her crew is dose-limited the way Ur crews are. Teo, what's Ur's surface allowance?"

"They don't have one. They have an annual. A hundred and fifty millisieverts for Margin-class crews, waivered. She can't sit in the torus. She'll sit where the dose falls off." Teo was already pulling a table. "There." She drew it on the slate with a stylus: an isodose curve wrapping Europa's orbit like a cuff. "Above that, a few tenths of a millisievert an hour through hull. Below it you start paying."

"Then that's where she'll be," Piq said, "and that's where we have to get to."

---

She rebuilt the ledger on the back of the war-risk sheet, because it was the sheet with the most room.

Mule dry, 51 tonnes. Crust when complete, 21.8. Crew and consumables, 4.2. Survivors, 2.4 tonnes for four people, Teo's medical margin included, written in Teo's hand on a sticky note Piq had refused to throw away. Isp nine hundred seconds. Legs: three point three two to Europa; three point five nine down; three point five nine up; three point three two home.

She crossed out the fourth leg.

She didn't cross it out cleanly. Her pen dragged through it, she had to go over it twice, and for a second the tiredness came up so close to the surface that the numbers swam. Teo's hand came down on the edge of the sheet to hold it still. It didn't touch hers. It was two centimetres away. Piq logged that too and went on.

There would be no Callisto return. There would be ascent from Conamara, then a climb up the cuff of Teo's isodose curve to a tanker that would already be waiting with its own reaction mass and its own reactor, and an airlock that had been to a wedding.

Lucky called it out from his slate. "Ascent onto the transfer and the match at her height, with her doing her half of the match. Three point one one. If she does more than half, less. If Ziva does it, she'll do more than half."

"I'm not ledgering your wife's generosity."

"You should. It's very reliable."

Piq ran it. She ran the mass ratio forward and backward, the way her father had taught her: forward to see whether it closed, backward to see whether it had been lying. Her fingers knew exponents the way other people's fingers know prayers. She heard the hangar going on around her: the transfer line's note, Koskinen's cutter on the skid, Juno saying *less ice?* to someone in a voice that meant she was asking again because she hadn't believed the first answer.

The equals sign came. Piq wrote the number on the far side of it, and for the first time in two days it had no minus in front.

*+68 m/s.*

Sixty-eight metres a second. You could spend that on a single bad hover. A nav error could eat it. So could a crawler that came back heavy, or a pilot who tapped the hull three times and then hesitated one second too long over a crevasse. It was thin.

It was also on the right side of the line.

"Say it," Teo said.

"It closes."

Nobody in the hangar cheered. Seven people had stopped what they were doing, and the sound they made wasn't a cheer. It was the sound of something heavy set down that had been carried a long way. Rafe sat on the skid. Achterberg put both hands on top of his head. Koskinen turned the cutter off. In the new quiet the transfer line was very loud.

"It closes if he says yes," Piq said. "I have a number, not a ship. The ship belongs to a man who has spent a month talking to us in formal notices." She stood. Her knees told her how long she had been crouched, and in this gravity that was saying something. "I'm going to call him."

"Openly?" Teo said.

"Openly." Piq looked at her. "Saar's had everything we've done through a crack in the wall. Prew copies him on your tables. The monitor has your three emissions. He'll know within the hour whatever I say. So I want him to know it from a record. Intra-Jovian traffic doesn't go through the relay. It's timestamped at both ends. If I call the head of Ur Station on an open line and ask him to help us bring four people out of the ice, then whatever happens next happens to the people who said no in public."

Teo held her eyes for a moment longer than the logistics needed.

"Danso's shack," Teo said. "I'll walk you. You're not walking anywhere alone today."

---

Danso had been awake as long as she had. He was eating dry cereal from the box, one piece at a time, the way people eat when they've forgotten food comes in quantities.

"I didn't tell anyone," he said as soon as she was through the door.

"I know."

"Two people asked me. I said I was very busy with the conjunction queue. I pointed at the screen. You'd be amazed how often that works."

"I'm going to make it not work," Piq said. "Open channel to Ur Station. Commissioner's office. Log it at both ends, and put my notary number in the header."

Danso looked at her, then at Teo, then at the box of cereal as if it might advise him. "Saar's office copies that log."

"Yes."

"Every word."

"Every word," Piq said. "Danso. If this goes badly, you put the call through because a registered notary invoked her standing and you had no basis to refuse. Say that."

"I put it through because a registered notary invoked her standing and I had no basis to refuse," Danso said. "I also put it through because I want to see what happens." He raised the link. "Ganymede's at about five and a half light-seconds this morning. Round trip eleven. Don't talk over him. He hates it."

"How do you know?"

"I listen to the wedding feed when I'm sad."

The grey Ur seal came up and held while the handshake crossed the gap. Piq stood in front of it and did the sum she had been putting off since the floor.

Notary and mass auditor. That was the true half of her, the half with a certificate and a stamp. It was the half that had sat across the table from Saar and said *routine audit uploads, Ibis Metrology, check with them in a hundred minutes if you can.* It had held because he couldn't check. Say only that half now, and it would go on holding.

It would also leave 244 tonnes of Authority ice inside a hull that a group of station workers had diverted, and an unlicensed launch into a claimed zone. On the record it would be theft carried out by people she liked. Pell's ninth message, the last one, the one that had finally caught up with her, had put the other half in writing: *Lagrange's salvage clause can fund the rescue.* A salvage clause was paper, and paper needed someone of record to invoke it before the event, in a place where it could not later be unsaid. There was exactly one adjuster of record in the Jupiter system. The channel in front of her was the most public record she would ever have.

The cost was simple to state. Ibis was finished. Saar would have the line within the hour, and he would know that every upload he had failed to verify had gone to Special Losses. Lucky was somewhere in the corridor behind her, as he always was when something mattered. So was half the Brigade. They had taken her in as the auditor who could count, and they had never asked whose money she counted for.

Asset: the salvage claim, the Brigade's cover, a rescue that someone would be obliged to pay for. Liability: herself.

The columns weren't close.

"Piq," Teo said quietly behind her. It wasn't a question. It meant *I can see you doing it.*

"I know," Piq said, and the seal resolved into a man.

Yusuf Haddad-Ferreira was smaller than she had pictured. He sat very straight, with a sense of having been sitting very straight for a long time. Behind him there was no window, only a wall of paper binders labelled by hand. Piq liked him at once, which she distrusted, and she set it aside.

"Commissioner. This is Piq Varga, registered notary and mass auditor on Asgard Yard, and an adjuster of record for Lagrange Mutual Special Losses. I'm on an open channel. This call is being logged at both ends and copied to the JDA Office of Security on Asgard." She took a breath. "I believe the Steadfast Margin is staged to recover survivors of the Ninefold Grace from Conamara Chaos, and I believe your dose questions were the planning for it. I have a vessel and a crew that can land. I don't have the delta-v to bring them home to Callisto. I believe I have it to bring them to your tanker. I'm asking whether you'll take them."

Then she waited.

Eleven seconds was long enough to count, and she counted. Teo stood behind her shoulder, not touching. Danso stopped chewing. Out in the corridor, someone who had been leaning on the wall stopped leaning.

Haddad's face, when the delay ended, had not changed at all. Ur Station had received her words five and a half seconds earlier. She was watching him hear them now, unhurried. His eyes moved once, to something off-screen. Then he spoke.

"Ms Varga. Ur Station acknowledges your communication and notes, with appreciation, your observance of open procedure. It notes also your second designation." A pause, not a delay. "Ur Station has, as a matter of humanitarian prudence, positioned certain assets in anticipation of a possible recovery operation. Ur Station has not undertaken that operation, because an Ur vehicle landing within the zone claimed by the Jovian Development Authority may be construed under Compact Article Nine as a hostile incursion." He stopped. Then, with no change of tone at all: "We have heard them for most of a year. Our listening post at Nicholson Regio caught a beacon on a retired maintenance band within months of the loss, long before the Margin was fuelled. We could not read the keying. We could resolve a bearing, Conamara, within forty kilometres. We could not resolve a site. We have been waiting for someone to tell us where to land without being shot for it."

Most of a year. The tanker, the war-risk cover, the questions: none of it had been in reaction to anything. Forty kilometres. Piq heard Teo breathe in behind her.

She keyed. "Your bearing is right. I have the site to within three hundred metres from four emissions and the Doppler track. I'll send the fix on this channel when we're done. Our vehicle is Authority-registered and is currently impounded under audit custody. If our vehicle lands, an Ur vehicle hasn't. If your tanker meets us above the torus, she meets us in open space on a Ganymede transfer. We need your tanker for the last leg. You need our registration for the first. And Lagrange Mutual is invoking salvage on the Ninefold Grace as of this call, so the recovery is underwritten. Whoever ends up paying for it, it won't be the people who built it."

Eleven seconds.

"That is an elegant construction," Haddad said. "It will not be read as elegant in every office." For the first time something moved in his face. It wasn't a smile. It was a man setting down a binder he'd been holding for a long time. "Ur Station accepts. The Steadfast Margin is coasting to her phasing node now. She will burn there tomorrow, D29, and hold station above the cuff of the torus until your ascent. She will be waiting before you arrive. Commander Kalnins has asked me to tell your pilot, verbatim, that she has kept the tanks full." The pause came again. "I am told he will understand."

Behind the shack wall, from the corridor where nobody had been invited, came a sound that could only be Lucky Mbeki trying and failing to be quiet. There was more in it than one thing.

Piq didn't turn. "Commissioner, one more item. On the record."

Eleven seconds of a man waiting.

"Lagrange Mutual sent me here because of an intercepted fragment," she said. "Seven words. *Throw before conjunction, no one will see it until it's done.* It reached us through a commercial intercept broker in Ceres. It was encoded in an Ur-standard codec. VB4, lot 2214-0417." She kept her voice level. "Did Ur Station send it?"

The delay, then the answer, and in all of it nothing moved on his face. That was how she knew he was telling the truth. A lie would have been allowed some expression.

"No," Haddad said. "Ur Station did not originate that text. No one in my chain would have sent anything to a broker in Ceres. If it had gone out under my authority, I would have signed it. I have not signed it." He let that sit. "If it travelled in an Ur codec, Ms Varga, then someone wished it to look as though it came from us. I would very much like to know who. So, I suspect, would you."

The skin along her arms had gone cold, and she couldn't account for it.

"I'll put it in the square," she said, and immediately wished she hadn't, because he couldn't know what that meant. Then she said, "Later. I'll look later."

"Later is a luxury," Haddad said. "Allow me to spend some of yours now. You understand what will follow. A recovery inside the claimed zone, a vehicle in Authority registry, an Ur tanker on the zone's margin: the Compact will answer it. Not this year. Earth is fifty light-minutes away and its ships are slower than its lawyers. But it will answer." He folded his hands. "When it does, it will want people who were there. I am noting in this log that you made this call, on this channel, at this hour, under both your names, and that you made it first. I am naming you witness. I am sorry. It is the only protection I can give you that will survive conjunction."

"Understood," she said, and found she meant it.

"Send me your fix," Haddad said. "And send the dose warden my thanks for tables by eight. They were here at seven fifty-one."

The link closed on the grey seal. Piq sent the fix. It was three numbers and an error ellipse, derived from four emissions and a Doppler track she had reduced by hand. She copied it into her ledger beside the closing equals sign, because she copied everything. Danso put the cereal box down very slowly, as if it might go off.

"Well," he said. "That's going to be a fun log for the tall one."

---

Lucky was in the corridor. He had his back against the wall and his face in a state she would have needed three columns to record. Ziva was in it, and the tanks full, and something else that had arrived on the same line eleven seconds earlier.

"Special Losses," he said.

"Yes."

"Lagrange." He said it the way he had said it the first night she'd watched the hangar: *Lagrange's water blankets cost me three hundred metres a second.* "The clause. The Grace. The denial. That's your shop."

"That's my shop," Piq said. "The clause was ours. The denial was ours. I recited it to your crew the second day I was here like it was scripture."

Behind him, Rafe and Achterberg had come up the corridor from the hangar, because whatever was said in Danso's shack was said to Saar, and nothing said to Saar stayed out of the hangar for long. Achterberg looked at her as if a slip he had squared to her ledger that morning had come back reweighed.

"You weighed our blocks," Rafe said. "For them."

"I weighed your blocks for the Director's signature," Piq said. "As of twelve minutes ago, every one of them is cargo on a recovery that an adjuster of record put under a Lagrange salvage notice, on a log Saar can't edit. If it works, you're not the people who stole two hundred and forty-four tonnes. You're a contractor Lagrange owes. I couldn't do that as a notary. I could only do it as what I am."

"And if it doesn't work?" Achterberg said.

"Then I'm the one who said it out loud."

Nobody spoke. Down the corridor the transfer line hummed on, indifferent. Lucky looked at the deck, then at the shack door, then at her.

"You could've said it in the hangar," he said. "Before. To us."

"Yes," Piq said. "I should have. I put you in the wrong column too."

He let out a breath that was mostly air and partly a laugh with nothing funny in it. Then he pushed off the wall.

"Ziva kept the tanks full," he said, to the corridor generally. "I'm flying." He walked back toward the hangar without waiting to see whether anyone would follow. After a moment Achterberg did, and then Rafe, and none of them said anything kind. Piq hadn't expected them to, and she logged the silence at its correct weight, which was heavy.

Teo hadn't moved from her shoulder.

"That cost you," Teo said.

"It cost what it cost." Piq found her pen was in her hand, which was a habit. "It bought more."

---

The crew argument took four minutes. Piq had budgeted twenty.

They had it standing around the Mule's main hatch, beside her seal, the little fused polymer square with her thumbprint and the date. Lucky said, "I'm flying," again, and nobody argued, because nobody could. He said it plainly, without the patter, which made it sound like a vow.

Teo said, "I'm going as medic," and that was argued.

"Your dose," Piq said.

"Is mine to calculate."

"You're the dose warden. You can't sign your own..."

"I can." Teo had the tablet out. On it was the medic cell, the field Piq had seen once before: a ceiling, and next to it, faint, a higher number in pencil. Teo erased the pencil with the side of her thumb and wrote a new one, higher still, in pen, with a timestamp. "There. Logged. Deputy Ilunga takes the office while I'm gone. She knows the tables and she's meaner than I am." She looked up. "There are four people in that ice. One of them's had a cough for six weeks that I can't diagnose by Morse code. One has a dose history I've rebuilt from pulse fragments, and it frightens me. You can land them without me. You can't keep them alive in the transfer without me. Write that down."

Piq wrote it down.

"And you," Teo said.

Piq had expected to have to argue for herself. She had the argument ready: the margin is sixty-eight metres a second, someone on board has to watch every kilogram, and nobody else on Callisto counts the way I count. She had it in order, gross to exceptions. She had a second argument underneath it, about whether the Brigade would want a Lagrange adjuster aboard at all, and she found she was not going to make that one.

"You're at a hundred and ninety-one millisieverts lifetime," Teo said. "Plus eleven point four microsieverts from Ring D. A surface round at Conamara inside a borated crawler will put you through point two before you're back up the ladder, and I'll be drawing your blood every week for the rest of your life." She paused. "You're going as mass master. You're the only one who can count it."

The hangar was very quiet.

"Are you grounding me or clearing me?" Piq said.

"I'm logging you," Teo said, and looked at her. It lasted perhaps two seconds, the length of the wedding delay. Something passed between them that didn't need a light-second to arrive. It settled the way a weight settles on a scale that has been swinging: not stopped, but done with the swinging. Piq didn't name it. She didn't have a column for it. For once she didn't go looking for one.

"Right," said Lucky, a little hoarse, not looking at Piq yet. "Right. Good. Three. Three's luckier than four, everybody knows that." He tapped the hull three times and then, after a moment, a fourth time, quietly, as if for someone not present.

---

Hesper was in the depot pump gallery when Piq found her at 1140. She had one arm through a manual bypass to the elbow and her forehead against a cold pipe. The gallery smelled of ozone and wet regolith. Frost had grown in feathers along the shadowed ribs overhead. Piq knew now exactly where it came from, and she couldn't look at it without seeing ninety-five point nine tonnes.

"Pump three's pulling slush," Hesper said without lifting her head. "I can hear it. If I take my hand off this, it'll cavitate inside a minute."

"Then don't take your hand off it," Piq said. "I'll come to you."

"Dov called down." Hesper still didn't lift her head. "Who you work for."

"Yes."

"I asked you what you were really weighing. Weeks ago." A breath against the pipe. "I suppose now I know."

Piq crouched and took the sewn cardboard book out of her own ledger: the depot-floor tally, every block in Hesper's square, stubborn hand, closing at 244.1. She held it where Hesper could see it.

"You promised," Hesper said.

"I promised to give it back. I'm giving it back." Piq slid it into the thigh pocket of Hesper's coverall, the only part of her that wasn't holding the station together. "Every number in it is in a certified ledger with the Director's signature, and as of this morning every one of them is cargo under a Lagrange salvage notice, invoked on a public log by the adjuster of record. Nobody can hang you for supplying a salvage the insurer has already said it will pay for. Keep it anyway. It's good work."

Hesper laughed into the pipe. It turned into something else partway through.

"Bring them up," she said. "Bring Dov up. He'll talk all the way there and he'll be useless at the bottom, and then he'll do something no one else could, so let him." She turned her head at last, cheek to the cold. "Tell Amaya I'm sorry about the margin."

"You can tell her yourself. Ganymede's eleven seconds away."

"Eleven seconds," Hesper said, "and a lifetime of ice." She shut her eyes. "Go. I've got the pumps."

---

At 1258 Saar shut the doors.

He didn't touch the Mule. He couldn't: it was under her seal, outside his jurisdiction, in neutral custody he had read twice. He didn't cut the feed again. Rook's line on the restore order had made the feed the notary's, and Saar had learned once that week what reading a statute twice felt like.

What he did was lock the hangar's outer pressure door on a security interlock for a structural inspection of the cradle approach. Then he put four officers on the cradle track to carry out the inspection. The tall one stood in front.

Piq read the order on the hangar terminal. It was perfect. It was pure procedure. It touched no asset; it only stood between the asset and the sky. It was the cruellest thing she had seen him do, because it was entirely legal and would take exactly long enough.

"Inspection estimated at six hours," Rafe read off. "Six hours from 1258..."

"Is 1858," Piq said. "The window opens at 1612 and closes at 1700. He's done the arithmetic." She had the ordinance open in her head. Schedule C, vehicles. Schedule D, ex-vehicles: the Mule was registered under D, and she could argue the lockdown's scope for an hour and lose. Annex 7, already spent. Nothing in the 14,206 pages let a notary open a door. Notaries certify things. They don't open things.

Teo had gone very still beside her. It wasn't the stillness of someone without an answer. It was the stillness of someone looking at an answer she didn't like.

"There's a code," Teo said.

"Which?"

"One I've never used for anything it didn't say." She was already walking.

Piq went after her. The outer pressure door was a slab of composite and regolith with a security plate glowing red. The officers on the far side were visible through the viewport, their suits pale in the light from the cradle track. Behind them the Sling ran out across the basin for forty kilometres, a line of coils under frost, with Jupiter low and banded at the end of it.

Teo set her palm on the override plate. The red stayed red. For a moment her hand just rested there, and Piq understood that she was not waiting for the plate.

"Dose warden override," Teo said to the door, in a voice Piq had last heard at intake on her first day. "Code Four. Source removal." The plate went amber. The officers on the far side turned.

Teo keyed the intercom.

"Officers on the cradle approach, this is the Dose Warden of Asgard Yard. The vessel in Hangar Four carries an operating fission reactor, drum warm, which I am now classifying as a mobile radiation source in an occupied structure under Dose Code Section Eleven. A source of that class at an occupied boundary shall be removed to the nearest unoccupied exclusion area. The cradle approach is that area." She didn't hurry. "Under Section Three I have authority to ground any person whose projected dose exceeds their allocation. You are standing forty metres from the source and inside the drum's line. I have your badges. Officer Hale, your projected exposure by the end of a six-hour inspection is zero point four one seven millisieverts above your quarterly allocation. Officer Nakashima's is zero point three nine eight. I'm grounding all four of you, effective now. Clear the approach."

Nobody moved for a long moment.

Then a new voice came on the channel from somewhere on the security net. It was dry and unhurried, and it smiled without needing to be seen.

"Warden Anand. Inventive."

"Chief Saar."

"Your numbers are correct. They always are." A pause, measured. "Your classification is not. A drum-warm reactor inside a shielded hangar is a contained source. You have run that hangar's survey every quarter for three years and signed every one. You know it, and as of this minute the log knows you know it." The smile stayed in his voice. "I'll clear the approach. Your order is lawful on its face, and I'm not going to have four officers stand in a dose field to argue a definition. But I am filing a dispute with the Director and with the Compact medical board: misuse of dose authority for operational ends. You'll answer it when you're back. If you're back." Another pause. "Safe travels to the auditor."

The channel closed. On the far side of the viewport the four officers turned and walked back along the track in long, flat, deliberate strides, every one of them visibly hating it. The plate turned green. The door began to open.

Teo took her hand off the override plate. It was shaking a little. She put it in her pocket so no one would see, and Piq saw.

Then Teo took it out again, opened her own log on the tablet, and wrote while the door was still grinding up its track. Piq read it upside down: *1306 D28. Code Four invoked, Hangar Four. Classification contestable. Purpose: to clear the cradle approach for launch. Dispute expected. T. Anand.*

"You logged the pretext," Piq said.

"It's my book." Teo didn't look up. "I've just bent a rule for the first time in twelve years. I'm not going to lie in the one place that's never lied to me as well." She closed the log. "He's right. That's the part I'll have to answer for."

"You quoted his dose to three decimal places."

"I always do," Teo said. "Those were true. The rest was a door." She said it like a reading, and Piq heard what it had cost her to say it.

At 1540 the Mule went out onto the cradle under its own sealed authority. Lucky was in the left seat. The voice of the custodian of record came through the depot speaker from somewhere below, with an arm still through a valve. The thirty-one blocks of the crust were fitted at last, Juno's dripping one among them. Tank C stood at a hundred percent. Piq had weighed the whole ship once more at the cradle load cells, because she would have been ashamed not to: gross, tare, contents, exceptions last. It came to within four kilograms of her ledger. Two of the four were the hollow comms unit she still hadn't let herself put down.

She went through the nav pack the same way, line by line against the manifest, because a file was cargo too. Ephemerides, relay tables, Europa control network, *EUR CONAMARA SECTOR SURVEY, rev. 2214.7c, issued JDA-SEC 0350 D28, checksum verified.* That was the pre-conjunction refresh JDA Security pushed to every hangar terminal before every blackout. Lucky had loaded it at 0412 with the rest of the standard pack. It was the only Conamara survey in the system. Every chart she had ever seen of that ground descended from the Authority's, so there was nothing aboard to set it against except the ground itself. She copied the revision and timestamp onto the ledger line under her own site fix, three hand-reduced numbers and an error ellipse. On approach she would lay one over the other, as she would on any chart, on any approach, for any client. Gross, tare, contents.

The cabin was the size of her quarters, and full of water.

Teo was strapped in across from her with the medical kit between her boots and the dosimeter turned face-out for once. Piq noticed that, and didn't know what to do with it.

"Thirty-seven days on the drum," Teo said into the quiet, to nobody, to herself. "Four hours of surface. Sixty-eight metres a second."

"Sixty-eight," Piq said.

"Cradle's live," said Hesper from below, tired and steady. "Coils are warm. Pumps are holding. The Sling is yours, Dov. It's a gentle throw by her standards. Don't insult her by enjoying it."

"I'll enjoy it a little," Lucky said, "privately." His hand went to the bulkhead. One, two, three. "Ziva. Margin. If you're listening."

Nine seconds later the answer came from a tanker still coasting toward the node where she would burn tomorrow. It was a flat, unreadable voice that had once said *I do* before it was asked. "Tanks are full."

"Throw on my mark," Hesper said. "Three. Two."

Lucky turned his head, just enough, and looked at Piq for the first time since the corridor. It wasn't forgiveness. It was a pilot checking that his mass master was strapped in. She nodded, and he turned back.

Piq looked once across the cabin. Teo was already looking back.

"Mark."

The Sling took them.

There was no roar, only weight arriving all at once from behind: more than three Callistos, then more than Earth, pressing Piq into a couch that had been built for slugs of ice and adapted for people in a single night. Through the frosted port the coils went past like the teeth of a comb, faster and faster, until they were one grey line. Jupiter didn't move. Jupiter never moved here. Everything else moved toward it.

The ledger lay in her lap under her palm, open at the equals sign. Below it were two lines she had not yet compared: her fix, and the Authority's chart of the ice they were going to land on. Behind them, on a public log, the name she had given up was already on its way to every office that would want it.

At 1612 D28 the Mule left Callisto.
