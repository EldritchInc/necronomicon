# Chapter 7: Echo

"Ice," Piq said.

Hesper Okafor-Lund didn't move. She stood in the Ring C east junction with her thermos tucked under one arm and her free palm flat on the bulkhead beside her, the way she touched hardware she had decided to trust. Piq understood that she was not being offered the same courtesy. The junction lights were still on their night setting, a dim amber that made the old woman's stubble of white hair look like frost on a cable tray. Somewhere above them a fan caught on a burr in its housing and ticked once each revolution.

"Ice," Hesper repeated.

"Your depot is short." Piq had decided on the walk down from her quarters that she would give up one true thing to keep the rest, and that it would have to be a heavy one. A light truth reads as a lie. "Not a little short. I've reconciled six quarters of intake, throw and reclamation, and the books come out light every time. Someone has restated the shortfall flat, six quarters running, under one office code. I'm the auditor of record. When this goes to the Authority it goes under my name, and I don't sign totals I can't foot."

"How short?"

Piq told her. She watched the number land and filed the reaction: no surprise, only a careful kind of attention, like a pilot hearing a figure she already knew read back by a stranger.

"Three hundred and forty," Hesper said.

"Give or take the reclamation losses, which are badly logged."

"And that's what you're weighing."

"That's what Ibis sent me to weigh."

"That isn't what I asked." Hesper unscrewed the thermos, considered the tea, and closed it again without drinking. "Ninety-one spoons in Canteen Two. I told you that at the Sling. You let it go by."

"You were wrong. It's two hundred and ninety-one."

"I know it's two hundred and ninety-one. I counted them myself before you landed, because I wanted to see how long it would take you." Her eyes stayed level. "A plain auditor corrects you on the spot. They can't help it. It itches. You let it ride, so I'd keep talking. You weren't checking spoons. You were checking me."

Piq made herself breathe out slowly enough that her collar badge would have no reason to log anything. *Read the number, then look at the thing,* her father used to say. The thing in front of her was a sixty-year-old engineer who had set a trap with a spoon count and waited a week to spring it.

"Everyone on a station like this is hiding mass," Piq said. "Accordions. Bricks. A cat. I check people because people are where the mass goes. That's the job."

"Then do it." Hesper pushed off the bulkhead, and the motion carried her half a meter up and forward before her boots found the deck again. "Count what you like. But there are things I'd put my body between you and, Auditor, and I am an old woman with a great deal of body." She stopped at the mouth of the corridor. "And there are things on this moon you won't hear with an auditor's ears. You can trust me on that or not."

She left. The fan ticked. Piq stayed where she was until her heart rate came down to a figure she could have signed for.

At the bottom of her tally pad she wrote: *H. knows 340. Did not ask who restated.* She looked at that second line for a long time. An innocent person would ask whose code it was. A guilty one would ask too, to sound innocent. Hesper had asked neither, and Piq had no column for a person who didn't need the answer.

---

When she got back, an invoice from Intake was waiting on her terminal under Fitch's signature block. It billed her 160.68 mk for 0.39 kg of ceremonial protein slab "consumed by unlogged animal" during the wedding, on the grounds that the officiant had exercised "procedural control of the venue." It closed with EVERY KILOGRAM IS SOMEONE'S.

She replied that half the cake had been on Ur, outside JDA intake jurisdiction, and that the animal was not hers. *The animal is nobody's, which is the whole problem with the animal.*

Ninety seconds later, faster than anything else on the station had ever answered her: *Then whose is it?*

*Pending,* she wrote, and closed the screen.

---

She had built the prediction the way she built everything, out of plain arithmetic.

The first gap had started at 1940 on Day 4, the second at 0742 on Day 9, the third at 1942 on Day 13. The first interval was a hundred and eight hours and two minutes, the second a hundred and eight hours flat. Each silence lasted thirty hours and ten minutes, to within a couple of minutes. Projected forward in pencil, the series gave her 0742 on Day 18, give or take two minutes. She drew a box around it.

Day 17 went to honest work: Galloway's tug blanket ledgers, a reweigh on a shipment of air-scrubber cartridges, and two hours on the hydroponic tithe. The tithe turned out to be a tax on lettuce that was owed in lettuce. In the margins she kept checking her box.

Her theory was that the gaps were Hesper's solo coil shifts. Three for three. Thirty hours alone in the Sling's north coil gallery, where an engineer with a hand key and a reason could transmit, or rather not transmit. Piq had been circling the obvious problem for days. A conspirator who went quiet exactly when she was alone and unobserved was behaving backwards. Piq had patched it with an off-shift relay: a second operator somewhere on the station who keyed only while Hesper was off the net, so that the silences were the relay standing down while Hesper took delivery in person, out of sight of any logger. It explained the coincidence, and it explained the silence. It also needed a second person, a hidden courier route and a reason for both. It had too many moving parts and still held together, which is what decent stories do just before they come apart.

The duty roster, which she read under her audit credential with only a slight bending of purpose, put Hesper on the coils again from 0700 on Day 18. Four for four, if it held.

Piq set the receiver to log on its own, ran the antenna lead up the light-pipe, and stuffed the towel back into the vent grille. Then she did one thing she hadn't done before. She wrote a ten-line watchdog that sat on the receiver's buffer and, if ten minutes passed with no pulse, sent a single entry to her pad's calendar over the station's maintenance mesh, disguised as an ordinary reminder. Anyone watching would see an auditor's to-do list. She slept about as well as people sleep before an exam.

---

At 0725 she went to Canteen Two for breakfast. She didn't want breakfast. She wanted to be in a public place with witnesses when the theory held or failed.

Hesper was there.

She was not in the north coil gallery. She sat at the long table under the ventilation return with a tray of reconstituted oats and the thermos, Juno from the pumphouse on her left and a young Sling technician on her right, six or seven others within arm's reach. She ate slowly, the way people in low gravity learn to eat, so the spoon carried the oats instead of throwing them. She saw Piq come in and lifted the spoon a centimeter in greeting. It was one of the two hundred and ninety-one.

The roster said 0700. Piq had read it herself. Either the roster was wrong, or Hesper was about to be very late, or the box on Piq's tally pad was about to be wrong in a way that would cost more than lettuce.

She took her tray to a table by the wall, where she could see both the canteen clock and Hesper, and sat with her pad face-down beside the oats.

At 0731 Marek Saar came in.

He moved through the room easily, nodding at people. The nods came back half a second late and slightly stiff. Everyone knew how to react to the security chief, and nobody had decided how to feel about him. He took a cup of something hot, looked around the room once in a way that seemed to measure it, and sat down across from Piq.

"Auditor."

"Marek."

"You're up early."

"Lettuce tithe. It's owed in kind. I want to watch the weighing."

"I've heard you take lettuce seriously." He let the smile work. His grey jacket was pressed the way spin-born people pressed things, as though gravity would be along to check. "I wanted to ask about your reference masses."

"They're in my quarters, wrapped in certificate paper. They don't like being handled."

"You told Intake they were for calibrating the depot scales. Have they been to the depot?"

"Not yet. The depot scales have bigger problems than calibration."

"I'd like to see them." He drank. "Facilities is bringing a densitometer cart through Ring C on Day 20 for the habitation shielding survey. It does a full sweep, walls, closets and personal storage, every twelve years whether we need it or not. I mentioned your masses to the tech running it. Certified E2 standards, here in Ring C. He said they'd make an excellent check source. Known density, known geometry. He'll sweep them first thing, to zero the cart."

The clock said 0738.

Piq thought about what a densitometer cart would make of 1.3 kilograms of certified reference mass that was mostly a hollow shell around a Lagrange transceiver. An E2 standard is solid stainless steel, 7,950 kilograms per cubic meter all the way through. Hers would light up the tech's screen like a skull with a missing tooth.

"First thing Day 20," she said.

"Oh-eight-hundred. Ring C east, starting at your corridor. He was very pleased. He doesn't often get real standards." Saar turned his cup a quarter turn on the table. "I told him the auditor would certainly want to help. You're so fond of procedure."

"I'll be there with them in my hands."

"Good." He looked past her, toward the long table. "Hesper's in early. She's usually on the coils by now."

"Is she," Piq said.

0742. Hesper scraped the last of the oats into a neat ridge and ate it. Juno said something and the technician laughed. Nobody in the room was keying anything. Piq's pad lay face-down by her tray and did nothing, which was what it was supposed to do for the next ten minutes if the gap had come on time, and what it was supposed to do forever if it hadn't.

Saar talked about the shielding survey and the way the regolith had settled over the north galleries in the last quake. She answered in full sentences. Some part of her was keeping track of what she said, and she trusted that part to be competent.

At 0752 the pad buzzed once against the table.

She turned it over as though it were nothing. *LETTUCE TITHE — WEIGH NOW.*

The receiver in her quarters had heard nothing for ten minutes. The gap had started on schedule, to the minute, with Hesper Okafor-Lund forty meters away, in a lit room with eight witnesses and the head of station security, holding a spoon.

"Duty calls," Piq said.

"Fifty hours," Saar said pleasantly, and lifted his cup to her as she stood. "I'll tell the tech you're looking forward to it."

She walked out of Canteen Two at a pace an auditor late for lettuce would plausibly use. In the corridor she wrote on her tally pad, under the box, *Day 20 0800 — densitometer — Ring C east — masses first,* and drew a second box around it. Then she drew a line through the first.

---

The theory didn't fail gently. It failed the way a rotten strut fails, all at once and along its whole length.

She sat on her bunk with the receiver log open and made herself take it apart in order, the way she would for a client who had been cheated by a model.

One. The gaps did not track Hesper. Hesper had been on the coils during the first three because the roster put her there, and the roster put her there because someone had asked for those shifts. Piq pulled the shift-request history. All three were Hesper's own requests, logged a day or two ahead. Today's was too, for 0700. She had swapped it at 0640 with a technician named Abara, citing "breakfast."

Two. The off-shift relay needed a second operator, and in eleven days of sweeps Piq had never found a second emitter on the station. She had explained the absence with a better hiding place. She was paid well to notice when a model explained missing evidence by assuming better concealment. She had not noticed it in her own.

Three. A cell does not hold a thirty-hour silence to within two minutes across four repetitions. People are late. People swap shifts for breakfast. This was a clock, and nobody on Asgard Yard kept a clock that ran on a hundred and eight hours.

Nobody on Asgard Yard.

She sat very still. Then she heard Teo in the viewing dome on Day 11, both of them pretending not to be aware of the other's shoulder, Jupiter sitting low and banded over the frost of Valhalla:

*Europa ducks behind the old man every four and a half days. The dosimeters sing differently when it does.*

Four and a half days was a hundred and eight hours.

Piq opened the station's navigation almanac. The Sling ran its throw solutions off it, so it carried full ephemerides for the Galilean moons, and an auditor reconciling throw records had every right to read it. She asked for Europa's occultations by Jupiter as seen from Asgard Yard, Days 1 through 20. The almanac returned a tidy column.

She put it beside her own column and began to foot them.

Gap one: silence from 1940 Day 4 to 0150 Day 6, midpoint 1045 Day 5. Occultation: Europa behind Jupiter's limb from 0854 to 1233 Day 5, midpoint 1043.

Two minutes apart.

Gap two: midpoint 2247 Day 9. Occultation midpoint 2246.

Gap three: midpoint 1047 Day 14. Occultation midpoint 1046.

Gap four had begun at 0742 that morning. By the series it would end at 1352 tomorrow, with a midpoint at 2247 tonight. The almanac put Europa's next occultation at 2245.

Four for four, to within the scatter of her own timestamps. There was no Hesper in it. The silences were centered on the minute Europa slid behind Jupiter as seen from this crater.

The occultation itself lasted three hours and thirty-nine minutes. The gaps lasted thirty hours and ten minutes, and she made herself find the difference rather than wave it away. Europa is tidally locked to Jupiter, so anything sitting on its surface turns once per orbit relative to the planet. Relative to the line toward Callisto it turns once per synodic period, a hundred and eight hours, three and a third degrees an hour. Thirty hours of silence meant a hundred degrees of sky the transmitter could not see. The gap was symmetric, the same number of hours on each side of the occultation. So the transmitter wasn't on a hill with a clear horizon. It sat looking up out of a slot, something with walls, a cut in the ice. Callisto rose into that slot, crossed it, and set again. Then the transmitter went dark, and Jupiter's body sat squarely in the middle of the dark.

She pulled up the frequency log next, the one she had shrugged at on Day 16 between the vows and the cake. The carrier on the old maintenance band, nominally 410.250 megahertz, had wandered by about eighteen kilohertz either side of center. She had written *crystal, cheap, thermal* beside it and moved on.

A cheap crystal drifts with temperature. It wanders, it jumps when someone opens a hatch, it settles a little lower every week as it ages. It does not trace a clean sine wave with a hundred-and-eight-hour period.

She fitted one anyway. The amplitude came out at 18.8 kilohertz. Europa's orbital speed is about 13.7 kilometers a second. Divided by the speed of light, that is a fractional shift of 4.58 parts in a hundred thousand, and at 410.25 megahertz it comes to 18,802 hertz. She subtracted Callisto's own motion, a residual of a few hundred hertz. The fit tightened.

The zero crossings fell where the geometry required. The carrier crossed center at the middle of each gap, when Europa was behind Jupiter and moving square across the line of sight. It ran low going into each silence, while the source was still receding around the back of the planet, and high coming out, while it approached on the far limb. Then it sagged back through center fifty-four hours later as Europa crossed in front.

That wasn't hardware drift. It was the source moving, the signal of something that rode on a moon.

She wrote it in one line, because one line was all it needed: *Transmitter on Europa. Surface or near. In a cut. Keying since at least Day 4.*

Then she wrote down what that line cost the rest of the ledger, because a correction you don't carry through is just another error.

Hesper had not been going quiet when she was alone. She had gone to be alone when it went quiet. There was nothing to listen to for those thirty hours, so the old woman took the dead shifts on the coils and spent the live ones somewhere with a receiver. *Things you won't hear with an auditor's ears.* The Brigade wasn't coordinating anything over that band. It was listening to it.

*Throw before conjunction, no one will see it until it's done.* Piq had read it the way Pell had read it, as a launch someone wanted hidden. Water ice going missing, a tug being wrapped in it, all of it timed against the blackout. Seen through a mass auditor's ledger, the ice around the Mule meant theft. Seen through a dose warden's, it would mean shielding. If someone on Europa was calling, the throw was not a weapon. It was a rescue, scheduled to fly while the Compact couldn't watch and couldn't forbid it.

And somewhere in the Mars relay sat Pell's order of Day 11, forty-nine minutes stale when it reached her and staler every hour since: *Identify the throw cabal. Treat the depot crew as hostile.*

Europa's trailing hemisphere took five sieverts a day. A person unshielded on that surface was dead within a day. Under meters of ice, a person might live. Anyone keying a hundred-year-old dosimetry protocol on a band nobody listened to was either a machine left running or someone who had worked out that the only people who might still be listening were the people who had once used it.

There was one person on Asgard Yard who would know which.

---

The dose office sat at the end of Ring A, behind a door with a radiation trefoil that someone had at some point carefully re-stenciled by hand. Teo was at her desk with a column of badge readings, her reading glasses pushed up into her dark hair, and she didn't look up when Piq knocked on the frame.

"Your next consult isn't until Day 21," she said. "Your dose is fine. Your dose is boring. Go and be boring somewhere else."

"I found something in the maintenance spectrum."

That got her attention, though she didn't show it with anything but her pen, which stopped.

"The auditor's sweep includes the RF registry," Piq said. It was true as far as it went. "Every licensed emitter on the station gets reconciled against its band allocation. There's a carrier on the old dosimetry maintenance band, the one retired in eighty-something. It shouldn't be anything. It's somebody's, and I can't find whose. I thought the dose warden might want to know what's using her band before I put it in a report."

Teo took off the glasses and set them on the column of readings. "Nothing uses that band. It was decommissioned before I got my ticket."

"Something does." Piq held out the pad. "Raw log. Eleven days."

For a moment Teo didn't take it. Piq could see her calculating, as she always did, the cost of picking something up. Then she held out her hand, and Piq put the pad in it without letting their fingers touch, the way you hand over a sample.

Teo scrolled. Her face did what a careful face does with unfamiliar data: it went flat and attentive. She paged through the pulse trains, the group lengths, the gaps. Piq had arranged the log so the Europa conclusion was not on the first screen. She told herself it was method, letting the data speak before the theory did. Some of it was cowardice. She didn't know how to tell a woman that her dead might be calling from an ice field, and she wanted the data to say it first.

"These are counts," Teo said slowly. "Grouped. It's badge-dump format, or it's built on it. Field number, then value. That's the old maintenance protocol. They used to key dosimeter readings out of the deep galleries by hand when the wired net went down." She scrolled. "No. That isn't standard. The fields are wrong. Nobody used thirty-six fields. The badges only had..."

She stopped.

Piq watched her stop. It was not dramatic. Teo's hand did not shake and her breath did not catch. Her thumb rested on the edge of the pad, and for the length of three full pulse groups she did not scroll at all.

"Teo," Piq said.

"Six by six," Teo said. Her voice was quite level. "Thirty-six cells. We drew it on the underside of the top bunk in marker, because I was studying for my warden's ticket and she was fourteen and bored and I needed something to do with her that wasn't her climbing the reactor ladder. I taught her badge-dump keying so I could quiz myself. Then she wanted letters, so I made her a grid. Row and column, keyed as counts. Tally fives as long pulses. So you'd only ever need four kinds of mark." She swallowed. "I told her it was a stupid code. Anyone with an afternoon could break it."

"Did anyone else ever use it?"

"No. We used it on the bunk wall. Knocking. After lights-out." Teo turned the pad so Piq could see, and touched the screen at the end of one group. "Look. There."

At the end of the group was a doubled final pulse: two short marks where one would close the frame.

"She does that," Teo said. "Every frame. A little tail. It wastes a pulse. It costs time on the band and power, and it carries no information, and I told her that a hundred times. She said it was her signature. She said a code should know who's talking." Her thumb moved down the screen, and at the end of every frame the same small redundant flourish sat there. "Every frame."

She set the pad on the desk very precisely, parallel to the edge, and then put both hands flat on either side of it, and Piq saw her press down, as if the desk were the only fixed thing in reach. Teo's face was not crumpling. It was just failing to hold its usual shape, in small places at the mouth and around the eyes, like ice taking load and showing it in hairline lines before anything gives.

"Where is this coming from," Teo said. It was not a question so much as an order.

Piq told her. She went through the occultations, the four midpoints within two minutes, the hundred degrees of sky, the eighteen-point-eight kilohertz. She said it all in the flattest voice she had, because she understood that what Teo needed from her now was arithmetic, a ledger she could stand on, and not comfort. Teo listened without moving. When Piq said *Europa*, Teo closed her eyes, and kept them closed until Piq said *in a cut, under ice*, and then opened them.

"The ruling said automated echo," Teo said. "Saar signed it. The pulses after the loss were an echo off the debris field. A beacon with nobody behind it." She looked at the doubled pulse. "Beacons don't do that."

"No."

"I need to log this." She reached for her own terminal, then stopped with her hand above it. "No. I can't log this." She laughed once, a sound with no humor in it at all. "I've logged every microsievert on this station for nine years and I don't know which form this goes on."

"It goes on mine for tonight," Piq said. "We decode it first. Then you decide what's true enough to write down."

Teo looked at her then, really looked, as if Piq had become a quantity she would have to measure later and could not yet. Piq held still under it. She thought about how much of what she had just said was true and how much was arranged. The arithmetic was all true. The reason she had been sweeping that band was not.

"All right," Teo said. "Sit down. Don't touch anything I haven't calibrated."

---

They worked through the night under the office's single task lamp, the door locked and the ventilation turned up to cover their voices. Teo had the grid. She drew it from memory on a sheet of dose-report flimsy, six rows by six columns, and her hand did not hesitate over a single cell. Piq had the timing.

The first problem was framing. The pulses arrived Doppler-stretched, so their spacing breathed in and out across the hundred-and-eight-hour cycle and the boundaries between groups would not sit still. Piq corrected every timestamp against the fitted velocity curve, then rebuilt the frame clock from the corrected series. After that the doubled tails lined up like fence posts, and they could see where every frame began and ended.

The second problem was noise. Jupiter is loud on most of the radio spectrum, and the log was full of crackle and false pulses where the planet's own bursts had stepped on the band. Teo knew the badge-dump format well enough to reject impossible values, counts no field could hold. Piq marked the surviving pulses by confidence. They took only frames that appeared at least three times, because whoever was keying repeated, again and again, the same short message, the way you would if you did not know whether anyone had heard the first thousand times.

Somewhere past 0200, Piq saw the second thing.

It sat underneath the pulses, not in them: a slow ripple in the carrier's amplitude, a fraction of a decibel, too faint to show unless she stacked a hundred frames and averaged. It had a period of about two point seven seconds, and the period was not steady. It stretched and shortened over hours with no relationship she could find to the pulse clock or to the Doppler curve.

"Teo."

Teo leaned in. Their shoulders were nearly touching. Neither of them moved closer or away.

"That isn't keying," Teo said after a while.

"No. It's under the keying. It's on the carrier itself."

"Interference."

"From what?" Piq ran it. Jupiter's decametric bursts are tied to Io's position, and Io was nowhere near the right phase on any of the days. Mechanical coupling from the reactor's coolant pumps was a candidate, but pump vibration would be steady or harmonic, and this was neither. Multipath off the walls of the cut would track the geometry, and this didn't. She tried it as a second channel on Teo's grid, row and column from the peaks. It gave nonsense. She tried it as pulse-position code, and that gave more nonsense. She tried the ratios between successive peaks, in case it was counting something. The ratios were not integers and did not repeat.

"Did she know any other scheme?" Piq asked. "Anything you might not have taught her?"

"She knew a dozen. She was an engineer." Teo looked at the trace for a long time. "She didn't put that there. Her hand is in the pulses. That isn't a hand."

Piq opened a new column in her pad and couldn't think what to call it. She left the header blank and logged the period, the depth and the drift. Then she put it aside, the way you set aside a discrepancy that is real and too small to chase tonight, and knew she would come back to it.

They went back to the pulses.

By 0400 they had letters. They came out of the grid slowly, cell by cell, Teo reading row and column aloud and Piq checking each against the repeat frames. Teo's voice stayed even, except once, when a frame resolved into the letter A and then the letter A again, and she stopped and said nothing for a while, and Piq waited.

There was a number in the second frame, keyed in badge-dump digits instead of letters because digits were cheaper. Piq checked it four times across four separate repeats. Teo checked it against the sixth. Neither of them said anything when it agreed.

At 0517, with the lamp hot and the flimsy covered edge to edge in Teo's grid and Piq's corrections, the frames came together into a message that had been going out from the ice every hour Europa could see Callisto, for at least fourteen days, to a band nobody used, in a code only one living person could read.

Teo wrote it out in block capitals at the bottom of the sheet. Then she put the pen down and laid her palm flat beside the words, and Piq read them over her shoulder.

FOUR ALIVE. REACTOR DRUM 60 DAYS. PLEASE.
