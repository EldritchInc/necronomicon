# Chapter 9: Point of Origin

The block was still in the lift at 1040.

Piq passed the Ring C east freight lift on her way down to L6 and slowed. She told herself it was the way any auditor slows for an open discrepancy. The lift's cage doors stood folded back. Juno's block sat inside on its pallet: 0.46 tonnes of hand-cut stockpile ice with a red tag wired to the strapping. The tag's signature box and time box were still empty. The JDA guard from 0911 was still there too, and he had found himself a job.

He was crouched at the pallet's edge holding a graduated sample cup under the low corner, where the meltwater collected. A tablet lay balanced on his knee. As she watched, a drop swelled on the ice, considered the 0.126 g for a while, and let go. It fell with the leisure of something that had nowhere to be and landed in the cup without a sound. The guard tapped the tablet.

"How many?" Piq asked.

He looked up. He was young. His collar badge said KOSKINEN, and his face said he had been told nothing useful in his life. "Two hundred and six," he said. "Millilitres, I mean. I'm logging by drop count and checking against the graduations every fifty."

"Why?"

"Directive says uncountersigned mass is held at point of origin." He nodded at the cup with real misery. "If it drips off the pallet, it's moving. If it's moving, I need a countersignature for it. I haven't got one for the block, so I definitely haven't got one for the drips." He paused. "I asked if I could put it back on the block. They said that's also a movement."

Piq looked at the cup, the ice, and the young man's hands. They were steady on the tablet and would have been more use doing almost anything else.

"Log the cup's tare," she said. "Separately. Before it gets wet. Otherwise you'll book the cup as water when someone finally signs."

His face cleared with gratitude. She went on down the tunnel before he could ask what she was doing at the bottom of Ring C on a Tuesday. Behind her she heard him start the drop count over.

---

The ozone smell from the bad seal met her at the L6 service door, and so did the noise. She had expected silence after this morning: the Brigade on upturned crates with nothing to cut and nowhere to put it.

Instead the saws were running.

They weren't cutting stockpile. Hesper had two of the big wire saws set up on the hangar floor itself, three metres off the Mule's port skid. The floor was Callisto: the ancient dirty ice-and-rock crust of the Valhalla basin, sintered under a skim of regolith concrete. Rafe and Abara were lifting out a block with a pry bar and a lot of swearing, and the block came up dark. It was brownish grey and streaked like old marble, with a rind of glittering frost where the cut face met the hangar air.

"Point of origin," Hesper said when the saw spun down. Her voice was perfectly flat. She pointed at the new hole in the floor, then at the Mule. "Eleven metres. I'm going to hold it so hard."

Piq crouched at the edge of the grid. The cut face gave off cold like a held breath. Fine dark sand was locked into the ice, with here and there a pebble the size of a fingernail.

"It's dirty."

"It's crust. Forty to sixty percent silicate by mass in this layer. We can't drink it and we can't burn it. You don't need to do either with a shielding block. You need to put mass between a body and the sky."

"It's worse mass," Piq said. She was reaching for her stylus before she knew it. "Per kilogram, water beats it. Hydrogen is what breaks up the heavy nuclei and slows the neutrons. Silicon and oxygen just shatter and throw secondaries at you. This stops more per metre because it's denser, so it fits on the hull. But to match a tonne of clean water you'll need more than a tonne of this. And every extra tonne is Lucky's delta-v." She looked up. "We'd be using it for one reason. It's the only mass on Callisto nobody has to countersign."

"Yes," Hesper said.

"And I don't know what it weighs. Your stockpile blocks are 0.46 because the stockpile is clean ice and you cut them to a half-cubic-metre template. This will run one point two five to one point five five grams per cc, depending on where the saw went. If Lucky straps forty of these on that hull, you could be off by three tonnes either side."

Hesper smiled for the first time that morning. It was a small smile, and she meant all of it. "I was hoping you'd say that. I don't have an auditor."

Piq felt the weigh-master's daughter in her straighten up and roll up its sleeves.

"I need a bucket of known volume," she said. "And something I can hang a block on."

---

By 1400 she had a gantry crane, a load cell borrowed from the Sling yard, a sample core from every ninth block, and a table on the back of a hangar requisition that she kept turning over to find more room. Each template block cut half a cubic metre. They came out between 0.62 and 0.81 tonnes, and the heaviest had been sawn next to a buried boulder. None of them was booked anywhere, because legally each one was the floor.

Lucky followed her from block to block with the frank interest of a man watching a magic trick he intended to learn.

"So it's a floor."

"It's a floor." She chalked 0.71 on a face and initialled it.

"Until I bolt it on."

"Then it's a floor attached to your tug. The directive governs movement. The floor hasn't moved. It's been reorganised."

He knocked three times on the block. "I'm putting that on my headstone."

"Do it after Teo's finished with you," Hesper said from the saw. "Marrow counts at fifteen hundred. All of you."

---

The blue door at the end of Ring A had a queue.

Eight Brigade members sat along the corridor wall on the floor. Achterberg was at the end of the line, a lanky man with a beard like steel wool, holding a paper cup of something orange that Teo had evidently pressed on him. He regarded it with suspicion.

"Depot spine's sweating again," he was telling Rafe. "Condensate on every conduit from Gate Four down. I put a towel over the junction box and it came back heavier than my boots."

"Scrubbers," said Rafe.

"Scrubbers don't make water. They take it." Achterberg sniffed the cup. "Something's putting it in the air."

Piq made a note of it out of pure habit, *humidity, depot spine, anecdotal*, and did not sit down. Ring A was the administrative ring. Prew walked it between Rook's office and the commissary, and Prew was Saar's adjutant, a soft-voiced man who logged faces the way Piq logged masses. An auditor sitting on the floor with the Brigade would appear in his evening summary as a line item.

Juno came out pale and dignified, holding gauze to her elbow. Teo's voice followed her. "Next. And if it's Varga, she's not on my list."

"She's on mine," Piq said, and went in.

The office was smaller than she had pictured it. That was a strange thing to notice. She had evidently been picturing it, and she logged the fact in a column without a heading. A centrifuge sat on a shelf, a rack of stoppered tubes beside it. A hand-lettered card over the counter read COUNTS ARE NOT GRADES. Teo wore her reading glasses and two layers of gloves.

Piq laid the table on the corner of the desk. "Hesper's cutting floor. It's denser than stock and worse per kilo. You'll want real numbers for your shielding geometry."

Teo stripped one pair of gloves and held the sheet close to her glasses. Her mouth did something complicated. "Point of origin."

"Point of origin."

"Saar will hate that." She tapped 0.81. "That one goes behind the pilot." She pulled out a chart from under a cup of tea gone to tar. It was an outline of the Mule's pressure hull with seats pencilled in and isodose lines curling across them in red, and she began to write Piq's numbers onto it in a hand much neater than her typing. "I'll need more of them than I'd need of stockpile. Maybe a third more."

"I know."

"Of course you know." She didn't say it unkindly. "Rafe's lymphocytes are at the bottom of normal. That's either Rafe, or six months next to that reactor collecting 2.223 MeV capture gammas that I logged as 'unattributed.' Everyone in that corridor has lied to me for five months. Nicely. And I let them. I'd see a neutron-capture line on a woman who works in hydroponics and write 'source unattributed' and feel clever about how precise it was." She looked up over the glasses. "Precise isn't the same as honest. You'd know that."

It landed somewhere under the sternum. Piq felt the 'any means' line in her notebook as though the notebook were warm.

"I'd know that," she said.

Teo called "Next." Rafe came in rolling his shoulder, and she found his vein on the first try.

"The other thing," Piq said, while the tube filled dark red. "I want to go up to the tank farm tomorrow. Tank Seven. It's been flagged unreconciled for fourteen months. The meter disagrees with the book, and farm rules bar any draw until an independent mass auditor verifies the contents in person."

"What's in it?"

Piq had looked it up at 1350 on a public terminal, between blocks. Then she had sat with the answer for a minute before she shut the screen.

"Twenty-six point eight tonnes of water. Booked as the return-leg reserve to the Ninefold Grace."

Teo's hands did not shake. She drew the needle, pressed gauze, folded Rafe's arm over it and said, "Hold that." Then she sat down in her own chair, which Piq had never seen her do with a person in the room.

"For when they came back," she said.

"The tug pool staged it before the Grace launched. Standard practice on a Europa run." Piq kept to the facts, because the facts were the gentlest thing she owned. "A reserve stays booked until the vessel is struck from the registry. Strike-off needs the hull claim closed, either paid or with the dispute ended."

Teo looked at her.

"Your appeal against the denial," Piq said. "It's still open. As long as it's open, the Grace isn't legally finished, and neither is her water."

She did not say the rest. Last night, in the lining of her own luggage, she had read Lagrange's claim file. The denial was built on four words, *deviated to low-energy trajectory*. On the asset schedule, Tank Seven sat under OFFSETS, waiting to be deducted from a settlement that would never be paid.

Teo took off her glasses and set them on the chart, across the pilot's seat.

"I filed that appeal so somebody would have to say it out loud," she said. "That they got it wrong. I didn't know I was keeping a tank full for her." She breathed out, a long and careful exhalation. "Fourteen months I've been paying Lagrange's dispute fees, and the whole time there's been a glass of water with her name on it, sitting in orbit."

Rafe had gone very still with his arm folded up.

"If I verify it," Piq said, "the operator can draw. Hesper's the operator."

"And Saar's directive?"

"Says *ice*. Tank Seven is pressurised liquid." She let a beat pass. "It's water until someone freezes it."

Teo laughed once. It surprised all three of them, and it wasn't quite a laugh. She put the glasses back on.

"You're not going outside the shell without a dose warden signing your exposure," she said. "Orbit is a whole different sky. I'm coming."

"You don't have to."

"I know what I have to do. Hold still, Rafe, you're leaking."

---

At 2310 the covert receiver woke against Piq's ribs.

Her quarters were a three-metre box with a fold-down desk, and she had stopped noticing the hum of the vent above it. Ballast had passed through the vent twice that evening. Piq could tell by the grit on the louvre. She unrolled the receiver and let it decode.

The pulse came in Amaya's keying. Piq could see it now as a hand and not a protocol: short marks clipped tight, long marks lingering as though Amaya resented letting go of them.

DOSE 38 MSV DAY. DRUM 55. RHEE COUGH BETTER. ODUYA SAYS TIDAL. ICE TALKS AT NIGHT. SLEEP OK. TELL T EAT.

Piq transcribed it twice and copied the line *tell T eat* onto a separate scrap. She would hand it over without comment.

Then she opened her Lagrange queue.

One message from Pell had come in on the evening batch, stamped as 51 minutes old at the relay and older than that in fact.

VARGA. THROW CABAL PRINCIPALS? ADVISE INTENT, MASS, LAUNCH DATE. ANY MEANS STANDS. PELL.

She read it the way she read every Pell message, which was as though it cost him a kilogram a word. Below it sat her own outbound column: the long report from four days ago, *four alive, reactor drum sixty days*, two follow-ups and an amended ledger. Each one carried the same small grey flag.

ACK pending.

The relay was batching. Everyone said so. The comms tech had said so in her second week, with the cheerful despair of a man who couldn't do anything about it. Pell never acknowledged anything until he had decided what to do about it, and he had always written like that. He had read *four alive* and replied with the question he had already drafted, because questions were what Pell sent.

She typed: *Re cabal: see my report of the 14th. No cabal. Repeat, survivors. Advise.* She marked it urgent. The urgency flag cost forty grams of her monthly bandwidth allowance, and she paid it.

It sat down in the queue beside the others under its own grey flag.

---

The hopper rose off the Valhalla pad at 0700 in a long slant of banded Jupiter-light. Callisto fell away as a pocked brown plate, and then as a curve. Teo sat across from her with her knees at her chest and a dosimeter on each wrist. She did not talk during the burn, and Piq liked her for it.

The tank farm was eleven cylinders strung along a truss in a 200-kilometre orbit, frosted on the shadow side, rotating slowly to keep their sun loads even. They cycled through the hub lock in suits, clipped to the truss rail, and went out into the full sky.

"Here's what's changed," Teo said over the suit channel. She had clipped her boots to the rail and let the rest of her swing outward, so that from Piq's angle she hung upside down against Jupiter. "On the surface, Callisto blocks half your sky. Half the galactic cosmic rays come up through the moon and never reach you. Up here you get the whole sphere. Your dose rate roughly doubles, plus whatever the old man throws when the field lines twitch. I'm giving you ninety minutes. At sixty I start nagging. At ninety I pull you in by your tether, and I've practised."

"You're upside down."

"Nothing's upside down. You're upside down. Gravity is an opinion up here, and I'm the one with the licence."

Tank Seven was the fourth cylinder: twelve metres long, with a pressure gauge on its end cap that read 21.4 tonnes. The book said 26.8. Somewhere, 5.4 tonnes of water was either missing or lying.

"You can't weigh anything up here," Teo said. "So how do you weigh it?"

"You push it and see how hard it argues." Piq unpacked the rig: a cold-gas thruster pack of calibrated impulse, a laser rangefinder and a reflector. "Known push, measured change in velocity, mass is the quotient. But the push isn't perfectly known. Thrusters drift. So first I calibrate against a reference mass." She pulled out the flat orange coil of a propellant bladder. "Five hundred kilos of water from the farm's metered line. The meter on the line is certified. The meter on Tank Seven is the one that's lying."

The fill took eleven minutes. The bladder swelled into a fat orange lozenge the size of a sofa, and Piq clipped it to the calibration bracket and fired the rig.

The bracket clip was rated for the load. It was not rated for the slosh. The water inside the bladder got a push a fraction of a second after the skin did, caught up and kept going. The clip pinged open with a sound Piq felt through her glove more than heard, and the bladder set off along the truss end over end, wobbling, its contents lagging behind each turn so the rotation stuttered and changed axis like a thing with intentions.

"That's half a tonne," Piq said. Her voice came out remarkably level. "Moving at about fifteen centimetres a second. It won't hurt anything slowly. It'll hurt it thoroughly."

"Where's it going?"

"Tank Eight's gauge cluster."

Teo unclipped her boots and pushed off, still upside down relative to everything, and drifted along the rail with her tether paying out behind her. "Keep talking. Which way is it going to flip?"

Piq watched the bladder. The water inside it was the problem and also the information. "It'll hang on the long axis, then dump to the short one. Every third turn, near enough. Don't grab the end. Get the middle when it's broadside."

"Broadside when?"

"Three. Two. Now."

Teo caught it across its belly the way a person catches a falling mattress. The water bulged around her arms, and for a second the bladder carried her, both of them turning slowly toward Tank Eight's gauges. Then Piq hit the tether winch, the line went taut and the whole assembly stopped and swung back toward her, with Teo spread across the orange skin like a woman hugging a cloud.

"Dose check," Teo said into the silence. "Thirty-one minutes. You're fine. I'm fine. That bladder is a disgrace."

"It was behaving physically."

"It was showing off."

They lashed it to the bracket with two straps, and on Piq's insistence a third, then ran the calibration. The thruster was 1.8 percent hot. Piq logged the correction, and then they went to push Tank Seven.

The first push read wrong, and so did the second. The tank moved, but the rangefinder showed a stutter. The velocity jumped, then settled, then jumped again.

"Something inside's not moving with the shell," Piq said. "The bladder in there isn't seated." She pulled up the schematic in her visor. "The ullage line. If the ullage valve's stuck, the gas side can't vent as the bladder shifts. The pressure gauge reads that trapped gas as missing water. It's been reading low for fourteen months because a valve froze."

The ullage valve was a hand wheel on the end cap, rimed white. Piq braced against the cap and turned. It didn't move. She turned again, and the only thing that moved was Piq, which in free fall meant she rotated gently around the valve stem until she was looking at Teo's boots.

"Equal and opposite," Teo observed.

"I'm aware."

"You need a fixed point." Teo clipped herself to the cap rail, set her boots against the tank, and reached around Piq to get both gloves on the far side of the wheel. Her visor bumped Piq's shoulder. "On three, you push and I pull. Same direction of turn, opposite arms. We cancel each other's reaction."

"That's a torque couple."

"It's a lecture. Shut up and count."

Piq counted. On three the wheel gave a quarter-turn with a crack that came up through the tank into both their bodies. A thin white plume of frozen gas sparkled out of the vent, and the end-cap gauge began to climb. It went through 22, 24 and 26 and settled.

They pushed the tank again. The rangefinder came back clean.

"Twenty-six point seven nine," Piq said. "Within ten kilograms of the book. Within the error of a calibration I'd sign." She signed it, in her visor and then physically on the farm's paper tag, because farm rules were old.

Teo was still braced on the end cap with her glove flat on the tank's frost. She didn't say anything for a while. The sky turned around them.

"Fifty-eight minutes," she said finally. "I'd like to start nagging."

"Go on."

"Come inside."

---

The inner lock at the Asgard shuttle port was a vertical tube two metres across. It had been built when the station's architects still believed people would be glad to stand close together. The repressurisation cycle took four minutes. They stood in it in their suit liners with their helmets under their arms and their hair flattened and steaming slightly, and neither of them pretended to read the panel.

"You counted the bladder's flips," Teo said. "In your head. While it was tumbling."

"I watched the water. The water told me."

"Most people would have panicked."

"Most people don't think in tonnes." Piq looked at her. "You went after it upside down."

"I keep telling you, there's no such thing." Teo's mouth twitched. "I don't trust you, Varga. I want that on the record. But you're the first person in a long time I'd want holding the other side of a valve."

The panel chimed at half pressure. The air thickened, and with it came the smell of the station, ozone and wet regolith and somebody's lunch. Piq could hear her own pulse. She could also hear, distantly, the auditor in her starting a column, and she declined to let it.

Teo's hand came up, slowly, and pushed a wet strand of hair off Piq's forehead. Her glove was off. Her fingers were cold from the suit.

Piq leaned in by perhaps four centimetres. Teo didn't lean back.

The duct grille above them rattled, dropped open on its hinge, and released 4.1 kilograms of tabby cat into the 0.126 g. Ballast fell between them with tremendous dignity and all four legs spread, taking almost a full second to cover the distance. He landed on Piq's helmet, which was still under her arm, and from there leaped to Teo's shoulder. He settled there and began to wash a paw.

The panel chimed full pressure.

"That," Teo said, without moving, "is undeclared."

"I'm aware of the cat."

"There's a cat on my shoulder in a pressure lock. It's been through the vent in an unfiltered supply line. Do you know what its dose history is?"

"He's never been on a manifest. He hasn't got a dose history."

"Then I'll have to start one." Teo lifted Ballast off her shoulder by the scruff and held him at arm's length. He accepted it like a man accepting a summons. Her eyes came back to Piq's, and whatever had been in the lock was still there. It had been noted and deferred. "Later," she said, and it was not clear which of them she meant.

The outer door opened onto a corridor with two port techs in it, and the moment was gone. Ballast squirmed free, sprang to a cable tray and walked off along it into the dark with his tail up.

---

The hangar at 2200 was cold and bright. The Mule wore its first course of crust blocks along the forward hull like a mottled cheek. Piq spread the ledger across the top of a saw housing. Hesper, Lucky, Teo and half the Brigade stood around it in a rough ring.

"Dry mass," she said, and wrote it as she spoke. "Mule as stripped, reactor and drum included, fifty-one tonnes. Teo's geometry for a crewed Europa landing and four survivors: thirty-one crust blocks, all weighed, twenty-one point eight tonnes. If it were clean stockpile ice it would be about sixteen."

"Clause fourteen would have made it eighteen," Lucky said. "Water blankets. Three hundred metres a second, every Europa job."

"This is worse mass than the clause's water, and more of it. Crew, consumables, medical: four point two. Four survivors and their suits on the way home: nought point six." She drew the line. "Propellant is the Brigade's stockpile through the electrolysis train, plus Tank Seven as of this morning, at nine hundred seconds specific impulse with the reactor hot."

She wrote the legs down the left side.

*Callisto surface to transfer: 3.32 km/s.*
*Europa arrival, capture, landing: 3.59.*
*Europa ascent and departure for Callisto: 3.59.*
*Callisto arrival and landing: 3.32.*
*Margin, gravity losses, steering: 0.70.*

Then she ran the rocket equation leg by leg. She subtracted propellant as it burned, added the survivors at Europa and carried the mass forward. Nobody spoke. The saws were cold, and somewhere a pump ticked.

The outbound legs closed and the landing closed. The ascent from Europa closed with the blocks still on, because Teo would not take them off and Piq would not ask her to. That left the last line.

*Available at Callisto arrival: 3.18 km/s.*
*Required: 3.32.*

"A hundred and forty," Piq said. "Short. With Tank Seven. With every block weighed."

She heard Hesper breathe out through her nose.

"Strip blocks on the way back," Rafe said.

"Then the survivors cross the Callisto approach behind three tonnes less shielding," Teo said. "They'll already be at the edge of what I can sign. No."

"Different reactor mode," Abara said.

"I've got it at max Isp already."

Lucky had his head tilted at the ledger as though it were a bet he could still talk his way out of. "Europa to Ganymede's a thousand cheaper than Europa to Callisto. Easy. Shallower well, closer orbit." He grinned, but there was nothing behind it. "Ganymede's the cheap seats. But Ur would shoot us."

A couple of people laughed out of habit. Piq didn't. She wrote *EUR to GAN?* in the margin small, because a cheaper option is always worth a line even when it's useless. Then she crossed it out, because an option is only an option if you survive arriving.

"So we find a hundred and forty," Hesper said. "We've got days."

"Fifty-five days on the drum," Teo said quietly. "As of last night."

The hangar's main door opened.

Saar came in without hurrying. Two JDA officers walked behind him. Koskinen was behind them carrying a grey evidence case, and he had the hunted look of someone who had been told to hold something and not told why. Saar wore his smile, the one that audited back. It took in the Mule, the grid in the floor, the chalked blocks and the ledger, and it stopped on Piq.

"Point of origin," he said pleasantly. "That's very good. I'll be reading the statute tonight." He did not look at the floor again. He looked at the rack beside the Mule's port skid, where the Brigade's receiver sat with its patch-cabled antenna feed snaking up through the ceiling toward the surface array. "Unlicensed reception equipment. Configured to a retired band, with no frequency allocation, on a JDA facility. Officer."

One of the officers unplugged it. The receiver's status light went out. There was a small and terrible quiet as eleven people watched a box go dark that had been the only voice coming out of Europa for them.

"You can't," Hesper said.

"I'm afraid I'm required to." Saar watched Koskinen fit the unit into the case and seal it, and his voice did not change at all. "Interference on maintenance bands is a safety matter. There's been a great deal of chatter on that one. Automated echoes, I'm told."

He nodded to the room as if he had attended a meeting, and left. Koskinen trailed after him with the case held out in front of him like a cake.

Nobody moved. Teo had gone white along the jaw. Hesper put both hands flat on the saw housing, on top of Piq's ledger, on top of the hundred and forty.

Piq stood with her arms folded over the lining of her jacket. Under her left forearm, sewn into the panel of the Lagrange comms unit she had declared on arrival as 1.3 kilograms of audit reference masses, the covert receiver lay warm and silent. It was the only ear left on Callisto that could hear Europa. Europa would clear the limb of Jupiter in forty-one minutes, and Amaya would key into a room that no longer had a box in it.

Teo's eyes found hers across the ring of faces. Piq understood, coldly and with complete arithmetical clarity, that within the next forty-one minutes she would have to decide what to tell her.
