# Chapter 14: Custodian of Record

The question hung in the comms shack long enough for Ballast to finish turning his back on both of them.

Ruairi Danso sat with his knees against the rack and the lentil pouch still in one hand. Piq had come to think of the look he wore as his relay face: polite and patient, a man used to answering questions that had arrived out of order.

"Who has to obey a seal," he said. "Everybody who wants their mail, apparently."

"Besides the mail."

"Courts. Inheritance clerks. Anyone selling a ship." He rubbed his nose with the back of his wrist. "The Authority, when it wants its own paperwork to stand up. You saw that this morning."

She had. Her seal sat at the bottom of a regret notice Saar had needed to be beyond contest. In the interrogation room the most dangerous man on Callisto had stopped short of breaking her terminal tape, because breaking it would have cracked the foundation under his own document. A seal was a small thing that larger things rested on.

"I need the Authority Asset Ordinance," she said. "The whole thing, with schedules. Not the residents' summary."

Danso already had a hand on his keyboard. "You know it's fourteen thousand pages."

"Then I'll need tea."

"The tea's in the potable allocation. You'd need a countersignature."

She looked at him. He looked back. Three hours ago Marek Saar had made every gram of moving ice on the station subject to his office, and nobody had yet decided whether a kettle counted as moving ice.

"I'll go without," Piq said.

---

The Ordinance did not run to fourteen thousand pages. It ran to fourteen thousand two hundred and six, because the Authority had amended its definition of *asset* nine times since Asgard was dug. Each amendment was appended rather than merged, so the history of the Jovian Development Authority's opinions about property lay in strata, like the regolith over the rings.

Piq read the way her father had taught her to weigh a hold. First the gross: index, schedules, definitions. Then the tare: everything that was only procedure, only the weight of the container. Then the contents. Exceptions last.

Danso watched for twenty minutes, fidgeted for ten, and then started narrating, which she gathered was how he fidgeted.

"Schedule C is vehicles. Schedule D is vehicles that used to be vehicles. Schedule E is the argument about whether a Sling slug is a vehicle while it's still on the rail."

"Is it?"

"Depends who's paying for the rail. There's a footnote. My predecessor said the Ordinance was written by a committee that believed every object in the universe was secretly trying to escape taxation."

"Your predecessor was right." Piq marked her place with a fingertip. "Here."

*APPENDIX F. ASSETS UNDER NOTARIAL IMPOUND.*

It was four pages long, which in this document counted as a haiku.

*F.1. A notary registered under the Correspondence Act and holding current audit standing may, on a finding of material discrepancy in the declared mass, provenance, or valuation of an Authority-registered asset, place that asset under impound pending certification.*

*F.2. An asset under impound shall not be moved, modified, disassembled, fueled, defueled, or otherwise altered except under the direction of the impounding notary or a custodian of record named by that notary.*

*F.3. Impound is effected by the physical application of the notary's seal to the asset's registration plate and to its primary access, and by entry in the Authority asset register.*

*F.4. Assets under impound are held in neutral custody and are outside the jurisdiction of operational offices (see Schedule of Operational Offices, Annex 7) pending certification or release.*

*F.5. The impounding notary shall, within the impound period, produce a reconciled certification of the asset's mass and provenance, including all materials incorporated into the asset since its last registered weighing. A seal applied to an unreconciled ledger is void ab initio, and the notary shall be liable under Section 40.*

*F.6. Release: on certification, to the registered owner or a custodian of record.*

She read it twice. Danso read it once over her shoulder, very slowly, and she could hear him working out the shape of it.

"You want to impound a ship."

"I want to impound a ship that is about to be scrapped for security reasons." She did not say which ship. He was a comms tech on a station of nineteen hundred people, and he knew. "F.2. Nothing can be done to it without me. Not defueled, not disassembled."

"Not launched."

"Not launched by anyone I haven't named."

Danso sat back. Ballast, on the exhaust grille, flicked one ear as if he too had a stake in the matter.

"Annex 7," Danso said. "Operational offices. Is security one?"

She had already gone looking. The cross-reference opened a page that said *Annex 7 is maintained separately by the Office of the Administrator and is incorporated by reference.* Below it was an empty frame where a document had once been linked. The Office of the Administrator had last touched the link six restated quarters ago, during the same period it had cut the depot sunshade budget to zero.

She drew a question mark in her notebook. It was the honest symbol: *I do not know this yet.* Beside it she wrote *F.4, Annex 7, Rook's office*.

"So you don't know whether the seal keeps Saar's people off it."

"I know it keeps everyone else off it. I know he can't defuel it without breaking F.2. Whether he has to obey it, or only should, is a different question."

"That's a big difference."

"It's the difference between a wall and a sign." She thought of Saar's ironed coveralls, and of the tape he had not broken. "He respects signs more than most people. He likes them to be his. If all I get is a sign, it still buys a day."

Danso was quiet. Then he said, carefully, "The nine messages. Your boss. He's the one paying for whatever this is?"

She had decided on the answer during the walk down to the shack, and she found she had decided correctly. "He's paying. He's not here, and he won't be for nineteen days."

"Right." Danso nodded several times. "I won't say anything."

"You'll say something," Piq said. "You'll say it at the canteen, to one person, quietly, because it's a good story and you watched it happen. I'm asking you to wait until it's over."

He had the grace to look caught. "How long?"

"The window opens at 1612 tomorrow. Twenty-three and a half hours."

"I can do twenty-three and a half hours," Danso said, the way a man says he can hold his breath.

---

The lockdown had already changed the station's sound.

Asgard ran on ice. It came up from the depot, through the melt loops and filters and down the potable lines, and the station heard it constantly without listening: the knock of a valve in the wall, the long sigh of a transfer pump cycling up two rings away. Walking Ring B's curve, Piq could hear the gaps where a pump sat idle because nobody would sign its order.

At Junction 12 a gardener from Gallery Two stood next to a four-litre ice bucket with a printed form in her hand. Piq recognised her as the lettuce woman at the same moment the gardener recognised Piq.

"Countersignature," the gardener said. "For irrigation. Lettuce doesn't care about conjunction."

"Lettuce doesn't care about anything."

"One point three seven kilos of it cared about you." The gardener lowered the form. "Forty minutes I've been waiting. One officer signing for the whole ring, and he reads every line. He asked me whether the bucket was going to Europa."

Piq had nothing useful to add, so she said, "He'll get to you," and kept walking. Behind her she heard the bucket set down, the patient scrape of an object that would wait as long as it had to.

---

Teo's door was open. It was always open. Piq had once read that as a dose warden's policy and now understood it as a statement of character: *anyone can come in, and I will be here, and I will have the number.*

Teo was at her terminal in her reading glasses, typing with two fingers, with a mug of tar-dark tea at her elbow that had no business existing under the new regime. She did not look up.

"Your Ring D timestamps."

Piq set the slip down: entry 0938, exit 1131, the interrogation room's minutes broken out. Teo slid it under her keyboard without reading it, which in her dialect meant she believed it.

"You haven't slept."

"I've had a busy relay."

The corner of Teo's mouth moved. She took the glasses off and looked at Piq the way she looked at a badge read that had come in a little high. "What is it?"

Piq had carried it for more than thirteen hours, through the blackout tones and Saar's announcement and fourteen thousand pages. She had kept it folded behind her sternum, where she kept things she had not yet decided how to book. She had told herself she was waiting for the right moment. That was a lie of omission told to herself, and she had a professional's contempt for those.

"There was a pulse at 0340," she said. "Before the dark. Amaya's mark on the end."

Teo went still. It was the stillness of someone holding a full cup in low gravity, knowing the slightest motion would send the contents out in a slow, silver, unrecoverable sheet.

Piq laid the tally sheet on the desk, with the raw pulses above and her decode below in square capitals.

DRUM 39. DOSE HOLDING. ICE LOUD TONIGHT. TELL HER.

Teo put two fingers on the last line, the way she touched a dosimeter face in bad light.

"She always did that," she said. Her voice was perfectly level. "When she was fourteen and I was nineteen and Mum was on nights at the clinic. She'd leave me a pulse on the old maintenance band, two walls apart. *Fed the plants. Done homework. Tell her I'm fine.*" A breath. "She'd have me pass it on, like I was the relay." She took her hand back. "You didn't bring this at 0340."

"No."

"Why?"

*The blackout. The Brigade. Being let out of an interrogation room.* Every one of those was true, and every one was tare.

"Because I didn't know which column to put you in," Piq said. "Yesterday you weren't speaking to me. I couldn't tell whether bringing you her words was something I had the right to do or something I'd be doing to get back into your good books. I couldn't tell them apart, so I did nothing. That was worse."

Teo looked at her for a long time.

"That," she said, "is the most honest thing you've said to me that you weren't paid to say."

It was not forgiveness, but it went in a column Piq could read.

"I want to answer her."

"Europa's clear tonight. The spectrum monitor logs every emission. Each time I key, I give him a bearing."

"Then it'll be short. She taught me short. I taught her short." Teo wrote in the margin in the six-by-six bunk-grid shorthand their mother's clinic had once used for badge codes: HEARD. COMING. HOLD DOSE.

"Coming," Piq said.

"I'm not going to key her a hedge." Teo set the pencil down. "Also, Haddad answered. An hour ago. I gave him the public surface tables last night, copied to Prew, so Saar has it too. Here's his follow-up."

Ganymede was not behind the sun. Ur's packets crossed the Jovian system in seconds and never touched Mars. Piq read the message, a tower of courtesies built around one question.

*The Commissioner would be grateful for the Dose Warden's professional estimate of cumulative exposure for a surface party of three persons, duration not exceeding four hours, in chaos terrain of the Conamara type, assuming crawler shielding of the standard Ur pattern (eleven centimetres borated composite), together with any guidance the Warden may offer on the sequencing of non-ambulatory persons.*

Conamara was where the Ninefold Grace had come down. *Non-ambulatory persons.* Piq filed both phrases under *Ur* and *threat*, because that was the column she already had open.

"He's planning a landing in the Authority's zone," she said.

"He's asking how not to kill people." Teo's mouth thinned. "I'll give him the tables. The sequencing I'm still thinking about."

"Non-walkers first," Piq said, before she meant to. "Heaviest dose last."

Teo looked at her sharply.

"It's your triage plan. I relayed it to them."

"Yes." Teo looked back at Haddad's message like a woman reading a dosimeter she did not trust. "Yes, you did."

---

The alcove behind the Ring C scrubber stack smelled of ozone and old filter media, warm from the electrolysis cells on the other side of the wall, where water became breath. Piq threaded the antenna lead out through the light-pipe coupler. Teo knelt beside her, a little too close, because the alcove was built for one person and a scrubber.

Piq held the key out. Teo looked at it.

"If I key it, she gets a message," Piq said. "If you key it, she gets you."

Teo keyed slowly, two-fingered even here, and ended on a doubled final pulse. Piq logged it: 2246, nineteen seconds. Somewhere a spectrum monitor was writing down that someone had transmitted from Ring C on an unlicensed band. That made three emissions from the alcove, and each one narrowed the cone.

"Varga," Teo said. It was the second time that day. "You're giving him bearings for me."

"For her. And for me." Piq coiled the lead. "It's all on the same ledger."

Their fingers did not touch when the key passed back. Piq noted the not-touching with the precision of someone who had been keeping a running total and was beginning to lose count.

---

At half past eleven on the first night of the lockdown, the hangar sounded like a church that had lost its funding.

The Mule sat in its cradle under the work lights, a fat ugly hull in a broken coat of hand-cut ice. Twelve blocks were on, and nineteen more stood in a ragged row off the port skid. The stockpile had wept and refrozen, and a translucent rind glazed the floor. Koskinen's cup sat under the drip from Juno's block, full to the brim.

Eleven people sat on crates and tool lockers and an upturned bucket. Teo stood by the reactor bay with her badge face-out. Hesper sat on the edge of the cradle, ankles crossed, waiting for a test result she already knew.

Lucky was standing. He did not appear capable of anything else.

"They put a man at the door. An actual man. He asked me what was in my supper."

"What was in your supper?" asked Rafe.

"Ice! It was frozen! It's a *frozen burrito*!" Lucky rapped the hull three times. "He wants a countersignature for a burrito. This is how civilisations end."

"Lucky," Hesper said.

"I'm setting the mood."

"The mood is set." Hesper looked at Piq. "Numbers."

"Two things first," Piq said. "You'll want to throw me out after the first. Hear the second before you do."

Koskinen, who leaked everything to everyone, put a hand over his own mouth in a gesture of preemptive restraint that Piq estimated would last until breakfast.

"I hold the Ibis credential, and it's real. Everything I've weighed for you is weighed true. But I came here for Lagrange Mutual, Special Losses. I was sent to find out what 'throw before conjunction' meant. My handler thought it meant a weapon. I thought it meant you."

The silence had mass. It settled on the hangar floor like dust in 0.126 g, slow and thorough.

"Lagrange," Abara said. "The ones who denied the claim."

"Yes."

Achterberg stood up from his crate. He didn't come toward her. He stood as if sitting had become a statement he no longer wanted to make. "You were in here counting our blocks. And sending it to them."

"Yes. For a while they didn't get it. My reports sat in a relay queue at Mars. My handler spent eleven days answering a version of me who still thought you were building a bomb. Last night he got all of it: the pulses, the drum, the four names." She set her slate on a crate where everyone could see it. "Before the dark came down, he invoked Article 31, the salvage clause. Recovery of insured crew counts as salvage of a covered asset, and Lagrange pays. I have funding authority. It's real money, and it's on my seal."

Lucky had stopped moving. That told Piq more about the room than anything she could have measured.

"So the company that killed them," Rafe said slowly, "is paying to bring them back."

"That's the second thing," Teo said from the wall.

Every head turned. Teo did not speak in Brigade meetings. She stood in them like a load-bearing column, and people checked their badges when she was in the room.

"Clause 14(c) locked eighteen tonnes of the Grace's water out of her engine," she said. "That forced her onto the slow road through the torus. The torus froze her flight computer. Rhee flew her down by hand. Lagrange calls that pilot error. Saar calls the antenna in the ice an automated echo. I've checked it three times."

Lucky said, very quietly for Lucky, "Three hundred metres a second."

Piq looked at him.

"Every Europa job, Lagrange's blankets cost me three hundred metres a second. I said it in front of you." He looked at the Mule as if it had betrayed him personally. "I thought it was a joke. It's the joke. It's the whole joke."

On the cradle, Hesper had not moved. Then she said, in her ordinary engineer's voice, "I did the retrofit."

Nobody else in the room had known. Piq could see it land on them one by one.

"Nineteen days before departure. Lagrange stamp on the work order. I put the shielding in, I balanced the mass, I signed it. I trained Rhee. I trained Amaya. I did the margin myself."

"You did the margin right," Piq said. "You booked the water as reserve, the way our template told you. Somebody at L4 changed the clause and not the template. That error is ours. I recited that clause in Bay Three like scripture, so it's on my ledger too."

Hesper's eyes came up, flat and dangerous. Piq saw that being absolved had not helped. It had only cleared the ground for something worse.

"Then here's mine," Hesper said. "I put them on that road. So I'm flying her."

"I'm the pilot," Lucky said.

"You're the pilot. I'm going. You'll need someone who knows that reactor with her eyes shut, and I'm not sending anyone down there to clean up my arithmetic." She stood. "That's settled. The other thing isn't." She turned to the room. "You can throw her out. She'll go. She won't report you, and there's nobody to report to for nineteen days anyway. Or you can keep the only person here with funding authority, a notary's seal and the only working ear to Europa, while four people sit in the ice with about thirty-eight days of drum."

"Thirty-eight as of tomorrow's count," Piq said automatically. "Thirty-nine at 0340."

"See," Hesper said to the room. "Can count."

The silence came back, distributed differently.

Koskinen took his hand off his mouth. "I'm going to have to tell someone about this eventually."

"After," said six people at once.

Lucky sat down on the bucket as if his legs had filed a separate opinion. "Okay. We have money now. What do you buy with money? Do we buy delta-v?"

"You can't buy delta-v," Piq said. "You can only buy mass, and spend it."

"That's the saddest thing I've ever heard."

"Here's a sadder one." She opened the ledger. "The Mule dry, fifty-one tonnes. Crust, about 21.8 when the last block's on. Crew and consumables 4.2. Survivors 2.4 with Teo's medical margin. Isp nine hundred seconds. Callisto to Europa, land, lift, back to Callisto: 3.32, 3.59, 3.59, 3.32. With them aboard, we're a hundred and forty metres per second short on the return."

"Less ice?" Juno said.

"Then you're under 14(c), and Teo grounds you."

"14(c) set the wrong number," Teo said. "The torus doesn't care what the clause says. Under eleven centimetres of composite and twenty-one tonnes of ice, a surface party takes a survivable dose. Under twelve, it doesn't. I won't put a crew into the torus with less water than physics wants."

"Then we need a different road," Hesper said.

Piq thought of a line she had written in her ledger a week ago and crossed out: *EUR to GAN?* She thought of an Ur tanker loitering with far more propellant than any routine run needed. She had those entries in separate columns, each labelled *threat*.

"I don't know yet," she said. "There's a question mark."

"She uses question marks," Teo told the room. "It means she'll find out."

---

The meeting broke the way Brigade meetings broke: people drifted back to blocks and pumps as if work were the only adequate response to anything. Lucky went to the cockpit to talk to the instruments, which Piq gathered was his version of prayer.

Hesper caught her by the reactor bay. She pulled a battered paper ledger from her coveralls, the sewn kind with a cardboard cover, a tally book from the depot floor.

"Every block," Hesper said. "Every haul, every kilogram we took off the depot since the first pulse. I wrote it down so that when they hanged us they'd hang us for the right number."

The running total ran down the right margin in small, engineering-neat figures and closed on the last page with a double line.

*244.1 t.*

Piq read it three times.

"The gap in the depot ledger is three hundred and forty."

"I know what the gap is. We didn't take the rest. I've been in that depot every shift for six years. Nobody moves ninety-six tonnes past me with a forklift and a prayer. Nobody took it."

Ninety-six tonnes. The number arrived in Piq and sat down. It was specific and real and unaccounted for, the kind of figure that stopped an audit dead.

She thought of F.5. *A seal applied to an unreconciled ledger is void ab initio.* The Mule was built of depot ice. To certify the Mule, she had to reconcile the depot, and the depot had a ninety-six-tonne hole in it that no one had dug.

"I'll give it back," she said, and tucked Hesper's ledger inside her own.

"Don't hang us with it."

---

At 0118 Piq sat on her bunk with the two ledgers open side by side.

*Depot gap: 340 t.*
*Brigade (Okafor-Lund): 244.1 t.*
*Unaccounted: 95.9 t.*

She drew a square beside it: *later*. Then she rubbed it out with her thumb, because later had run out.

Things she had filed began to come back like slugs arriving on trajectories she had thrown long ago.

The first was the frost she had seen through the shuttle port on the way down, glittering on the sunward face of the depot where no frost should be.

The second was a budget line in Rook's restated quarters, depot sunshade maintenance, cut to zero in the year the colony was repriced.

The third was the residents complaining about humidity, on a station that electrolysed every drop it could find.

She laid the three side by side, and they stopped being three.

The shade had failed. With nothing between the sun and the sunward face, that ice was warmer than any ice on the depot had been designed to be. At Callisto's pressures warm ice does not melt; it goes straight to vapour. The loss was a thin, steady sublimation over a face the size of four Sling cradles, running for six quarters. The vapour did not go far. It refroze wherever it met something colder: the broken shade struts, the lee of every rib, the shadowed seams. That was the frost she had seen, ice robbed from the sunlit face and redeposited where the sun couldn't reach. Whatever didn't refreeze was drawn into the depot-side intakes and pushed into the station's air as damp.

Nobody had taken ninety-six tonnes. The sun had, one molecule at a time, and someone had seen the dashboard and said nothing.

She wrote *sublimation, shade = 0* and drew a line to *95.9 t*. Beside it, very small, she wrote *Rook*.

The PA chimed with the three ascending security tones, and Saar's office spoke, giving the clock time before every sentence like a man initialling each line of a contract.

*0131. Notice. Under Authority emergency statute, the Office of Security will inspect Hangar Four at 0600. The inspection will include reactor and propellant systems of any vessel present. Personnel without inspection duties will vacate the hangar by 0545. Obstruction will be treated as hostile action against Authority infrastructure. Notice ends.*

Four hours and twenty-nine minutes to the inspection. The window opened at 1612, ten hours after that. *Propellant systems.*

She put both ledgers in her breast pocket, clipped her badge face-out, and went to find out whether Ilse Rook was the kind of woman who slept.

---

She was not. The light under the Administrator's door on Ring A was the colour of a terminal, and when Piq knocked, Rook said "Come," as though she had been waiting for someone, though not this person.

Rook sat in a cardigan with a cup of something gone cold. On her screen was the depot schedule. Piq had seen her glance at it in every meeting since the first, like a woman checking a mole she had promised herself she would have looked at.

"Auditor. It's half past one."

"The sunshade," Piq said, and sat down without being asked.

Rook did not pretend not to understand. That was the first thing Piq booked in her favour.

Piq laid it out: the frost, the zero line, the humidity logs she had pulled off Danso's board on the way up. Then Hesper's ledger, open to the double line, with her thumb over the name. "Three hundred and forty missing. Two hundred forty-four point one accounted for, by people I won't name to you tonight. Ninety-five point nine unaccounted, which is what an unshaded face of that area loses to sublimation over six quarters. You knew."

Rook turned her cup a quarter turn on its saucer. "I knew the shade was failing. I didn't know the number."

"You didn't look for the number."

"No." The warmth in Rook's face had not gone, but it had nothing behind it now. "We were being repriced. A disclosed depot loss on top of that is a rate class. A rate class is forty families on the next shuttle out because the colony can't carry them. I thought I'd fix it in a quiet quarter."

"There hasn't been a quiet quarter in six."

"No," Rook said again.

"I'm going to tell you what I am," Piq said, "because you'll find out by lunch. I work for Lagrange Mutual. Special Losses."

Rook went grey around the mouth.

"So here's the arithmetic. If I find a concealed loss, it's misrepresentation, and misrepresentation voids your cover. Every ship and slug and person on this station uninsured on the first day the relay comes back. If you disclose it to me tonight, as a notary, it's late notification. Late notification is a premium adjustment. The rate goes up either way. The ice is gone either way. The only thing you still get to choose is which of those two it's called."

Rook looked at the depot schedule for a long time, the way someone looks at a thing they have been looking away from.

"What do you want?"

"Your signature on the sublimation finding, so the depot reconciles. And Annex 7."

Rook frowned. "The operational offices schedule? That's my predecessor's. I don't think it exists anymore."

"It's incorporated by reference. If it doesn't exist, that's another finding."

Rook stood, went to a cabinet that had not been opened in some time, and began going through physical folders. Paper had mass, and on Callisto people kept it only when they wanted something to outlast a database purge. Piq watched her and did not offer to help. Some things a person has to find herself.

At 0412 they had the finding drafted. At 0447 Rook signed it. Her hand was steady. Piq countersigned, and the depot ledger closed for the first time in six quarters: 244.1 to unauthorised transfer into an Authority-registered asset, 95.9 to sublimation, 340.0 total. The ice had never left Authority property. It had only changed which Authority object it was part of. Piq wrote that sentence very plainly, because someone was going to read it under oath.

Rook was still in the cabinet when Piq left.

---

At 0545 the Brigade filed out of Hangar Four past a security officer with a slate. At 0600 Saar's inspection team filed in: four people in pressed coveralls with a cutting rig on a trolley.

Piq went in at 0604 on her notary standing, which no one had yet thought to suspend. The officer at the door checked his slate, failed to find her on it, and failed to find a reason to stop her.

They did not inspect the reactor. They went straight to the propellant manifold where the depot feed came through the hangar wall, a braided line as thick as a thigh, frosted where it ran cold. One of them clamped it on both sides of the coupling. Another fitted the cutting collar.

"Inspection finding," the team lead said to his recorder. "0618. Propellant feed to unregistered fueling operation presents a hazard to Authority infrastructure. Isolating."

The collar sang. The line parted with a soft cough of vapour that hung in the low gravity like breath on a cold morning and took nearly a minute to settle. The Mule's tank gauges stopped climbing. Piq watched the needle on tank C sit at sixty-one percent and stay there.

She walked to the Mule's registration plate on the forward skid and pressed her seal to it. Wax would have been theatre. The seal was a thumb-sized polymer stamp that took her print and the date and fused to the metal with a sound like a kiss. She climbed the cradle ladder to the main hatch and did it again. Then she entered the impound in the Authority asset register from her slate, citing F.1, *material discrepancy in provenance*, and attached the reconciled certification signed at 0447.

"What is that," said the team lead.

"Appendix F," Piq said. "This vessel is under notarial impound. Under F.2 it may not be modified, defueled or disassembled except under my direction. I'd ask you not to touch it."

He called Saar.

She had assumed it would end there, with a sign and a man who respected signs, and a day bought before the emergency statute rolled over it. She was counting on the day. Saar would invoke the statute. It would take him hours of paper. Hours were what she had.

Saar arrived at 0631, unhurried, ironed. He held out his hand for her slate as though it were already his, and she gave it to him.

He read Appendix F. Then he read it again from the top. Piq watched his eyes stop on F.4 the second time and stay there.

"Annex 7," he said, "is not linked."

"It is now," said Ilse Rook from the hangar door.

She had changed out of the cardigan. She was carrying a paper folder with a coffee ring on it and a digital copy on her own slate, and she handed him the slate. "Schedule of Operational Offices, current as of the third restatement. I've restored the link to the register. Item four."

Piq had not seen item four. She read it over Saar's arm.

*4. Office of Security.*

The Authority's own Ordinance placed an impounded asset outside the jurisdiction of operational offices, and the Authority's own schedule named Security as one. The emergency statute ran through Saar's office. Saar's office could not reach the Mule.

She had meant to put up a sign, and it had turned out to be a wall. She booked it honestly: it had worked for the wrong reason.

Saar read Annex 7 twice as well. When he looked up, his face showed nothing she could audit.

"Release on certification," he said.

"Certification is attached. Reconciled at 0447 and countersigned by the Administrator."

"Then the asset is released to its owner, which is the Authority."

"F.6. On certification, to the registered owner *or a custodian of record*. The impounding notary names the custodian." She took her slate back. "I haven't released it yet."

He looked at Rook. "You signed a sublimation finding at five in the morning."

"At a quarter to five," Rook said. "I'm precise."

For a long moment the only sound in the hangar was the drip into Koskinen's cup.

"You understand," Saar said to Piq, at the pitch he had used in the interrogation room, "that a seal is a promise. When you break it, and you will, because there are four corpses on Europa and you can't make them anything else, I'll be the one reading the Section 40 liability to you. Slowly. Both times."

He handed the team lead his own slate, said "Stand down," and left. His team took the cutting rig with them, and the severed feed hung from the wall, frosting.

---

"Restore it," Rook said to the depot ops board on her slate. Her voice was the one she used to approve canteen menus. "Feed to Hangar Four. Authorising under audit custody, at the direction of the impounding notary." She glanced at Piq. "That's the correct phrase?"

"It is now," Piq said.

The Brigade came back in through the door in a slow wave, like water finding a level. Hesper went straight to the cut line and put her palm flat against the frosted braid. Piq watched her read it through her skin.

"They can patch the coupling in an hour," Hesper said. "That's not the problem. The problem's the other end." She turned. "Your finding. The sunward face."

"Sublimating since the shade went."

"Then it's soft. Every transfer pump on that side is pulling slush and vapour instead of clean ice, and it's been fighting that for six quarters. The automatics keep tripping. I've been resetting them by hand every shift and calling it a firmware fault." She laughed once, a short dry sound with no humour in it. "I've been holding the depot together with my thumbs and didn't know why."

"With the lockdown," Rook said, "the automatics are on countersign. Security still has the depot, even if it doesn't have the ship."

"So someone feeds the Mule by hand from the depot end," Piq said. "Eight hours of manual transfer off a rotten face, and then the depot to keep from cavitating for nineteen days after."

Nobody said a name. Everyone looked at Hesper.

Hesper looked at the Mule: the ugly crust, the hatch with Piq's seal on it, the cockpit where Lucky was not yet talking to anything. Piq watched the guilt she had confessed seven hours ago arrive at the same place as the arithmetic and find no room left in the column.

"I put them on the wrong road," Hesper said. "I was going to go and get them, so it would be me paying. That's not a margin. That's a debt with my name on it." She took her hand off the feed line. "If I'm on that tug, the depot cavitates by Thursday and you've no propellant to come home to, no water for the station, nothing. I'm the only one who knows those pumps." Her mouth worked. "I trained Amaya. I don't get to be the reason she has nowhere to land."

"Hesper," Lucky said from the hatch.

"Fly my ship, Dov." She was already walking toward the depot access. "Bring them back. I'll hold the pumps."

Piq wrote in the register under *Custodians of record*: *H. Okafor-Lund (depot transfer); D. Mbeki (vessel)*. She initialled it and watched the entry take.

The feed coupling came back at 0709. Down the line, faint and far off, she heard a depot pump cycle up under human hands. Tank C's needle shivered and began to climb.

She opened the ledger. Fuel or no fuel, seal or no seal, the column had not moved. Callisto to Europa, land, lift, back to Callisto: 3.32, 3.59, 3.59, 3.32. Four survivors at 0.6 tonnes each. Twenty-one point eight tonnes of crust that Teo would not let them shed.

The clock on her slate read 0712. The window opened at 1612.

Nine hours, and the Mule was still a hundred and forty metres per second short of home.
