# Chapter 1: Weigh-In

The shuttle *Prudent Interval* came down into the Valhalla basin the way a coin settles through water: slowly, rocking, with the strong suspicion that it had stopped trying.

Piq Varga had the window seat because nobody else wanted it. The other eleven passengers were contract labor rotating in from the Ganymede transfer. They had seen Callisto before and slept with their mouths open, heads lolling against the padded rests in the half-gravity of deceleration. Piq kept her forehead a centimeter from the polycarbonate and counted.

The basin's rings came first, three hundred kilometers of them, ridge after concentric ridge. They looked like the ripple of a stone dropped into a pond four billion years ago and frozen before the water could settle. Everything was dark, a dirty gray-brown so old that the craters had craters. Above the rim, low in the west, Jupiter hung where it always hung, because Callisto had stopped turning its face long before anyone came to look. The planet was a fat, banded lamp the size of her outstretched palm. Its cream-and-rust light lay over the regolith in long smears and turned every frost-dusted crater wall the color of weak tea.

She had seen pictures, and the pictures had not mentioned the stillness. On a cycler there was always motion: the slow wheel of the spin section, stars marching past the ports every ninety seconds. Here the sky simply stayed where it was put.

"Asgard approach, *Prudent Interval*, thirty kilometers, on profile." The pilot's voice was bored and Martian-accented. "Hold onto your teeth."

The station resolved out of the dark as a scatter of geometry on the inner ring plain. There were landing pads, a cluster of radiator fins, and the dull glint of the regolith berms. Under those berms nineteen hundred people lived like ticks under a hide. To the north the Sling ran out toward the horizon, a single ruled line forty kilometers long, so straight it looked drawn with a blade. Its far end was lost in the curve of the moon.

Then the depot came into view.

It sat east of the pads, a squat terraced mound of stored ice blocks, each the size of a shuttle cabin. Over its sunward face stretched a gauzy canopy, the sunshade, hung from a lattice of masts. The sun was a hard white point, small and far, and it had climbed perhaps a third of the way up the sky in its sixteen-day crawl. Its light came in at a low angle across the depot's eastern terraces, under the edge of the shade.

The eastern terraces were frosted.

Piq looked at them for a long four seconds. Sunlit ice lost its frost; it did not grow it. A white rime on the sunward face meant water was coming off the depot somewhere, in quantity, and landing where the light should have stripped it away.

Or there was a perfectly good explanation in a maintenance log she hadn't read yet.

She wrote it down anyway, on the tally pad clipped to her knee, in the shorthand her father had taught her before she could reliably spell: *Depot E face, frost, sunward. ?* The question mark was the important part. Gustav Varga had been weigh-master on the cycler *Long Patience* for thirty-one years, and he had held that the question mark was the most honest symbol in mathematics. *Everybody wants to write the equals sign,* he used to say. *The equals sign is where you stop looking.*

The shuttle kissed the pad. In 0.126 g the kiss lasted a long time.

---

Asgard Yard's mass intake was a room built for disappointment.

It was long and low, with a ceiling of sprayed sealant over regolith fill and a floor that had been scrubbed so often the antislip grit had worn to a shine. Six weigh stations stood in a row, each a steel platform flush with the floor beside a terminal and a clerk. Above them a sign in four languages read EVERY KILOGRAM IS SOMEONE'S. Below it, in smaller letters added later and less officially: DECLARE OR PAY. ALSO PAY.

The air smelled of ozone and wet stone. Piq breathed it in and felt something in her chest unclench a quarter-turn. It was the smell of electrolysis, of water being cracked into breath somewhere close. Every habitat she had lived in smelled like this, and none of them had been home for longer than a contract.

The contract laborers went first, in a shuffling line, their kit bags on their shoulders. Piq watched them weigh in and noticed which ones paid without blinking: the ones who'd hidden something better elsewhere. A broad woman with a Tharsis co-op patch came in three hundred grams under and looked insulted about it.

"Varga, Piq," said the clerk at station four. He was a narrow young man with the boneless posture of someone born in low gravity. His name tag said FITCH, and his face said he had been awake for a long time. "Ibis Metrology, certified auditor, inbound. Personal allowance twenty kilos. Bags on the plate, please, and then yourself."

She put her duffel and her instrument case on the platform. The display considered, settled, and blinked.

**21.304 kg.**

Fitch looked at the number. He looked at Piq. He did not look surprised. In low gravity it took a long time for anything to fall, so it took a long time to build a sigh, and he built a very thorough one.

"One point three oh four over," he said. "Excess personal mass is tariffed at the Jovian Development Authority standard rate, which is"—he consulted nothing, because he knew—"four hundred and twelve Compact marks per kilo, prorated to the gram. Or you can jettison."

"Jettison where?"

"Reclamation bin. We crush it, sort it, and render it into feedstock." He gestured at a hopper by the wall, which had a slot in it like a mouth. "People leave all kinds of things. Last week someone left a wedding dress. Silk. Very heavy, silk."

Piq did the arithmetic while he spoke. Five hundred and thirty-seven marks was not ruinous; Lagrange Mutual would reimburse it under a miscellaneous code. But a line on a JDA tariff ledger reading *1.304 kg excess, personal, unspecified* would sit on file in a security office. It would be there for anyone curious enough to ask what an auditor carried that she couldn't do without. And there was a second problem. Every kilo over allowance was flagged for itemized declaration, which meant opening the instrument case on Fitch's counter and explaining, item by item, why a metrologist needed a hollow cylinder with a receiver inside it sensitive enough to hear a dosimeter cough on the far side of the moon.

"It's not personal mass," she said.

Fitch's expression said that everyone said that.

She unlatched the instrument case, which was what an innocent woman would do, and lifted out the top tray of calipers and strain gauges so he could see the foam bed beneath. Five polished cylinders sat in it, graded from fist-sized down to a pair of pins no bigger than rice grains. The lid bore an Ibis Metrology certification plate, and the cylinders gleamed with the faintly smug finish of objects that knew precisely what they were.

"Reference masses," Piq said. "OIML class E2. One kilogram, two hundred grams, one hundred grams, two grams, two grams. I'm here to audit the depot's mass flow, which means calibrating every load cell between your pads and the Sling breech. You can't audit a scale with a scale. You need a standard."

"They're mass," said Fitch.

"They're the definition of mass." She tapped the certification plate. "JDA Fiscal Code, Schedule Nine, paragraph four. Metrological standards certified for the calibration of Authority weighing equipment are classed as Authority-serving instruments and tariffed at the instrument rate. Not the personal rate."

Fitch looked at her for a while. Then he pulled up the code on his terminal, because he was the kind of clerk who checked, and Piq liked him for it. She watched his lips move through paragraph four. They stopped moving at the part where it agreed with her.

"Your overage," he said slowly, "is exactly a calibration set."

"That's why I'm over."

"Exactly. To the gram."

"It would be strange if it weren't. I packed to the allowance and then added the standards. I was raised on a cycler." She said it the way other people said *I was raised Catholic*: as an explanation for a set of habits that would otherwise look like a disorder.

Fitch absorbed this. He gave the impression of a man filing a new category of passenger.

"Instrument rate is nine marks a kilo," he said. "But instruments have to be logged individually. Serial, nominal mass, measured mass, purpose." He slid a small certified scale across the counter, a palm-sized plate under a draft hood with a display that read to the tenth of a milligram. "Measured mass. Here. Now."

"Of course."

She did the small ones first, because that was correct practice and because it gave her hands something to do. The two-gram pins came in at 2.0000 and 2.0001. The hundred-gram read 100.0002, and the two-hundred read 199.9998. Fitch typed each into the ledger with two fingers, slowly, reading the serials aloud off the engraved bases as though they were the names of the dead.

Then she lifted the kilogram.

It was the largest of the five and the only one that mattered. It was machined from a nickel alloy and polished to the same mirror finish as its siblings. Inside it, in a cavity Lagrange's engineers had lined with tungsten shot until the whole assembly came in at nominal, sat a burst transceiver, a wideband receiver and four months of encryption pads. Somebody at L4 had spent eleven weeks getting it to one kilogram. Piq had spent two evenings on the inbound leg checking their work with a borrowed balance, because she did not trust anyone's mass but her own.

She set it under the draft hood. The plate took the weight a fraction of a second slower in this gravity than it would have on Luna, and the display ran through its digits like a man counting change.

**1000.0004 g.**

"Four tenths of a milligram over nominal," Fitch said.

"Within E2 tolerance. Half a milligram either way at one kilo."

He leaned close to the hood and looked at the cylinder. Piq knew what he was seeing, because she had looked at it the same way on the inbound leg. A true E2 kilogram in stainless steel was a squat little thing you could close your hand around. This one was a finger's width taller than it should have been, because a hollow object had to be bigger to weigh the same. Her father would have spotted it from across a cargo bay. *Mass is honest,* he used to say. *Volume is where people lie.*

Fitch did not know her father.

"It's bigger than the others," he said. "Proportionally."

"Different alloy." That was true. "Lower density." That was also true, in the aggregate. "The air buoyancy correction is in the certificate. Page two."

Fitch opened page two. Page two was a table of air densities, temperatures and correction factors in eight-point type. Piq had found in her working life that very few people would argue with a table of correction factors, and Fitch was not one of those few. He typed *1000.0004*. He typed *Calibration of Authority weighing equipment*. He typed the serial, which had been engraved on the base by a Lagrange machinist working from a genuine Ibis numbering sequence. Then he sat back.

"Total instrument mass, one thousand three hundred and four point zero zero zero five grams," he said. "Instrument rate, nine marks per kilo. That's eleven point seven three six zero zero four five marks."

"Eleven seventy-four."

"Eleven seventy-four, plus the rounding fee."

"There's a rounding fee?"

"Point four of a centimark is a fractional mark," Fitch said, "and fractional marks have to be rounded, and rounding is a service." He turned the terminal toward her. "Three copies. JDA Fiscal, Ibis Metrology, and the station instrument registry. Each one's a separate attestation. You're certifying under your Ibis seal that the declared items are metrological standards and nothing else, and that you'll present them for inspection on demand."

So there it was, in plain type: *nothing else.*

Piq picked up the stylus. She had signed a great many things in her career with Lagrange's Special Losses division, under her own name and under others, and she did not lose sleep over signatures. A signature was a mass declaration for a promise. It recorded what you were willing to be weighed for. The cylinder really did weigh one kilogram. It would really calibrate a load cell, if she ever pointed it at one. Every number on the form was true. She was lying only about what the number contained, and she was very good at that kind of lie, because it was the kind nobody ever thought to weigh.

She signed the first copy. She signed the second. The stylus slipped as she reached for the third and left her fingers, and she watched it go. It drifted down in a long, lazy arc, turning once end over end, so slowly that she had time to think *I could catch that* and then time to decide not to bother. It landed on the counter with a tap and rolled half a centimeter. Fitch did not even look up. Everyone on Callisto dropped things all the time, and nobody hurried after them.

She picked it up and signed the third.

Fitch pressed a small adhesive tag onto each cylinder's case slot, a printed square with the JDA crest and the word INSTRUMENT. The kilogram got one too. It sat on her contraband like a halo.

"Welcome to Asgard Yard, Auditor Varga," he said. "Dose intake's through the blue door. Don't argue with the warden."

"Why would I argue with the warden?"

Fitch gave her the look of a man who had just watched her argue her way through Schedule Nine, paragraph four.

"Everybody does," he said. "Once."

---

The blue door led to a room half the size of mass intake and twice as quiet. The walls were lined with something dense and gray. Piq guessed borated polyethylene behind the paint, from the faint plastic give when she brushed it with her knuckles. A single bench ran along one side. On the other stood a desk, a terminal, a rack of dosimeter badges in charging cradles like eggs in a carton, and a woman.

Piq catalogued her the way she catalogued freight, because it was faster than having an opinion.

She was perhaps a hundred and seventy centimeters tall and fifty-eight kilos, give or take a liter of water, built along the lean, long lines of someone who had grown up in the gravity of a real planet and then stopped eating like it. She was forty, or close to it, with gray coming in at both temples in a pattern too even to be stress and too early to be age. Her hair was dark and cut short, regulation length for a helmet seal. She wore a JDA medical service jumpsuit with the sleeves pushed up, and a dosimeter of her own hung on a cord around her neck, worn and scuffed, an older model than anything in the rack. A thin white scar crossed the back of her left hand. Her nametape read ANAND, T. — DOSE WARDEN. Her eyes were brown and fixed on the terminal, and they did not come up when Piq came in.

That came to a total. Piq added it twice, and both times it came out a little higher than neutral, which was annoying, because she had not asked it to come out anywhere at all.

"Varga," the dose warden said. "Ibis Metrology. Sit."

Piq sat. In 0.126 g the bench took her weight like a polite suggestion.

"You came in on the cycler *Ascending Node* to Ganymede, then the *Prudent Interval* transfer." Anand was reading, and her voice had the flat cadence of a recitation she had performed many times without ever coming to enjoy it. "Nineteen months of cycler transit under a water wall, which was adequate. Two days in Ganymede orbit inside the magnetosphere, which was not. Your lifetime accrued dose as of the shuttle's last telemetry is zero point one eight seven sieverts."

"That seems high."

"It is high. You've spent a lot of your life on cyclers." She still did not look up. "Your contract runs four months. JDA occupational guidance for non-radiation workers caps annual exposure at zero point zero five zero sieverts, and your lifetime trajectory puts you on the conservative curve, so I'm allocating you zero point zero one seven for the duration of your stay. That's seventeen millisieverts. Callisto's surface gives you about a third of one per day unshielded. Inside the berms you'll take almost nothing, and you'll spend almost nothing, and if you go outside without a reason I'll know."

"I'll have a reason," Piq said. "I'm auditing the depot."

"Then I'll know your reason too." Anand pulled a badge from the rack and held it out at arm's length, the way one offered a treat to a dog of uncertain temperament. "This logs dose, location and time. It talks to the station mesh every ninety seconds. You wear it at all times outside your quarters."

Piq did not take it. She took a folded document out of her jacket instead.

"I'd like to request a waiver," she said. "My Ibis contract specifies a self-calibrated personal dosimeter, certified against our own reference sources. Audit chain of custody. If my dose logs feed a station network, the station can alter them, and an audit has to be independent of the thing it's auditing." She held out the waiver form, already filled in, because she had filled it in on the inbound leg and had expected to need it. "I'll still report daily. You'd have my readings. You just wouldn't have my position."

That was the real point, and she had wrapped it as well as she knew how. A badge that pinged her location to the mesh every ninety seconds would hand anyone with mesh access, which meant JDA security, a perfect map of where an auditor went at night.

Now Anand looked up.

It was, Piq would think later, a little like the shuttle touching down. It took a long time in this gravity, and it was unmistakably contact.

"No," Anand said.

"You haven't read it."

"I've read forty of them. They all say the same thing. 'Chain of custody.' 'Independence.' 'Our own reference sources.'" She did not take the form. "You know who files dosimeter waivers, Auditor? People who want their dose to be a private matter between themselves and their employer. Contractors who want to work a double shift on the surface without me pulling them in. Ice crews who want to stand next to a reactor a little longer than they should, because the bonus is good and the bill comes in thirty years. And companies that want to own the number." Her voice did not rise. It got more precise. "On this station I own the number. I set it, I watch it, I'm the one who grounds you when you run out, and I'm the one who writes it down if you don't."

"I'm not trying to cheat a dose limit."

"Nobody is. Until they are."

"I'm an auditor," Piq said, and heard in her own voice the edge her father used to get when someone disputed a load cell. "I'm here to make sure numbers are true. That's the job."

Anand laughed. It was short and without much air in it.

"Ibis certifies for Lagrange Mutual," she said. "Half the scales your company stamps feed straight into Lagrange's risk tables. You count it, they price it, and somewhere down the line someone gets told their claim's denied because a decimal point didn't reconcile." She set the badge on the desk between them and slid it across. "Auditors and insurers. Same species of vulture. One of you lands after the thing's dead and counts what's left, and the other decides what it was worth."

It was a fair hit, fairer than Anand could know. Piq felt it land somewhere under the sternum and logged the mass of it without looking at it directly. There was a story behind that sentence. You did not get that much contempt into seven words without having paid for it. She filed that away too, under *?*.

"I'm not here to deny anyone anything," she said.

"Then you won't mind the badge."

Piq looked at it. She looked at the waiver in her own hand, which had been perfectly drafted and which nobody was going to read. She weighed the cost of pushing harder against the cost of being remembered as the auditor who pushed harder, and the second number was larger.

She took the badge.

The clip was stiff. She fumbled it against the thick seam of her collar, and Anand made a small, impatient sound, rose, and came around the desk. In this gravity she did it in two long strides that were almost a glide. She took the badge from Piq's fingers and pressed it onto the collar herself, two fingertips flat against the clip until it seated with a click. Her hand was warm. It smelled faintly of nitrile and of the ozone that hung over everything here. It stayed against Piq's collarbone for perhaps half a second longer than the clip required, or perhaps exactly as long, and Piq could not tell which, and that bothered her more than the badge did.

"There," Anand said. She stepped back. "Zero point zero one seven. Don't make me come find you."

"Is that a threat?"

"It's a budget." The dose warden was already sitting down again, eyes back on the terminal. "On this station they're the same thing. Next."

There was no next. The room was empty except for the two of them. Piq stood up and went out through the far door, and she did not look back. She was proud of that for nearly eleven meters.

---

Her quarters were in Ring C, two levels down from the surface, at the end of a corridor that curved slightly to follow the buried contour of the berm. They measured six cubic meters, which the station directory described as *efficient*. There was a fold-down bunk, a fold-down desk and a fold-down sink, and there was a wall locker that did not fold down and so looked faintly embarrassed among the rest.

The door would not close.

She pushed it. It slid three-quarters of the way and stopped with a gritty, grinding hesitation. Piq crouched and ran her thumb along the seal track. It came away black. The regolith here was fine as flour and charged with old static, and it got into everything. It worked into the rubber lip of the seal in a dark, glittering line. She picked at it with a thumbnail, cleared enough to get the door to seat, and heard the soft pneumatic sigh of the gasket meeting its frame. On a cycler a bad seal was an emergency. Here it was apparently a Tuesday.

There was a port. She had not expected one. It was a narrow slot of thick laminated glass set high in the wall at the end of a light pipe that angled up through the berm to the surface. When she stood on the edge of the bunk she could look up the shaft and out.

The outer pane was frosted. Fine feathers of rime grew across it from the corners inward, where warm air leaking from the habitat met the cold of the glass and gave up its water. Through the frost, through the gap at the center where the rime had not yet closed, Jupiter hung low over the basin rim, banded and immense and absolutely still. It was in exactly the place she had seen it from the shuttle, an hour and a quarter before and four hundred kilometers away. It would be in that place tomorrow, and in a month, and for the rest of her life if she stayed. The frost on the glass caught its light and broke it into a thousand pale copper threads.

She watched it for a while. Then she got down, unpacked her duffel, laid out her tally pad and stylus on the fold-down desk, and opened the instrument case.

The kilogram came apart at a seam so fine that only someone who had been told where to look could have found it. She twisted the cap a quarter-turn against a detent, then another quarter-turn the other way, and it lifted free. Inside, in its nest of tungsten shot, the transceiver's status light came up a patient amber. She keyed her passphrase, a cycler weigh-master's mnemonic for the trim tables of the *Long Patience*, and the briefing unpacked onto her slate.

Augustin Pell wrote the way he weighed risk, as though every word were billed by the gram and the society were short of grams.

*VARGA.*

*FILE SL-2214-0391. ASGARD YARD, VALHALLA DEPOT.*

*Depot ice inventory reported to Authority and to us flat for six quarterly cycles. Our independent tonnage estimate, from Sling throw logs against consignee receipts, does not support flat. Shortfall indicated, magnitude unknown, est. hundreds of tonnes. Not declared. Not claimed. Not explained.*

*Attached: intercept fragment. Acquired through commercial intercept broker (Halloran Clearance, Ceres). Purchased in a mixed lot. Provenance unverified. Originating station unknown. Confidence in authenticity moderate. Confidence in meaning low.*

*Text, in full: "throw before conjunction, no one will see it until it's done."*

*Two readings.*

*One. Weapon. The Sling throws ballistic. A shelled slug on an unlisted inner-system trajectory is a kinetic weapon with a multi-year fuse. Undetectable once released if released during conjunction blackout. Target unknown.*

*Two. Fraud. Mass declared thrown, never thrown, then declared lost en route and claimed against our cargo book. Or a hull loss staged around a throw. Undetectable until filed.*

*Either costs the society. The first costs more than money.*

*Jupiter solar conjunction: Mars relay begins degraded batching shortly after your arrival, total blackout 38 days from your landing. Blackout duration 19 days. During blackout you will have no line to me and I will have no line to you.*

*Identify the throw. Identify who. Report early and often. Use the cover. You hold it legitimately; use the legitimacy.*

*Trust the ledger. Not the people in it.*

*PELL.*

She read it twice. The second time she heard it in his voice, which she had met in person exactly once, across a conference table at L4 under the long gray light of the habitat's false afternoon. It was a dry voice with all the commas taken out, the voice of a man who had spent forty years discovering that the warmest thing you could do for an adjuster in the field was to tell her the truth with nothing on it. *Trust the ledger. Not the people in it.* On the page it read like an order to be cold. Piq suspected that in Pell's head it read like *be careful*. She also suspected that the difference between the two would cost somebody something before this was over. She filed that under *?* as well. The column was getting long.

Thirty-eight days. She wrote it on the tally pad and boxed it.

*Throw before conjunction.* It was an ugly little sentence. It had been written by someone who expected nobody to look up until the thing was done, and who had perhaps been right about that until a broker on Ceres had sold it in a mixed lot to the one institution in the solar system whose whole business was looking up.

She reached for the stylus. It rolled away from her fingers, off the edge of the fold-down desk, and began its descent, a slow pale arc through the cabin air, turning once. She watched it all the way to the floor. In thirty-eight days, she thought, she would be alone with whatever it was. She picked the stylus up and put it in her breast pocket where it couldn't leave.

---

Station night on Asgard was a convention, not a fact. The sun would not set for another five Earth days. The corridor lights in Ring C dimmed to amber at twenty-two hundred by station clock, and the ventilation dropped half a note, and people went to bed because the lights said so.

Piq did not go to bed.

Her Ibis credential opened the depot ledger at read-only depth, which was all an honest auditor would need and therefore all she would ask for. It was a genuine credential; she had earned it, at a genuine desk, by genuinely failing the practical exam once and passing it the second time. The ledger came up on her slate in the standard JDA layout, columns of tonnage running back six years. She started where her father had always started, with the conservation equation, because mass did not care who you worked for.

*Opening inventory, plus intake, minus outflow, minus declared losses, equals closing inventory.*

Intake came from the harvester crawlers working the ice-rich crater floors to the south, block by block, each block weighed at the pad scales and tagged. Outflow was the Sling, which threw shelled slugs of ice at a few kilometers per second toward buyers who would wait years to receive them. There were also propellant sales, ice cracked and sold as hydrogen and oxygen to tugs and tankers, and the station's own draw, the water that became air and drink and shielding for nineteen hundred people. Declared losses were the small honest leaks of any ice store: allowance sublimation, handling spill and the shell fraction rejected at the Sling breech.

She took the last quarter first, the smallest and freshest, and reconciled it line by line. Intake against pad-scale tags. Throws against breech logs. Propellant sales against buyer receipts, which she pulled from the trade register. Station draw against the life-support plant's own electrolysis meters, which logged the ozone-scented work of turning ice into breath in increments of a tenth of a kilogram.

It did not close.

She checked her transcription, then the tags. She checked the calendar alignment, because quarterly figures were sometimes cut at different hours by different departments, and a few hours of Sling throws could vanish into the seam between two reporting periods. That accounted for nine tonnes, and there was more left over than that.

She took the quarter before, and the one before that. She did all six cycles in which the reported inventory had sat flat, perfectly and improbably flat, the same number to the tonne from one quarter to the next, as if someone had decided what the depot ought to hold and stopped weighing.

Every quarter ran short. None of them was short by much. Fifty tonnes, sixty, forty, seventy. Spread across a depot that held tens of thousands, each shortfall was smaller than the error bars on the survey lidar. Each one, alone, could have been rounding.

Piq did not believe in rounding. Rounding was a service, and somebody always paid for it.

She ran it a third time, from scratch, by hand on the tally pad in her father's shorthand, because a slate could carry a mistake forward forever and a hand could not. The corridor lights had long since gone amber. Somewhere above her, through two levels of fill and a frosted pane, Jupiter hung where it always hung. On the depot's sunward terraces the frost that should not have been there went on not making sense. She wrote the columns and added them and carried the totals and subtracted, and at the bottom of the last page she wrote the figure the equation demanded and did not write the equals sign.

The reported inventory said the depot held what it had always held. The weights said otherwise, and the weights did not lie. Something less honest had been standing between them.

She underlined the number twice and sat back. The cabin hummed. Her badge ticked off a dose so small it barely registered.

Over six quarters, without a claim or a declaration or a single line anywhere that admitted it, three hundred and forty tonnes of water ice had gone missing from the Valhalla depot.
