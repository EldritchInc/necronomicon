# Chapter 8: Countersignature

At 0544 on Day 19 the light-pipe in Piq's cell was still running its night cycle, a weak bluish wash meant to stand in for a sky. She sat on the bunk with the tally sheets across her knees and could not make herself lie down.

She had written FOUR ALIVE at the top of a clean sheet. Then she had written it again lower down, because the first copy was in Teo's block capitals and she wanted one in her own hand. Under it she had drawn a column for the drum: 60, an arrow, and the square her father used to draw for *later*. Below that sat the blank-headed column for the ripple, 2.7 seconds and drifting, with nothing to put at the top.

The report came first. Everything else depended on it.

She opened the kit with a quarter-turn and a reverse quarter-turn. The cylinder came apart in her hands with its usual small reluctance, like a jar nobody had opened in years. She set the burst transceiver on the blanket and plugged in the pad.

*SL-2214-0927. VARGA TO PELL. PRIORITY: FLASH.*

She looked at the word FLASH for a while. Special Losses kept it for weapons, deaths and solvency events. This qualified on two counts, perhaps three. She left it.

*0874 AND 0911 SUPERSEDED IN FULL. NO THROW CABAL. REPEAT NO CABAL. PULSE SOURCE IS EUROPA SURFACE OR NEAR SURFACE. METHOD: GAP MIDPOINTS MATCH EUROPA OCCULTATION BY JUPITER AS SEEN FROM ASGARD WITHIN 2 MIN OVER FOUR CYCLES (TABLE A). CARRIER DRIFT IS DOPPLER, ±18.8 KHZ ON 108 H PERIOD, CONSISTENT WITH EUROPA ORBITAL VELOCITY (TABLE B). KEYING DECODED. CONTENT: "FOUR ALIVE. REACTOR DRUM 60 DAYS. PLEASE." ATTRIBUTED TO AMAYA ANAND, ENGINEER, NINEFOLD GRACE, DECLARED LOST 14 MONTHS. ATTRIBUTION BASIS: PRIVATE KEYING SCHEME AND OPERATOR SIGNATURE (TABLE C). REQUEST: SUSPEND STANDING ORDER RE HOSTILE DEPOT CREW. REQUEST: GUIDANCE RE SALVAGE AND RESCUE. CREW ALIVE. CREW ALIVE.*

She deleted the second CREW ALIVE, which read like panic, and then put it back, because it was accurate and she wanted it to be the last thing he saw. She attached the tables, the raw pulse logs and the spectrum stacks. She did not attach the ripple. She had no idea yet what it was, and she was not going to send her handler a question mark labelled as data.

The draft came to 3.1 megabytes. Her notes on the Deimos relay were clear about what a 3 MB packet looked like from an auditor's room on Callisto: something worth reading. So she did what she had done every time. She wrapped it in forty-four megabytes of Ibis audit upload: scale certificates, depot tare tables, nine years of scrubber cartridge reweighs, and the hydroponic tithe ledger in full, lettuce by lettuce. Bulk was dull, and dull was safe.

The transceiver took the packet and spat it at the sky in under a second, buried in the station's outbound noise. The queue display updated.

*0874 — DELIVERED.*
*0911 — QUEUED (DEIMOS).*
*0927 — QUEUED (DEIMOS).*

She glanced at the two QUEUED lines and let them go. Conjunction was nineteen days off, on Day 38, the date she had been carrying since the shuttle. The relay was triaging, and Lisk had told her it would only get worse as the window narrowed. She was paying for her disguise by waiting, which was the deal she had made. Pell would get both reports together, the wrong one and its correction stacked in order, and he would read them the way he read everything, line by line with a pencil held like a scalpel.

She had the cylinder halfway closed when the receiver chimed with an inbound message.

*SL-2214-0391/R7. PELL TO VARGA. COMPOSED L4 D14 2210 UTC-ADJ. PRIORITY: IMMEDIATE.*

It was five days old. She opened it with a feeling she did not want to name. It was close to the feeling of seeing your own name in a newspaper.

*RE 0874. UR STATION HAS BOUND WAR-RISK COVER OVER JDA-CLAIMED EUROPA EXCLUSION ZONE. PLACED VIA PHOBOS SYNDICATE, LEAD LINE OUR OWN WAR-RISK DESK, 34% SHARE. LIMIT 2.1 BN MK. TERM: THROUGH CONJUNCTION PLUS 60. INFERENCE: UR ANTICIPATES HOSTILE ACT IN ZONE. COMPACT COUNSEL ADVISES ANY INCURSION INTO ZONE MAY BE RULED ACT OF WAR UNDER ARTICLE 11. EXPOSURE TO SOCIETY ON BOTH SIDES. YOUR PRIORITY: PREVENT ANY UNLICENSED LAUNCH FROM ASGARD. ANY MEANS. CORROBORATED. — P.*

She read it three times, and then once more with a finger under each line, the way her father had read a cargo manifest he already suspected.

*Our own war-risk desk.* So Lagrange Mutual had written cover on the war it was afraid of. One floor at L4 had sold Ur a policy against something bad happening over Europa, while another floor sent Piq to make sure nothing happened. Whoever insures the ships knows where they all are. She had always thought of that sentence as a description of power. It was also a description of a conflict of interest, and she had simply never had to stand in the middle of one.

*Any means.* In Pell's compression those two words were not a figure of speech. He used them the way an actuary uses *total loss*, with precision and no feeling. He was not ordering her to kill anyone. Pell never ordered anyone to do anything. He removed limits and left the arithmetic to his adjusters.

The order was five days old. It answered 0874, the report in which she had confidently described a cabal of depot workers rotating shifts around a hidden emitter, and in which she had called the gaps a roster. She had written that paragraph and been proud of it. Pell was replying to the woman she had been on Day 13.

Fine. He would have 0927 within days. He would read it, the order would lapse, and she would get guidance on salvage. She put the square beside *ANY MEANS* on her tally sheet. Then she was annoyed with herself for drawing it, because the square meant *I will look at this again*, and she did not want to look at those two words again.

She pulled up the briefing package instead. The background annex had come down with her and she had skimmed past it, because it held old losses in the Jovian book: three tugs, a slug carrier and a survey drone. The Ninefold Grace was the second entry. Claim LM-C-2213-1146.

She had not read it on the shuttle because she had been looking for a weapon and the Grace was a dead tug. Now she read it as an auditor, and that meant reading the manifest first, before the conclusions.

*Vessel: Ninefold Grace, Class IV tug, Asgard registry. Crew 6. Mission: survey support, Europa trailing hemisphere, Conamara sector, JDA permit 2213-E-041. Filed trajectory: Hohmann-plus fast transfer, 3.9 d. Propellant: water, 61.4 t at departure. Shielding: 18.0 t water blanket, compliant with Clause 14(c), certified by Lagrange surveyor at Asgard.*

She ticked it. It was the one line in the file that was plainly in order: the right figure, the right surveyor, the right clause. She remembered reciting that clause in Galloway's office with the Paper Lantern's crew rolling their eyes. It was minimum shielding water scaled to transit time, and it was the reason any Europa crew came home with their marrow intact. She moved on.

*Actual trajectory (from Asgard tracking and final telemetry): deviation from filed path at T+4.2 h to a low-energy transfer of approx. 9.6 d. Vessel entered Europa plasma torus region on extended residence. Telemetry terminated at T+9.1 d. Debris field located on surface by JDA overflight, Conamara sector. No survivors detected. Beacon activity subsequently ruled automated echo (JDA-SEC ruling 2213-77, M. Saar).*

*Determination: Loss attributable to unauthorized navigational deviation by master to a low-energy trajectory outside filed flight plan. Deviation constitutes pilot error under Exclusion 9(b). Final dose log anomaly reviewed and classed terminal artifact. Claim denied. Beneficiary notified: T. Anand (next of kin, A. Anand).*

Teo's name was there in the same plain type as the tonnage. Piq caught herself looking at it the way she might look at a scratch on a weight.

*Unauthorized deviation.* Why would a master take a six-person tug onto a nine-day crawl through the worst radiation in the Jovian system when he had filed for four days and carried shielding for four? Pilots didn't do that for fun. They did it when the numbers forced them. Somewhere in the file there should be a reason, and the adjusters had not written one. They had written *pilot error* and stopped. Her father would have said the equals sign was where you stop looking.

She drew a square next to *deviation*. Her sheet was filling with squares.

Then, mostly to stop staring at Teo's name, she opened the original intercept. She had read the line itself forty times. She had never read the envelope.

*FRAGMENT — HALLORAN CLEARANCE (CERES) LOT 2214-0417. SOURCE CLASS: COMMERCIAL INTERCEPT, JOVIAN, BAND UNSPECIFIED. CODEC: UR-STD VB4 (DECODED BY HALLORAN). CONTENT: "throw before conjunction, no one will see it until it's done."*

UR-STD VB4 was Ganymede's voice-and-burst standard, the one Ur's tankers used for traffic they were content to have overheard. Asgard's JDA traffic ran on Compact codecs, and so did the Brigade's, if the Brigade ever transmitted at all. Lagrange used its own. If the line had been one Asgard worker speaking to another, it should have been carried in a Callisto codec.

Unless someone on Ur had been talking about Asgard. Or someone had taken an Asgard line and re-encoded it before selling it on. Or someone had wanted it found, and wanted it to look like Ganymede.

She gave this one a question mark rather than a square. Her father, Gustav, had always said the question mark was the most honest symbol on a ledger. She put the cylinder back together, slid it into the case with the four true masses, and lay down at last at 0722. She watched the light-pipe begin to go slowly gold, and somewhere in the middle of deciding she would not sleep, she slept. It was the flat, dreamless sleep of a body that had been awake for thirty hours. When she woke the pipe had run through its whole day and was going amber toward evening, and someone was at the door.

---

The knock came at 1900, and it was not really a knock. It was a single flat-palmed strike at door height, the way a person announces themselves when they want to give no impression of asking.

Teo stood in the corridor in a clean jacket, her hair tied back with the grey cable insulation, reading glasses folded into the breast pocket. She held a slate pressed against her chest, screen inward.

"I've been to Hesper's cell," she said. "She wasn't in it."

"She's on the coils tonight?"

"No." Teo's eyes went down the corridor and came back. "She's in L6. Where you got eighty-one point six microsieverts the week before last that you told me came from a calibration rig."

Piq said nothing. Any answer would have been a second lie stacked on the first, and Teo would have weighed it.

"I'm not asking," Teo said. "I'm telling you where we're going. I want you there because you counted the gaps. I couldn't have. I've stood under that dome for nine years telling every new arrival that the badges sing differently when Europa ducks behind the old man. I turned it into a story. A bedtime story for the night shift. I never once wrote down *when*." She turned. "Bring the tables."

They walked. Piq had learned the long, slow stride the moon demanded, a push that carried you a meter and a half while you waited for the floor. Teo walked like a native, low and flat, never giving the ceiling a chance. In Ring B she said, without turning her head, "Juno. Abara. Rafe. Achterberg on the hydroponic line, I'd bet a month's tea. I've had their badge spectra on my desk for half a year with that 2.223 line in them, and I logged it every time as *source unattributed*." A pause. "That isn't lying. It's an accurate description of a source I declined to attribute."

"I know the form," Piq said.

Teo glanced at her, then away.

The service tunnel to L6 smelled of cold regolith and the ozone tang of the electrolysis plant leaking through a seal somebody should have reseated. The hangar door had a hand-lettered sign: *AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY (YOU KNOW WHO YOU ARE).* Teo did not knock this time. She keyed the dose warden's override, which opened every pressure door on Asgard, and the door slid back on light and noise.

Eleven people stopped working. Piq counted them before she could stop herself, the way she counted everything. Hesper was at the reactor end with a torque wrench, sleeves rolled to the elbow. Lucky was on a scaffold, his hand flat on the Mule's hull as if feeling for a pulse. Juno was halfway through lowering a stockpile block on a hoist, and the block hung turning slowly, about four hundred and sixty kilograms of hand-cut ice glittering under the work lamps.

Nobody spoke. In 0.126 g a dropped shim takes nearly two seconds to reach the floor, and someone had dropped one. They all watched it fall.

"Teodora," Hesper said.

"Hesper."

"I suppose you've brought a form."

"I've brought a message." Teo turned the slate around and held it up so the work lamps caught it. She had copied it out by hand and photographed it, Piq saw, rather than typing it. That was her block capitals, and the smudge where her palm had rested.

FOUR ALIVE. REACTOR DRUM 60 DAYS. PLEASE.

Lucky came off the scaffold without using the ladder. It was a three-meter drop, which on Callisto was an act of impatience rather than courage. He landed in a crouch and was at Teo's elbow before the shim hit the floor.

"Oh," he said. "Oh, oh, oh. That's her? That's the clicks? You *read* the clicks?" He reached for the slate, stopped himself, and knocked three times on the nearest crate instead. "We've had a man on the coils every hour Europa's up for five months, and we couldn't read a word. Juno thought it was Rhee doing the old Compact distress code wrong. I thought it was a prayer. Rafe thought it was a song. I told him a song would be shorter."

"It's not a distress code," Teo said. "It's a dose dump. A bunk-wall cipher I made up when I was nineteen and she was fourteen. Nobody else ever knew it." Her voice stayed level, and that took some effort. Piq could hear what it cost. "Five months, Hesper."

Hesper put the wrench down on its padded tray so it wouldn't skate. "Five months and a bit."

"You heard my sister's hand for five months and you didn't come to my office."

"I heard a hand. I didn't know whose." Hesper wiped her palms on her thighs. "I knew it wasn't an echo. Echoes don't stop for the weekends. I knew it was coming from Europa, because I'm not a fool and I can read an ephemeris. I knew Saar had signed it dead. What I didn't know, Teo, was what you'd do with it."

"I'd have *read* it."

"You'd have logged it." Hesper's voice was gentle, and the gentleness was its own blow. "Then you'd have come down here and measured every one of these children, and you'd have grounded the lot of them by the end of the week, because every rule you've got says this tug is a coffin and every person who works on it is over budget. And you'd have been right. I've watched you hold this station's dose for nine years. I've watched you hold your own grief by the same method. I wasn't going to hand you a voice you couldn't answer and then ask you to sign us all off it."

Nobody in the hangar moved. Juno's block turned on its hoist, catching the light and letting it go.

Teo's jaw worked. "That wasn't your choice."

"No," Hesper said. "Nothing about this has been anybody's choice." She looked past Teo at Piq. "And you've brought the auditor."

"She counted the gaps," Teo said. "The silences line up with Europa going behind Jupiter, to the minute. The drift is Doppler. She worked it out alone, from a band she found during a registry sweep, before I ever saw it. I didn't know my sister's hand was in the sky until she put it in front of me."

Hesper's eyes rested on Piq for a long moment. She had the look of an old weigh-master checking a scale she had once caught reading light.

"The spoons," Hesper said.

"Two hundred and ninety-one," said Piq.

"And you let ninety-one stand."

"I wanted to know what you'd do with a wrong number I didn't correct."

"So did I." Something moved at the corner of Hesper's mouth. It wasn't a smile, but it was the place a smile would go. "Show me the tables."

Piq laid them out on a crate stencilled *MASS 0.64 T — FRAME — DO NOT STACK*. Table A gave gap midpoints against occultation midpoints: four cycles, residuals under two minutes. Table B was the Doppler fit, and Table C showed the keying grid. She talked them through it in the order she had once used on Teo's screens, data first and theory after. Partly that was method. Partly it was so that nobody had to watch her draw a conclusion before they could draw it themselves. The hangar crew pressed in around her. Somebody's breath was warm on her ear. Juno smelled of cutting fluid. Lucky narrated everything a beat behind her, like the wedding link's delay: "Gap. Gap. Moon goes behind the old man. Oh, that's *lovely*. That's the moon doing the math for you."

When she finished, Juno said quietly, "We've had those gaps on the wall for months." She pointed. There was a chart of hand-ruled columns, the silences shaded in pencil. "We just called them *Europa's down*. We never thought to work out the midpoints. We knew where it was. We didn't need to prove it."

"You didn't need to," Piq said. "I did."

"Why?" said a man near the back. He was broad, with burn scars across the backs of both hands, and he had to be Rafe. "Who's she proving it to? Auditors report to someone."

Teo started to speak. Piq got there first. It had to be her.

"Ibis Metrology," she said. "A certifying office on Ceres. They care whether my totals foot. I won't sign the depot reconciliation at a number I can't stand behind, and right now there's three hundred and forty tonnes I can't stand behind." She looked at the ice blocks on their pallets. "I don't think I'll be putting this in the Ibis upload."

That was true, every word of it, and it was easy. Too easy. She noticed how it felt leaving her mouth: smooth, like a mass with no burr on it. She noticed that Teo, beside her, relaxed by a degree at the word *Ceres*, because Ceres was nobody's enemy. She noticed that she disliked herself for noticing. She entered the omission in a column she did not show anyone.

Lucky slapped the crate. "She counts. I said she counted. At the wedding, I said, that's a woman who counts. She ran my vows like a cargo handover. Over. *Over.*" He turned to Hesper. "Boss, she's the auditor who can count. We haven't had one of those. Every bit of arithmetic in this hangar is you and a slate at two in the morning, and you add like a poet."

"I add like an engineer," Hesper said. "Which is to say I round in the direction that keeps the thing from breaking." She put a finger on Table A, then lifted it. "All right. The auditor who can count. Teo. What does sixty days mean?"

Teo had been waiting for that question. Piq could see it in her shoulders.

"It means her reactor drum had sixty days left when she started keying that message," Teo said. "I don't know when she started. Piq's logs go back to Day 4. Your logs go back five months." She looked at the receiver bolted to the hangar wall, an ugly beige box with a hand-wound coil feeding up through the roof. "If I can get clean frames out of your archive, I can find the first time she keyed sixty. That gives us an upper bound."

"It's all yours," Hesper said. "Juno, give her the archive."

It took Teo forty minutes at a crate table with her reading glasses on, typing with two fingers. Piq sat beside her and rejected false pulses, Jovian noise spikes she could spot by eye now. Nobody in the hangar went back to work. Lucky did, briefly, then came down to watch again. Someone made tea. Someone else put a wrapped protein bar at Piq's elbow without a word, and that was the closest she came, for a while, to being unable to speak.

"Here," Teo said at last. Her pencil had stopped on a timestamp. "Clean frame, sixty. Twenty-five days before today."

"And before that?"

"The archive's too noisy before that. She might have been sending sixty for months, or she might have been sending something else." Teo took the glasses off and put them on the crate very precisely. "So we have thirty-five days. At most. It could be less."

Lucky stopped talking. Nobody else in the hangar had been talking anyway.

"Blackout's in nineteen," Hesper said. "Day thirty-eight, the relay goes dark, and it stays dark for nineteen more."

Piq ran it without meaning to. Nineteen days to conjunction. Thirty-five, at most, on the drum. The drum ran out sixteen days into the silence.

"Then the rescue flies inside the blackout," Teo said, "and it flies with every kilogram of shielding this hangar can cut, and every one of you is coming to my office tomorrow to have your marrow counted. I am not doing this so you can kill yourselves on my watch." She stood. "That's my price for not having been asked. And Hesper, if you say *martyr* to me I'll put you on a dose plan so strict you'll be breathing through a straw."

"I wasn't going to say it."

"You were thinking it."

"I'm always thinking it," Hesper said. "It's my resting state."

Piq gathered the tables. At the door Hesper caught her sleeve. Her grip was strong.

"Tomorrow," Hesper said quietly. "Saar's densitometer cart is in Ring C east. Juno saw it come out of stores. Whatever you've got that you're carrying in your hands—"

"Reference masses."

"—I hope they're as solid as you are," Hesper said, and let go.

---

The cart arrived at 0758 on Day 20. It was a squat orange machine on four bounce-damped wheels with a mast of gamma detectors and, in a lead-tungsten pig on its back, the source: a cesium check pellet sealed and tagged, with JDA STORES 2201 stencilled in letters faded nearly to a guess. Behind it came the tech, a thin man named Oyelaran who wore earplugs although nothing made a sound. Behind him came Marek Saar in a pressed grey jacket, smiling at the corridor as though he had tidied it himself.

Behind Saar, against every probability Piq had calculated, came Fitch from mass intake, with a clipboard.

"Any undeclared mass found by an instrument survey," Fitch explained before anyone asked, "falls under intake jurisdiction until it's established whose it is. It's in the statute. I don't make the statutes. I'm just the only one who reads them."

"Mr. Fitch has been very thorough," Saar said. "We've done forty-one cells since six. He's found a great deal of tea."

"Undeclared tea," said Fitch, with feeling.

Saar turned the smile on Piq. "A station-wide densitometric survey, Auditor. Three hundred and forty tonnes of water ice are missing from a depot you were sent to reconcile. I thought you'd approve. I'm doing your job with better equipment."

"I approve of instruments," Piq said. "I'd want to see the calibration certificate."

Oyelaran held it up without being asked, as though people asked him often. It was current. She read the whole thing anyway, top to bottom, because Saar was watching to see whether she would, and because reading it slowly bought her eleven seconds in which nobody went near the case under her bunk.

"Go ahead," she said, and stood aside.

The cart rolled in on its damped wheels. The mast swept the bunk, the shelf, the wall cavity and the floor, and the display on its back drew her room in false colour: the blue of blanket and foam, the green of the water bladder, the hard white blots of anything dense. Five white blots sat in a neat row under the bunk.

"Reference masses," Piq said. "Five."

Saar looked at the display. "Four," he said, pleasantly. "And one that's somewhat less dense than its friends."

Piq felt the room go still around her the way a balance goes still before it reads. She kept her hands where they were.

Fitch was already turning pages.

"Day 1," he said. "Intake. Audit reference masses, five, Ibis Metrology, certified class F2, hollow-core sealed adjustment weight, one. Weighed by me." He tapped the line. "I remember. She argued about it. Thirteen hundred grams over allowance and she argued that a scale's own reference weights can't count against the scale's operator, which is in the statute, sub-paragraph six. I hadn't read sub-paragraph six in eleven years. She had." He said it in the tone of a man paying an unwilling compliment to a rival. "Declared, weighed, sealed. The hollow one weighs exactly what I wrote down. Density's not mass. Mass is my department."

"Adjusting cavity," Piq said. "Every precision weight has one. You trim it with shot and seal it. If anyone breaks that seal the calibration's void, and I'd have to recertify every scale on Asgard against nothing. The depot reconciliation would be pushed back past conjunction."

"Past conjunction," Saar repeated, as though it were a vintage.

"And declared mass under intake seal," Fitch added, "can't be resurveyed by anybody except intake, and only on evidence of alteration. The seal's intact." He bent and looked at it, which no one had asked him to do either. "Intact. I'm satisfied. I'm sorry, Chief, it's my department."

Saar regarded the five white blots for a moment longer. Then he lost interest in them. Piq saw it happen. It was not tact and it was not defeat. He had simply never come for the weights.

"Your weights are your own, Auditor," he said. "I wouldn't dream of voiding them."

The cart gave a soft, apologetic chirp.

Oyelaran took out one earplug. Everyone looked at the display. The mast had tipped back on its own sweep, and in the overhead duct above the door, where the air came in warm from Ring C, a small irregular blot glowed the green of water and meat.

*ANOMALY. 4.1 KG. UNDECLARED.*

The blot moved.

Fitch made a sound Piq had previously heard only from tug pilots on a good burn. "That's mine," he said. "That's intake jurisdiction. Undeclared, uncertified, ambulatory." The blot moved again, unhurried, toward the junction. "Oyelaran, I need a ladder. Chief, I need the corridor. Nobody touch that duct."

"Mr. Fitch," Saar said.

"Four point one," said Fitch, already backing into the corridor, eyes on the grille. "Nine months I've had a four-point-one in the scrubber reweigh residuals and nobody believed me."

A grey-striped tail showed briefly in the slats, as if to settle the question, and withdrew. Somewhere down the duct came the faint, unmistakable thump of four paws landing in low gravity, a long way from where they had started.

Saar watched Fitch go. Then he took a slate from his jacket and touched it once, and the corridor display beside Piq's door, which normally ran the dose forecast and the hydroponic rota, went white and filled with type.

*JDA SECURITY DIRECTIVE 2214-31. EFFECTIVE 0900 D20. PENDING RECONCILIATION OF DEPOT MASS DISCREPANCY, ALL MOVEMENT OF WATER ICE ON ASGARD YARD, IN ANY FORM AND OF ANY QUANTITY, INCLUDING INTRA-DEPOT TRANSFER, SLUG SHELLING, HYDROPONIC TITHE AND STOCKPILE CUTTING, REQUIRES COUNTERSIGNATURE BY JDA-SEC IN ADDITION TO EXISTING AUTHORIZATIONS. UNCOUNTERSIGNED MASS WILL BE HELD AT POINT OF ORIGIN. — M. SAAR, HEAD OF SECURITY.*

"You can't do a reconciliation while the mass is moving," Saar said. "I'm sure you'd agree. I'm freezing the ledger for you."

"Every kilo," Piq said. "That includes the shower allocation."

"I've delegated the showers." He put the slate away. "Thank you for your cooperation, Auditor. I'll let Mr. Fitch have his anomaly."

He went down the corridor after the cart, not hurrying, past the display, which kept the directive up and pushed the dose forecast into a narrow strip at the bottom where nobody would read it.

Piq read the directive twice. Then she went to Ring C.

The freight lift at the east end of the ring was the only one wide enough for a stockpile block, and it ran from the depot level up to the service tunnels that led, eventually, to L6. The lift doors stood open at 0911. A pallet sat inside, carrying a block of hand-cut ice the size of a coffin, sweating faintly in the warmer air of the ring. A red tag had been wired to its corner. A JDA guard Piq didn't know stood beside it with his thumbs in his belt.

Juno stood in front of the guard. She had a requisition slate in one hand, signed in the right places by the right people, and she was holding it out to him in the way you'd hold out a hand to someone you weren't sure would take it.

"It's the hydroponic tithe," Juno said. "It's been the hydroponic tithe every Day 20 for six years. Ask Achterberg."

"Needs countersignature," the guard said.

"From whom?"

"JDA-SEC."

"Which is who?"

The guard thought about it. "Chief Saar."

"And where's Chief Saar?"

"Countersigning," said the guard, without irony.

Piq looked at the tag. *HELD AT POINT OF ORIGIN. PENDING JDA-SEC.* There was a box for a signature and a box for a time, and both were empty. The block's mass was stencilled on the side in Juno's hand, 0.46 T. Piq knew where it was going, and it was not the lettuce. Under the block, already, the pallet had started to collect a thin film of meltwater, which in 0.126 g did not run off but beaded and climbed the pallet edge, as if looking for some way out.

Juno saw her. Her face did not change, but she lowered the slate.

"That's one block," Juno said quietly, when the guard had turned to answer his radio. "Hesper wanted forty more cut before the window. She's got the saws running down there right now. Every one of them has to come up this lift."

"And none of them is going to get a signature."

"He's not even going to say no," Juno said. "He'll just never get to it."

Piq took out her pencil. On the back of the tally sheet folded in her jacket she drew a new column and headed it with the directive number, and under it she wrote what she knew: forty blocks short, one held, and no signatures. Below that went the other numbers, the ones that did not move whatever Saar signed. It was Day 20. Conjunction was eighteen days off. The drum had thirty-four days at most.

The lift chimed, the way lifts do when they have been held open too long, and the doors began to close on the block with the red tag and nobody's name on it.
