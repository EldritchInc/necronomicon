# Chapter 4: Mule

It was Day 10, and she gave the hangar eleven more minutes. Eleven was the number left in the calibration window she had built for herself, once she subtracted the walk back to a live mesh node and a margin for being stopped and asked friendly questions.

The man who had knocked on the nose cone stayed close to the ice for a while, one hand on it, the way you'd stand beside a sleeping animal you weren't sure of. Then someone below called "Lucky, you're blocking the hoist," and he answered "I'm *blessing* the hoist," and moved off. Piq wrote **LUCKY** on her pad and put a box around it. A name was a fact. The voice she had already filed. She had heard six minutes and twelve seconds of it in Rook's wedding packet, a man who apparently breathed through his ears so he never had to stop talking. Here was the groom, telling two-thirds of a tonne of hand-cut ice that it was doing a good job.

She counted the people on the floor again and got eleven both times. She counted the blocks on the frame and got twenty-two, and she counted the empty bays in the strapping and got eight. The frame blocks were big, a long stride on the long edge and nearly a meter deep. She put them at a little under two-thirds of a tonne apiece, which made the frame about fourteen tonnes. She ran a slow tally of the stockpile under the thermal blankets along the near wall and made it ninety-four blocks, though two might be one block split. Those were not the same blocks. They had the same face but were cut thinner, perhaps three-quarters the depth, a shade under half a tonne each. Two sizes meant two jobs, and only one of the jobs was on the frame. The melt rig hummed. A long gray seam of slush, poured between two blocks, set as she watched, the way grease sets in a cold pan.

At 1911 she left the way she had come. On the gallery steps she kept her feet low and flat, because a stride that cleared the floor in this gravity carried you a meter and a half, and a meter and a half in the dark was how you met a handrail with your teeth. The L6 hatch seal ground grit when she closed it behind her. Thirty meters on, the first live mesh node blinked green on its bracket, and her badge reacquired with a small electronic hiccup she felt more than heard.

At meter D-7 she finished the paperwork on the calibration she had actually done. The meter really had been drifting, two-tenths of a percent high on the feed line, which was the kind of fact that made a cover sound true. Then she opened an addendum to the work order and typed it the way she would if it were real. Part of the reason she wanted it in writing was that it was half real.

*Traced unmapped branch of D-line feed downward from L5 junction. Branch continues to construction-era level 6. Mesh coverage ends approx. 30 m past L6 hatch; final node dark, housing cracked. Recommend Facilities node repair and as-built drawing update. Branch not metered. Further survey pending.*

She read it twice. Every sentence in it was true. She had followed a pipe, and Hesper had been walking beside the pipe at the time. A security officer reading the badge log would find a gap, and right under the gap he would find an auditor complaining about the gap in the dry, put-upon voice of a woman who wanted the drawings fixed. Her father had taught her this without knowing he was teaching it: the best place to hide a discrepancy is inside a report about a discrepancy.

She filed it at 1958. The Facilities queue acknowledged it in under a second and assigned it a ticket number with seven digits. Piq thought of Hesper on the Sling tour saying *they lose all my tickets on the shade*, and felt a small, unwanted pity for the ticket.

---

In her quarters she ate standing up. In six cubic meters, sitting down was a decision. The light-pipe showed Jupiter in its usual place over the western rim, banded and enormous and not moving, because nothing on this side of Callisto ever saw it move. The night was four days old, and the frost outside had gone the color of weak tea.

The receiver was running. She had left it logging while she worked. The pulses had come back at 1352 that afternoon, thirty hours and ten minutes after the second gap began at 0742 on Day 9, opening with the same pattern as before: short, long, short, short, pause, short. That made two gaps of thirty hours and ten minutes each, a hundred and eight hours apart. Two was not yet a pattern, but she wrote the third anyway, because a prediction you don't write down is a prediction you'll lie about later.

**Gap 3 (pred.): begins ~1942 Day 13.**

Then she pulled up the Sling crew roster and found Hesper's line for Day 13. *Coil inspection, north end, 1600–2400. Single crew.* Thirty-eight kilometers away, on her own, where no one could check.

Piq looked at that for a while. It sat a little too neatly in her ledger, like a crate that weighs exactly what the manifest says, and she didn't like the feeling. She ran it once more and it came out the same. She drew a line under it.

Under the line she wrote **MULE** and listed what she knew:

*Tug, ~1st-gen ore-hauler hull, reg. plate removed. Crew section only being wrapped: 22 blocks (~0.64 t), ~14 t now; 30 bays, ~19–20 t finished. Stockpile ~94 blocks, thinner cut (~0.46 t), ~43 t, plus bladders (depot fittings). Melt rig. Eleven crew. Hesper. Lucky (groom).*

Then the arithmetic. Fourteen tonnes in the frame, forty-three on the floor, a few more in the bladders. Call it sixty-five at the outside. Three hundred and forty were missing from the depot. That left about two hundred and seventy-five tonnes that she had not seen, which was a far larger quantity than the one she had.

And the eight empty bays would take five tonnes of the big blocks, not forty-three of the small ones. The stockpile was eight times what the frame still needed, and cut to a size the frame didn't use. She wrote *thin blocks: for what?* in the unexplained column and left it there.

Twenty tonnes of ice wrapped around a crew compartment was not ballast or trim, and it wasn't decoration. It was a shield. Clause 14(c), which she had recited to the *Paper Lantern*'s crew word for word, set minimum shielding water for Europa-bound vessels scaled by transit time. Twenty tonnes was a long transit or a hot one.

She wrote **Europa?** under the thin blocks. Saar's voice came back to her unprompted from the corridor on her second day: *If you hear Europa talk that worries you.* She had not liked that he was the kind of man who would ask. She liked even less that she now had some.

---

The public traffic board on the Ring A concourse was the closest thing Asgard had to a town square. Through the morning of Day 11, people stopped in front of it with their tea the way people on old Earth once stood in front of weather reports, wanting to be told that the sky was behaving.

It was a wall of slow light. Callisto sat at the center as a gray disc. Jupiter was a banded smear at the edge of the display, its scale fudged so it would fit. Ganymede was further in, with the Ur beacon ringed in amber, because amber in JDA iconography meant *Martian, technically allowed*. Small green tick marks were JDA-registered traffic: survey tugs, depot shuttles, the *Paper Lantern* six days out and crawling. The Sling's next scheduled throw was a dotted line wandering out toward the inner system, with a launch time nine days away printed beside it in a font that made it look important.

One mark was amber and alone, on a long looping ellipse that ran between Ganymede and Callisto and then bent back again.

Piq tapped it. The board offered a JDA traffic advisory, number 2214-188. She read it the way she read any document with a signature block, last line first.

*Issued by authority of the Office of Security, Asgard Yard. M. Saar.*

Then the body:

*Ur-registered propellant tanker STEADFAST MARGIN (SM-114) continues in a Ganymede-Callisto transfer orbit for which no destination filing has been received. The vessel has now completed three periapsis passages without circularization or declared purpose. The Office regards this as a provocative posture inconsistent with routine propellant operations. All JDA-registered traffic is advised to maintain separation of no less than 2,000 km. Further advisories will follow as appropriate.*

SM-114. *Master, SM-114.* She did not have to check, because she remembered Ziva Kalnins's entire contribution to the wedding packet: *Officiant. I accept the terms. Please do not let him write the vows.*

So the bride was the provocation.

Piq stood very still in front of the board while a maintenance tech with a bulb of tea leaned past her, read the advisory, and said to nobody, "*Posture*," as if the word had a smell. He moved on. Piq stayed where she was.

A transfer orbit between the two moons was a road you could leave cheaply at either end. A tanker coasting on it was parked. It was sitting in the one place in the system where it could reach Callisto's neighborhood for very little fuel, and it had been sitting there long enough for Saar to write *posture*.

She worked it through. A Sling throw was twelve tonnes of shelled ice at six point one kilometers per second, nominally sent on a multi-year trajectory toward a customer in the inner system. An Ur tanker in the right place could match a slug's path and take it aboard.

She stopped there, because that was the sentence a briefing would write, and she ran the number instead. Six point one at the muzzle was well over Callisto's escape speed. Take the moon's pull out of it and the slug still left at better than five and a half kilometers per second. A tanker idling at the Callisto end of its ellipse might be moving a kilometer or two relative to the moon. Matching the slug would cost four kilometers per second, likely more, and on a loaded tanker that was most of a tank. Nobody stole twelve tonnes of ice by burning a hundred.

Unless the slug never left at six point one. The Sling was a coil, and a coil could be turned down. Throw at two and a half, a little over escape, aimed to fall onto the tanker's ellipse at nearly its speed, and the tanker could meet it with a few hundred meters per second, a burn too small to file. Log the throw at nominal. The depot would book it as thrown, Lagrange would insure it as in transit, and it would arrive nowhere at all. *Throw before conjunction, no one will see it until it's done.* During conjunction the relay at Mars went dark for nineteen days. Nobody in the inner system would be watching the books, and nobody would see a slug fail to appear until long after the tanker had gone home to Ganymede with it.

That made it fraud. Pell had named two fears, and this one fit the facts with only one joint showing. The cradle crew would have to falsify the throw log and the coil power draw, and the pilot school had trained everyone involved. But Asgard's own tracking would watch a slug leave at less than half speed on the wrong heading, so somebody in the tracking office would have to be looking the other way. That was one more conspirator than she had any evidence for. The groom was marrying the tanker.

It fit, and she disliked how well it fit. She found she was biting her right cheek, and stopped.

It didn't explain the Mule or the twenty tonnes of shielding. You didn't need an armored tug to steal ice. You needed a bribed cradle loader and a quiet night. She made herself write both halves on the pad and leave the line between them open.

*SM-114 loitering G-C transfer, 3 periapses, unfiled. Ziva = master. Fits: short throw (~2.5 km/s, logged 6.1) onto SM-114 ellipse, cheap match, fraud. Weak: local tracking would see it, so who's in tracking? Doesn't fit: MULE shielding. Why shield a crew to steal ice?*

On the way out of the concourse she passed a bulletin screen showing the wedding date, Day 16, under the heading **COMMUNITY**, with a cartoon of two rings joined by a dotted line meant to suggest a link from one moon to another. Somebody had drawn a tiny tanker on the line in stylus.

---

The Facilities as-builts for Level 6 were thirty years old and had been scanned from paper. That gave every line a soft, furred edge, like a drawing that had been rained on. Piq had pulled them under the flow-meter work order, which was the one task she could justify, and she read them in a corner of the depot control annex while the shift around her argued about a pump schedule.

The construction hangar showed up on the sheet as a rectangle with a cross through it and the annotation **TEMP**. Like everything on Asgard marked temporary, it had outlived the people who planned it. Along the north wall the drawing showed a cable tray running the length of the roof, between the second and third ribs. In the station's first decade they had strung power through it to the big fabrication lamps. The tray was crawlable: the drawings gave its section as 0.9 by 0.7 meters, with a hatch at the eastern end that opened onto a ventilation plenum serving the levels above. The plenum, in turn, connected to a utility ladder that came out on Level 4, beside hydroponics.

She marked the route on her pad in pencil with distances in strides, then rubbed out the strides and wrote meters. Strides were too optimistic.

At 1830 she logged into the work order again. *Continuing D-line branch survey, L5–L6.* She packed a headlamp, a stylus, her pad, the small camera she used to photograph scale tags, and a protein bar in a sealed foil sleeve, which she had come to think of as standard procedure on Asgard. Then she went back down.

The tray was worse than the drawings. Thirty years of regolith dust had settled in it so finely that it behaved like a liquid. Every time she pulled herself forward with her elbows, it rose in a slow cloud and refused to settle, taking whole minutes to drift back down at an eighth of Earth's gravity. She learned to breathe through her teeth. The old cable was still there, bundled and dead, and she slid along beside it with her shoulders brushing both walls. Every three meters there was a grating, and through each one the hangar floor below was lit gold by the portable lamps hanging from the ribs.

Forty-one meters in, she reached a grating directly above the Mule's crew section and stopped.

From above, the tug looked less like a ship and more like an arm in a cast. The ice frame ran from just behind the stubby nose to the start of the tank section. The blocks were cut square and fitted with a tolerance she would have accepted on a shipping crate. Two new ones had gone in since last night: twenty-four now, the seams still wet, both of them the big cut. Behind the frame the tug was bare metal, old tanks and older plumbing, and the drive bell was scarred dark, as if somebody had once held a blowtorch to it out of spite.

Hesper stood at the frame with her palm flat on a block. The other hand held her thermos. She wasn't doing anything that Piq could see except standing there, and nobody bothered her.

Lucky was underneath the tug on a creeper, which in this gravity he didn't need. Piq gathered he used it because he liked rolling. He had a wrench and an opinion.

"I'm just saying," Lucky said, "for the record, which nobody is keeping, I want it noted."

"Noted," said Hesper.

"Every Europa job I ever flew. Every single one. Lagrange's water blankets cost me three hundred meters per second. Three hundred. You know what I could do with three hundred meters per second, Hes? I could go to *lunch*. I could take a *scenic route*. Instead I carry a swimming pool on my head around Europa so some actuary on L4 can sleep, and I cut every corner on the trajectory, and I come home on fumes with a dose badge like a Christmas tree, and that's the *safe* way."

"It is the safe way," Hesper said.

"I know it's the safe way. I'm not complaining it's the safe way. I'm complaining it's *expensive*." He rolled out from under the tug, sat up in a gentle arc, and pointed the wrench at the ice. "And now look. Twenty tonnes. Voluntarily. With my own hands. With my own hands and Rafe's hands and Mo's hands and everybody's hands, we are strapping a swimming pool onto this poor animal, and nobody is even making us."

"You'll want every centimeter," Hesper said. She said it quietly and without inflection, as though she were reading a gauge, and Lucky stopped talking for nearly two seconds, which Piq suspected was a record.

"Yeah," he said finally. "Yeah, I know."

Piq wrote down *300 m/s* with a careful hand. Twenty tonnes of shielding water on a tug that massed, by the look of the tanks, maybe forty or fifty tonnes fueled. That changed the mass ratio a great deal. The cost came out of the exponent, and an exponent never forgave you. Every kilogram you carried had to be pushed by fuel, and every kilogram of fuel had to be pushed too. Three hundred meters per second was a plausible bill. Lucky was a man who knew to the meter what his rules cost him.

*You'll want every centimeter.* Somebody was going to fly this thing somewhere hot.

"Speaking of hands," Lucky said, rolling back under, "Ziva says the officiant sounds like a mortician."

Piq stopped writing.

"Ziva hasn't heard the officiant," said someone else, a younger voice off to the left. Piq angled her head against the grating and found him: a lanky man with a shaved head, coiling hose. She thought it was the twenty-three-year-old cradle loader from the roster, Rafe Danjuma. His duty photo had made him look about fifteen.

"Ziva has read the officiant's *email*," said Lucky. "Ziva says the email was very correct. Ziva says it was the most correct email she's ever read and it frightened her. Ziva says—"

"Lucky," said Hesper.

"What? I'm only saying she's the auditor. She's the one going round with the scanner. She found the cat, did you hear? Juno in Ring B told Mo she crawled into the duct at Junction 14 and came out with *nothing*, no bar, no dignity, and she didn't log it. Didn't log the cat. Mo says that's either the nicest thing anybody's done on this rock all year, or she's planning something. Ziva says if she's doing the vows, we should find out which before the—"

"*Lucky*."

"—wedding. Before the wedding. I was going to say before the wedding."

Hesper said nothing at all. Below her, the Mule ticked as its old metal settled in the cold.

Piq lay in the dust with the pad against her chest and felt her face go hot in a way she couldn't put a number to. She didn't know which part had done it: being discussed, being called the nicest thing all year, or *planning something* from a man named Mo whom she had never met. She wrote **Mo** and **Juno, Ring B** and did not underline either.

She stayed two hours and seven minutes. In that time two more blocks went into the frame, someone brewed a pot of something that smelled of cardamom and machine oil, and Hesper talked once, briefly, to a woman at the melt rig about *the bladders and the timeline*, too low for Piq to catch any numbers. Nobody touched the thin blocks under the blankets. The dust settled on Piq in a fine coat until her sleeves were the same color as the tray.

At 2041 Rafe Danjuma climbed a ladder to rehang a lamp that was swinging, and looked up.

Piq didn't move. Moving was what got you seen. The lamp was between them, though, and the lamp was the problem. It put a hard gold disc of light straight onto the grating, and the grating had a face above it. Rafe's hand stopped on the lamp hook.

"Hey," he said, not loud, as though he wasn't sure. Then he was sure, and much louder: "*Hey!*"

---

She reversed along the tray by pushing off the cable bundle with both hands, and found out at once that going backward in a dust cloud in low gravity is like swimming through flour. She couldn't see. She counted instead. She had come in forty-one meters, and the drawing put the hatch at the eastern end, so it was forty-one meters back to a hatch she had last seen as a dim square. Shoulders against both walls, elbows working. The dust rose and hung around her.

Below and behind, a ladder clanged, and something heavy landed on a floor too fast and slid.

The hatch was where the drawing said. The plenum beyond it was a vertical shaft with a wind in it. Station air, forced through at speed, smelled of filters and faintly of the ozone that hung over everything in Asgard that had once been water. The utility ladder ran up the wall. She went up it hand over hand, hardly using her feet. In this gravity your arms did the work and your legs came along.

Somebody opened the tray hatch below her at the moment she reached the Level 4 landing.

The landing door read **HYDROPONICS — GALLERY TWO — CLOSE BEHIND YOU — HUMIDITY CONTROLLED**. She opened it, went through, and closed it behind her, because a door left open is a door someone mentions.

Gallery Two was a long barrel vault under the grow-lights, warm and wet after the plenum. Racks of greens ran in tiers on either side to the ceiling, with a central aisle seven meters wide. The air was heavy with nutrient mist and the green, slightly bitter smell of growing leaves. Water whispered through the troughs. A man in a smock was working at the far end with his back to her.

The far end had a door, which was the other point in its favor. It was perhaps sixty meters away.

Piq had grown up on a cycler's spin deck, most of her childhood spent walking its steady counterfeit gravity behind her father and his scales, and she had spent the last two years in the full weight of L4 headquarters. She had been on Callisto for eleven days. Eleven days had taught her that a running stride here was a slow bound. You learned to keep it flat and low and to skim. She had not yet absorbed that frightened legs forget everything they have learned. Behind her, the landing door rattled.

She pushed off.

It was a good push, an L4 push, the push of a woman with full-weight muscle she had kept by habit. It carried her up, then a little further up, and kept going. The aisle floor dropped away and the second tier of lettuce went past at eye level. She had a long, unhurried moment to understand, the way you understand a column that won't add up, that she was going to keep rising for some time yet and that the gallery curved.

Her trajectory topped out a meter below the grow-lights. On the way down she had time to see that she would miss the aisle, to see exactly which rack she would hit instead, and to put her arms up in a gesture that accomplished nothing.

She went into the third tier of the left-hand racks in a slow, spreading crunch, shoulder first. A rack of butterhead lettuce gave way under her like a mattress. Nutrient solution sloshed out of its trough and came down around her in fat, lazy drops. Several heads came loose and floated away in long, sorrowful arcs. She stopped, half in the rack, with a cold wet leaf plastered across her face and her right foot caught on a drip line, and stayed there.

Down the aisle, the landing door opened.

Piq held her breath, which was easy, because she had no idea what had happened to it.

Footsteps came in: Rafe, judging by the stride, long and young and badly managed. He stopped. A voice at the far end, the man in the smock, said with enormous injury, "*What* in the—"

"Sorry," Rafe said. "Did someone come through? Running?"

"*Running*," the gardener said, in the tone of a man repeating a swear word to make sure he'd heard it. "In my gallery?"

There was a pause. Piq could see none of it. She could see one leaf and the edge of a trough, and a single drop of nutrient solution falling past her nose at a rate she could have timed by hand.

"No," said Rafe, slowly. "No, I suppose not." He waited two more seconds and then left by the far door. She counted his footfalls until they were gone.

The gardener came down the aisle at a careful walk and stopped under her rack.

"Good evening," said Piq, from inside the butterhead.

He was in his seventies, she thought, with a seamed brown face, spectacles pushed up into white hair, and the stillness of a man who had finally found the thing he'd suspected all his life. His smock was embroidered **W. ACHTERBERG — AG SEC.** He looked at her, then at the rack, then at the lettuce that was still coming down here and there like slow green rain.

"You're the auditor," he said.

"I am."

"I've seen your picture. On the notice. 'Mass audit in progress, cooperation appreciated.'" He frowned. "Is this the audit?"

"I was," said Piq, freeing her foot from the drip line and descending from the rack in a controlled two-handed lower she was rather proud of, "verifying an unmapped branch of the D-line. I took the plenum ladder, misjudged the step-off from the landing, and I am very sorry about your lettuce."

He considered that. The truthful parts had gone in so neatly beside the false ones that she couldn't find the joins herself.

"Misjudged the step-off," Achterberg repeated.

"I grew up on a cycler. Spin gravity. My legs still think they know what a floor is."

"Mm." He went to the rack, gently removed a ruined head, and held it up the way an engineer holds up a failed part. Then he reached into his smock and took out a small handheld scale. It was old and yellow, with a brass weighing cup, and an Ibis certification tag was wired to the handle. Piq read the tag automatically and saw it was nineteen days overdue for recalibration.

"Incident report," Achterberg said, "requires the loss by mass."

"Of course."

He weighed every damaged head and some of the leaves. He weighed a fragment of the trough lip that had snapped off. He weighed, for reasons she didn't ask about, a single leaf he took from her collar, and wrote the number down with satisfaction. Piq stood beside him, dripping and turning slowly pale green at the edges, and was not allowed to help.

"One point three seven kilos of butterhead," he announced. "Plus point oh four of trough. Plus that leaf, which I'll grant you was already on its way out."

"Generous."

"Thirty-one percent of the rack's tithe-weight for the cycle." He looked at her over his spectacles, which he had at some point lowered. "Which is a *hydroponic yield for tithe* matter. Which is notary business. Appendix F. And you," he said, with a gardener's terrible patience, "are the notary."

Piq opened her mouth to say *conflict of interest*. She saw from his face that he had been waiting eleven years for someone to say *conflict of interest* to him, and shut it.

She notarized the loss. She signed as responsible party in the box marked *Cause*, then signed again, and sealed, in the box marked *Witness and Attesting Officer*. Achterberg read both signatures as if they might be forged and pronounced them in order. Under *Description of Incident* he wrote, in a careful small hand: *Auditor arrived from plenum, above. Recalibrating.*

"Your scale's overdue," Piq said, because she couldn't help it.

"I know," said Achterberg. "Facilities lost the ticket."

She left by the far door at an elderly, flat-footed walk, with a leaf in her hair she didn't know about. Rafe was nowhere in sight. She had no idea whether he had seen her face in the gold light through the grating, or only a shape, or only dust. She didn't know whether he had gone back to the hangar to tell Hesper *it was nothing* or *it was her*.

In the corridor she took out her pad and wrote **Rafe: saw?** in the unexplained column, as far down the page as possible, as though distance from the other entries would help.

---

She went back that same night.

She argued herself into it in her quarters, sponging nutrient solution out of her collar with a towel she'd have to account for, as though she were reviewing someone else's claim. If Rafe had seen her, tomorrow there would be a lock on that floor, a watch kept, the stockpile moved. Whatever she could learn from the Mule she could learn tonight or perhaps never. If Rafe hadn't seen her, nothing had changed except that she'd lost a rack of lettuce and some self-respect, and tonight was as good as any night. Either way, tonight. She noticed that the argument was symmetrical. Arguments like that usually meant the decision had been made earlier, somewhere she wasn't looking, and she let it go.

What she needed was the reactor. Weight told you how big a thing was. Shielding told you how bad it was. A tug wrapped in twenty tonnes of ice for its crew was carrying something hot: a reactor run hard, a long dose, or both. She wanted the shadow shield and the drum housings, any service tags, and the serial on the core plate. If this was a theft machine she wanted to know how far it could go, and if it was something else she wanted to know that too.

At 0230 on Day 12 she went down without a work order. The mesh would log her going down and stop logging her at the cracked node, and she would deal with it.

The drawings showed a personnel door at floor level, off the Level 6 corridor, close to where the gallery steps came down. The Facilities seal tag on it had been cut cleanly and reattached with a piece of yellow tape, and there was nothing on the tape. She hesitated at that, longer than at anything else that night, and couldn't have said why.

Inside, the hangar was dark and cold. The lamps were off and the melt rig was silent. She could hear, faintly, the ice ticking as it gave up the last of the day's heat. Her headlamp made a small moving room of light. She kept it low and walked along the wall, counting meters to the tug's tail.

The Mule's drive section loomed out of the dark like a cliff. Bare tanks, old plumbing, the scorched bell. Forward of the bell sat the reactor housing, a squat drum of metal ringed by six bulging control-drum actuators. Forward of that was the shadow shield, a thick truncated cone of layered tungsten and lithium hydride whose job was to throw a radiation shadow over the crew section so that the people up front lived in its shade.

She came at it from the front, from the ice side, inside the cone where the crew would sit, and photographed the shield face and its stencils from there. Then she looked for the core plate and found it where she had feared it would be. It was riveted to the aft quarter of the vessel and faced outboard and back toward the bell, out in the open where the shadow had never been meant to reach, because nobody was supposed to stand there. The plate would not come to her. She stepped out of the cone and went to it.

She had expected the plate to be scratched off like the hull registration, and it wasn't. Whoever had sanded the Mule's name had not thought to hide its reactor. She got the serial: *TK-Δ / 2187 / 22-0419*. That made it twenty-seven years old. The actuator cabling was another matter. It was new, bright and clean, zip-tied in tidy runs, and two of the actuator housings had brand-new seal rings with the grease still white.

Her badge made a small sound.

It wasn't the chirp. It was the soft sub-audible tick it gave each time it passed a dose increment, which she had stopped noticing on her first day because it happened only twice a day, at background. Now it happened again, and then again.

She put her hand on the vessel wall. The metal was faintly warm. Nobody runs a tug reactor in an enclosed hangar. But someone had brought this one up to idle, very low, for some length of time, recently enough that the structure still glowed with induced activity. It was something like a stove after the burner is turned off.

You test a drum actuator by running it. You run it because you mean to use it.

She had taken that warmth in through her palm and through everything else she had standing out here. Each tick was a line she would not get to strike. She took the serial a second time to be sure of it, then stepped forward along the vessel and back into the shadow of the shield, where she would have stood if she'd been crew.

The ticks slowed. They didn't stop. The cone was a geometry and the hangar was not. The core had warmed its own shield, the ribs and the bare tanks were throwing a little of it back, and twenty-four blocks of ice were scattering some of the rest into the shade. Nothing in a closed room was ever a clean shadow. She worked quickly from the edge of it. She photographed a maintenance tag clipped to an actuator housing: *ACT. 4 — RESEAT — HOL — FUNC OK — D9.* That was Day 9, three days ago. She photographed the floor around the tail, where a fine dark spill of regolith had been swept into a neat pile and left. For the drum housings she needed two angles so she could compute their size later, and the second angle was out in the open again. She leaned out for it, took it, and came back.

Tick. Tick. Tick.

She had been counting the ticks. Eleven, then twelve. Each one was a small slice of her budget that she couldn't get back. Somewhere a dosimeter reader on a mesh server was patiently waiting for her badge to come back into range so it could learn the number. Somewhere behind the blue door Teo would read it, because Teo read everything.

At fourteen she stopped. That was enough. She backed away from the shield along the ice, and the ticks slowed further, then stopped. She went out along the wall the way she had come, at a flat, counted walk.

The personnel door. The yellow tape. The cold corridor, every other light strip dead and the smell of freezer ice. She counted thirty meters to the cracked node, then the first live node beyond it, a little green light on its bracket like a cat's eye in a hedge.

Her badge reacquired the mesh with its small electronic hiccup.

"Ms. Varga," said Marek Saar.

He was standing beside the live node with his back to the wall, in the dark strip between two working lights, where she would have walked straight past him if he hadn't spoken. Gray jacket, iron hair, hands folded in front of him like a man waiting outside an office. He looked as if he had been there a while and had not minded at all.

"Chief Saar," she said.

"It's Marek," he said. "I did say." He let his eyes go over her: the camera at her hip, the dust in the seams of her sleeves, a small green leaf she now realized was stuck to her collar. He looked at the leaf with real interest.

"I read your addendum," he said. "The D-line branch. Very thorough. 'Recommend Facilities node repair.' I put a priority on it for you. Facilities should have a crew down here in a month or two." He smiled, and the smile took its usual careful inventory of her, item by item. "In the meantime I've been wondering what an auditor finds to verify in a hangar sealed for salvage, at half past two in the morning, without a work order."

She opened her mouth. She had something, an answer half assembled out of a pipe, a pending survey and her own good name.

"Auditors who wander," Saar said gently, "get audited."

At her collarbone the badge finished uploading its night to the mesh, compared it with the threshold someone behind a blue door had set for it, and chirped. It was bright, clear and unmistakable in the dead corridor, a small electronic bird announcing to everyone on the network exactly where she had been and how much of it she had carried back.

Twenty-six days remained to the blackout.
