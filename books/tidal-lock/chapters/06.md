# Chapter 6: Vows

The third gap opened at 1942 on Day 13, to the minute.

Piq watched it happen from her bunk with the towel stuffed in the vent grille and the receiver's thread of antenna running up the light-pipe. The carrier had been ticking along at its usual feeble strength: short-long-short-short, pause, the stammer, the long fallow stretches of nothing that might have been someone thinking. Then it was gone. It did not fade or sputter. It was there, and then the band was empty hiss.

She wrote the time on her tally pad in her father's shorthand and drew a box around it. Then she pulled up the depot roster she had copied from the shift board outside the pumphouse, and ran her finger down the column she already knew by heart.

OKAFOR-LUND, H. — COIL MAINT., SOLO — 1600–2400.

Three for three, if you were generous with the first one. Piq was not generally generous, and she noticed that she was being generous now. She noted the noticing and set it aside, the way she would set aside a weighing taken on a scale she had not personally zeroed.

The carrier came back at 0152 on Day 15. Thirty hours and ten minutes. The same as before, as if someone kept a very good clock.

When it returned it was lower in frequency than she had last logged it. That was a small thing, a few parts per million, but her receiver's readout was honest even if the hardware on the other end was not. She plotted the new points beside the old ones. The curve kept climbing and falling in its slow, patient swell, like a tide in a basin. She considered it for a while.

Sunrise had come over the Valhalla rim on Day 14. Eight days of night had ended in a long, grey brightening, and now the regolith overhead was warming by tenths of a degree. Somewhere in the cabal's equipment a cheap crystal was warming too, and drifting.

*Careless,* she wrote. She did not write *?* beside it, which her father would have told her was a mistake.

---

The wedding was in Commons Three, the oldest public room on Asgard. It sat at the base of the original shaft and had been dug when the station was forty people and a drill. The ceiling was raw regolith sealed in resin the colour of weak tea. Someone had strung lights along the cable trays, and someone else had argued for an hour with Facilities about the extra wattage and lost. So the lights were the low-draw kind, a scatter of amber points that made the room look like the inside of a lantern somebody had forgotten to finish.

Seventy-one people came. Piq counted them as they arrived, because she could not stop herself. She also counted the chairs, which were fifty-four. The difference was absorbed by the tug crews, who preferred to stand along the back wall with their knees slightly flexed in the way of people who had spent their lives expecting the floor to move.

The screen took up most of the forward wall. On it, nine hundred thousand kilometres and three seconds away in each direction, was the bridge of the Ur tanker *Steadfast Margin*. It was a tight grey space crowded with handholds. Five Ur crew had strapped themselves into various attitudes of formal attention, one of them upside down relative to the others, and in the middle of them was Ziva Kalnins.

Piq had seen her face only once before, in a three-second clip attached to the wedding packet. In person, or in whatever the screen counted as, Ziva was tall even folded into a crash couch. She had pale hair cropped close and a long, still face that seemed to be keeping its expressions in a locked drawer. She wore an Ur flight jacket with the collar turned up. On it someone had pinned a single sprig of something green, which must have cost her a hydroponics tithe and a favour.

Lucky Mbeki stood under the screen in a borrowed jacket a size too large, vibrating. He had knocked on the doorframe three times on the way in, on the table three times, and on the bulkhead beside the screen three times. Piq expected that if the ceremony ran long he would knock on her.

"You're the mortician," he said, shaking her hand with both of his. "She said you sounded like a mortician. I told her morticians are very reliable. Nobody complains about a mortician's work, you know? It's one of the few—"

"I've read the packet," Piq said.

"All of it?"

"I read everything." The phrase came out in Teo's cadence, which she had not intended. Somewhere in the second row, she was aware without looking, Teo Anand had just sat down.

The link tech was a young woman called Ines with a headset and the expression of someone who had been told, firmly and by several parties, that this was not to go wrong. She gave the Ur camera a thumbs-up, then turned and gave Piq one. Six seconds after the first, a full round trip, an Ur crewman on the screen gave a thumbs-up back, by which time Ines had already looked away.

Piq stood at the lectern. It was an inverted cargo crate with a cloth over it, and under the cloth she could feel the stencil: MASS 14.2 KG TARE. She set her notary seal down beside her tablet. The seal was the size of a large coin, brass-cased, with a cryptographic chip and an Ibis Metrology mark. It was genuine, which remained the most confusing thing about her life.

"Good evening," she said. "We're here to witness the joining of Dov Mbeki and Ziva Kalnins—"

"Good evening," said Ziva from the screen, to the beginning of the sentence, some time after Piq had finished it.

"—under the Civil Schedule of the Jovian Development Authority—"

"Is it starting?" said someone on the Ur bridge, also late. Their muffled voice overlapped Piq's word *Schedule*, so that for a moment the commons heard *Civil Scheduling*, which was not a thing.

Piq stopped. She had officiated nothing in her life. She had, however, sat through three hundred and eleven cargo sign-offs on her father's cycler and in the warehouses after, and every one of them had had the same problem. Two people need to agree on a single number. They cannot both talk at once. Somebody has to say *over*.

"Ines," she said, "I'd like to suspend the ceremony for a procedural note."

Ines looked stricken. On the screen, three seconds behind, Ziva's eyebrows went up a millimetre. Piq had the impression that this was the equivalent of a shout.

"There's a three-second lag each way," Piq said, to the room and to the screen at once. "If anyone answers before the question has arrived, we'll have two weddings happening slightly out of phase. I'm only notarized for one. So we'll do this the way a weigh-master does a handover. One party speaks. When you've finished, say *over*. The other party repeats back the essential term, then responds. I'll countersign each exchange. Nobody speaks while the light's in transit. Are we agreed? Over."

There was a silence in the commons, then a laugh from the back wall, where the tug crews had recognized the protocol. Three seconds later the laugh arrived from the *Steadfast Margin*'s bridge as a second, smaller wave. Three seconds after that, the Ur crew were laughing at the Asgard crowd's laugh, which they had just heard, and then the Asgard crowd laughed at being laughed at. For a quarter of a minute the joke ricocheted between two moons at the speed of light, losing amplitude on every bounce, like a ball dropped in low gravity.

On the screen, Ziva waited until it had decayed entirely. "Agreed," she said. "Over."

"Agreed, agreed, absolutely agreed," said Lucky. "Over. Sorry. Over."

"Countersigned," Piq said, and touched the seal to the tablet. It gave a tiny tone. She felt, absurdly, steadier.

---

The poem was next. Ziva's single request in the packet had been a short Latvian verse, transcribed phonetically by someone who had clearly not met Piq. Piq read it out with the care she would give a manifest in a language she didn't speak, which was the only care she had.

"*Kā gulbis baltā,*" she said, "*pāri ezeram—*"

"*Ezeram,*" came Ziva's voice three seconds later. Her pronunciation was different from Piq's in a way Piq could not have described. The difference was the length of a vowel and the place a word leaned.

Piq kept going. "*—iet pa ūdeni, neslīkst—*"

"*Neslīkst.*"

"*—jo ūdens viņu nes.*"

"*Nes,*" said Ziva, a correction delivered with such gentle precision that nobody in the room laughed, not even the back wall. Piq looked up from the tablet and found Ziva's still face in the screen looking not at her but somewhere just below the camera, at Lucky, a full second before Piq's last word could have reached her. She had known where the poem ended.

"What does it mean?" Lucky whispered. He was not over. Nobody stopped him.

Ziva heard him three seconds later and, unusually, answered out of turn. "It means don't let him write the vows. Over."

"I wrote the vows," said Lucky.

"I know," said Ziva, three seconds into the future and also, Piq realized, not answering his statement but predicting it. The commons laughed, and so did the bridge. The two laughs crossed in the middle somewhere near the orbit of Ganymede.

---

Afterwards Piq would reconstruct the vows from the feed, because in real time they had become something closer to weather.

She had asked Ines, quietly, whether the link was holding. "Are we stable?"

Lucky, who had the attention of a man listening very hard for the one thing he had rehearsed, said, "I will. Forever. Yes. Over."

Ines looked at Piq. Piq looked at Lucky.

"That was a technical question," she said. "Countersigning nothing."

On the screen, three seconds later, Ziva said, "What did he agree to?" and the bridge crew lost all composure. One of the strapped-in crewmen came loose from his handhold and drifted slowly up out of frame, still laughing. The back wall of Commons Three heard that, three seconds later, and lost theirs.

Lucky's vows ran to four minutes. Piq timed them. They began with the first time he had seen Ziva, across a propellant dock at Ur three years earlier, through a viewport and a pressure suit. They moved on to the eleven reschedulings of the wedding itself, each listed with its reason (crew change, solar storm, tanker refit, a cousin's funeral, a dispute over the cake, two separate Facilities audits). He described the trajectory he had once computed by hand to estimate when their orbits would next put them in the same room. He knocked on the lectern twice during this, then a third time, then apologized to the lectern.

He forgot to say *over* at the end. There was a pause, three seconds of the room holding its breath and Ziva on the screen apparently holding hers. Then her reply arrived. Piq realized afterwards that Ziva had begun it the instant the four minutes stopped, without waiting to be sure he was finished.

"Dov," Ziva said. "I don't make speeches. You know that. I come home with the tanks full. That's my vow. I come home with the tanks full, and I come home. Over."

Lucky stood very still for the first time all evening.

Piq looked at her tablet so that she would not look at anything else. There, in the background of the shot, she noticed a panel on the *Steadfast Margin*'s forward bulkhead and filed it, out of pure habit, without reading it.

"Repeat back the essential term," she said gently, after a moment.

Lucky swallowed. "Tanks full," he said. "Come home. Over."

"Countersigned."

The questions were the formal part, the JDA Civil Schedule wording. Piq read Lucky's first and he said "I do" in two words without knocking on anything, which was a personal best. Then she turned to the screen.

"Ziva Kalnins, do you take Dov Mbeki—"

"I do," said Ziva.

It arrived while Piq was saying *Mbeki*. Ziva had started it before *Ziva* could have reached her. She had read the packet too, and knew where the question went. She had simply not seen the point of waiting.

"—to be your partner under the Civil Schedule, sharing in all—" Piq carried on, because a question had to be asked in full to be legally a question.

"I said yes," came from the screen, over the top of *sharing*.

"—obligations of mass, air and water—"

"Is she still going?" from the bridge, faintly.

"—as long as you both shall live? Over."

Three seconds passed. Ziva's face did not move.

"Countersign it before she changes her mind," Ziva said. "Over."

Piq countersigned it. She had to put the seal down very carefully because her hand was not entirely level. Then she gave Lucky his line. He had requested it in the packet, underlined twice, and she had looked up the Civil Schedule specifically to make it true.

"By the power vested in me by Ibis Metrology," she said, "and by Schedule Nine of the Jovian Development Authority Civil Code, all of which I have read—" and somewhere in the second row a short, involuntary breath came out of somebody who knew precisely what else Piq claimed to have read, "—I now pronounce you married. You may—"

She stopped, because the next word was *kiss*, and they were 900,000 kilometres apart.

Lucky put his palm flat on the screen, over Ziva's face. Three seconds later, on the *Steadfast Margin*, Ziva put her palm flat over the camera. For a moment the bridge vanished behind the warm blur of her hand. The commons went quiet, and the quiet arrived on the tanker three seconds later, and then there was nothing to delay at all.

---

The cake was a printed slab of sugared protein the size of a ration case, frosted white, with a crude tug and a crude tanker piped on top in green. The tanker was considerably larger than the tug, which Piq suspected was Ziva's influence. It sat on a trestle under the cable tray.

Piq was standing two metres from it, accepting congratulations she had not earned from a pumphouse tech named Juno, when a dark shape detached itself from the cable tray. It descended into the cake with the unhurried grace of an object falling at one-eighth of a gee.

Ballast landed with all four feet in the tanker. He looked around. He had the expression of a cat who has calculated his arrival exactly and sees no reason to apologise for it. Seventy-one people stared at him. Then he bit off the bow of the *Steadfast Margin* and the entirety of the tug, crouched, and went.

His leap took him back up past the cable tray in a long, slow parabola, frosting flecking off his white bib, and into an overhead grille. It must have taken almost three seconds. Nobody moved for the whole of it, because everybody was calculating, without meaning to, where he would land.

"That's the third time," said somebody near the back.

"Who's logging it?" said somebody else.

Clerk Fitch was there. He had come because Lucky invited everyone, and he had brought a scale, because he always brought a scale. He stepped forward and lifted the remaining cake onto his platform with a grunt.

"Two point eight one kilos," he said. "Originally three point two. That's four hundred and some grams. Who pays?"

"It's a wedding," said Juno.

"Everything is someone's," Fitch said, not unkindly.

On the screen, three seconds behind, the Ur crew were watching the feed of a cat flying through a wedding. Piq had no way of knowing what they made of it. One of them was pointing.

When she turned away from the cake she found that Teo had stood up from the second row. The dose warden was watching her across the trestle, past the ruin of the tanker. Her mouth had moved. It was not a smile. It was the place where one would be, if Teo Anand permitted herself to carry anything that heavy in public. Her eyes stayed on Piq for exactly as long as it took Piq to notice, and then went to the grille where Ballast had disappeared.

Piq wrote nothing down, because she had no pad in her hand. Somewhere in her ledger, though, a line opened that had no column. She left it open. It would not foot, and she knew it would not, and she did not close it.

---

The message came at 2117, mid-reception, while Lucky was trying to teach two pumphouse techs a dance that required a great deal more gravity than Callisto was offering.

Her personal tablet buzzed once, the specific buzz she had assigned to Ibis Metrology traffic. She stepped into the corridor outside the commons, where the noise thinned to a hum, and opened it.

ORIG L4 / PELL A. / SENT 2028 UTC / RECV 2117 LOCAL

RE YOUR 0874. RECEIVED. CABAL INDICATORS NOTED AND FORWARDED TO SIGNALS. CONSISTENT WITH PRIOR. STAND BY FOR ASSESSMENT.

RE YOUR QUERY, CAT. LOG UNDER LUNAR ANIMAL TRANSIT SCHEDULE CLASS 3 (NON-WORKING, NON-EDIBLE). MASS TO BE RECORDED AS MEAN OF THREE INDEPENDENT WEIGHINGS, NOT SINGLE. RECORD METABOLIC CONSUMPTION AS RECURRING LIABILITY, EST. 60 G/DAY PROTEIN, 150 ML/DAY WATER. DEPRECIATION NOT APPLICABLE. DO NOT NAME IT. NAMED ANIMALS ARE HARDER TO WRITE OFF.

PELL.

She read it twice. Then she read the header a third time, because the header was the part that would tell her what it meant.

Her 0874 was the first full report. She had sent it on the night of Day 8, dressed in Ibis bulk headers as a routine audit upload. It covered the 340 tonnes, the restatements, the pulses, the gaps, her theory of a roster and a cabal and a throw. In the last paragraph, because it had seemed like a reasonable cover question at the time, she had asked how an auditor ought to log an undeclared cat.

It had taken a week to get to him, or a week to come back; the header did not say which leg had dawdled. Everything was slow this close to conjunction. The light was fifty minutes each way on a good day, the relay at Deimos was a bottleneck everyone on Asgard complained about, and Pell had never in his life answered anything the hour it arrived. Somewhere in that stack of delays her report had finally reached L4, and Augustin Pell had read it, forwarded her cabal to Signals in one line, and devoted four lines of his compressed, expensive bandwidth to the cat.

*Do not name it.*

She found she was smiling at a corridor wall. She stopped.

There was a second report in the queue, the 0911. She had sent it on Day 12 after the dose consult. It covered the Mule, the reactor plate, Saar, and her conviction that the hangar was the cabal's workshop. Pell would see it when he saw it. By then, she supposed, she would have learned enough to need to send a third.

She thought about that for a moment, standing in the corridor with the dance music coming through the wall. She decided it was inconvenient. Pell's terseness was a known quantity, the lag was a known quantity, and the two together made a slow, clumsy instrument. But it was an instrument. It worked. It had just proved it worked.

She closed the tablet. The cat had not been logged. She had seen him twice now and named him in her own head on the first occasion, and she suspected Pell knew her well enough to have guessed.

---

Teo found her in the corridor ten minutes later.

"You read the whole Civil Schedule," Teo said.

"Schedule Nine."

"You said 'all of which I have read'."

"I have read all of Schedule Nine."

"Have you read Appendix F?"

Piq said nothing. Teo let the silence sit until it was obvious that it was an answer, then tilted her head toward the corridor junction, away from the commons.

"Come with me," she said. "There's something I want you to read. You'll actually read it, because it's short."

The blue door, the borated poly, the ozone. Teo's office was a cell behind the intake room. It had a single desk, three screens, a cot folded against the wall, and a shelf of physical binders. They looked like something left over from the station's first decade, and were probably Haralsen's. The cracked brass dosimeter hung face-in at Teo's collarbone. She pulled the chair out for Piq, which seemed to surprise them both, and stood behind it to work the keyboard two-fingered.

"This is Amaya's last log," she said. "From the *Ninefold Grace*. Crew badges telemetered home when they had line of sight. Mine was the station of record. So I have it."

The screen filled with a dose-rate plot: time along the bottom, microsieverts per hour up the side on a log scale. For most of its length it was flat and low, the familiar background of a tug in cruise. Then, eleven hours before the end, it climbed onto a shelf, a high plateau, and stayed there.

"Filed trajectory," Teo said, and tapped. A second line appeared. It rose with the first, but it rose for only four hours and some minutes before it dropped back. "That's what her dose should have looked like if they'd followed the plan. Into the torus, through it, out. Four hours. She sat in it for eleven."

"That's the deviation," Piq said slowly. "The low-energy trajectory. Lagrange's adjusters had this."

"They had this. They read it the way you just did. Eleven hours in the torus means they took the slow road. The slow road is pilot error. Pilot error voids the policy." Teo's voice did not change. "Case closed."

"And you don't believe it."

"I believe the shelf. The shelf's real. I'm asking about this."

She tapped again, and the plot zoomed to its last minute.

At the end of the shelf the line went straight up. It did not climb. Inside forty seconds, Amaya's badge recorded a dose rate three orders of magnitude above the torus. The trace ran off the top of the chart and then simply stopped. There was no tail and no fall. Somewhere above the frame the badge had ceased to send.

"The adjusters called this a terminal artifact," Teo said. "Sensor fault consistent with impact."

Piq looked at it. She thought about every scale she had ever seen fail. Scales failed to zero, failed high, failed noisy. They failed when they broke. They did not fail upward, cleanly, for forty seconds, and then stop.

"Impact isn't radiation," she said.

"No."

"The sensor saw something before it stopped."

"The spectrum's in the packet." Teo reached past her shoulder to pull it up. Piq reached for the same line on the screen to scroll it, and their hands came to rest on the desk a few centimetres apart: Teo's knuckles, the scar on the second one, Piq's own fingers with their flat clipped nails. Neither of them moved. The badge at Piq's collar pinged the mesh, a faint electronic chirp, ninety seconds since the last. Piq counted it without meaning to, and in the space after it she had the distinct sensation of a number she had not entered.

Teo straightened up and took her hand back. "It's hard," she said. "The burst. Harder than the torus. Harder than anything a reactor would throw at you through a shadow shield. I can't place it. Haralsen's binders can't place it. The Lagrange adjuster didn't try."

"Why are you showing me this?"

Teo was quiet for a moment. "You were in L6 last week," she said. "Near an operating reactor that's not on my register, wrapped in ice. Water, the most expensive shielding on this moon. You lied about it to my face. You lie very well, by the way."

"Thank you."

"It wasn't a compliment." Teo looked at the plot, at the line that ran off the top. "Somebody on this station is building something that's meant to go somewhere dose matters. I'm not asking you what. I'm not reporting it. I wanted you to see why I'm not." She closed the window. "That's all."

It was not all. Piq knew it was not all, the same way she knew when a manifest had been written by someone tired. But Teo had already turned to the shelf of binders and pulled one down, which meant the conversation was over by procedure, and Piq had learned by now that Teo's procedures were walls she had chosen.

"Collar," Teo said without looking, as Piq stood. "Left side. Face out."

Piq checked. It was. She went anyway.

---

She did not sleep. She pulled the station budget instead.

The budget was public, nominally, in the way that most of Asgard's numbers were public: available to anyone with an audit credential, a quiet room and a tolerance for six-level nesting of cost centres. Piq had all three. She worked from the bunk with the tablet propped on her knees and her tally pad beside her, the light-pipe throwing a faint banded stripe across the ceiling. Jupiter, fixed and low, was nodding a fraction of a degree in its slow libration.

She was not looking for the sunshade. She was looking for the Brigade's money: who paid for a reactor reseat, ice cutters, a hangar marked TEMP on the as-builts for nine years. She found nothing like it, which was what she had expected. Brigades did not pay. They borrowed.

What she found instead, three levels into Facilities, was a line.

DEPOT SOLAR SHADE — PREVENTIVE / CORRECTIVE MAINT.
FY2212: 412,000 mk
FY2213: 388,000 mk
FY2214: 0 mk

Beside the zero there was a note: *Deferred. Director's discretion. Repricing year.* The cost-centre authorisation code was Rook's office code. It was the same six characters Piq had seen stamped beside every quarterly inventory restatement for a year and a half, flattening the depot's dwindling numbers back to level.

*They lose all my tickets on the shade,* Hesper had said. Nobody had lost them. Somebody had zeroed the line and let them pile up against the zero.

Piq sat with it. Then, because her father had taught her that a number was only half a fact, she got up and went to the light-pipe. She could just see the depot's edge from there, if she pressed her cheek to the cold casing and looked down along the reflection. The sun had been up for the better part of two days. On the depot's sunward face, where every crystal of frost should long since have gone to vapour, there was still a pale bloom. It was fainter now, but stubborn, catching a stripe of tawny band-light from Jupiter.

She went back to the bunk and wrote it down: *Shade unfunded FY14. Rook code. Same code as restatements. Frost persisting post-dawn.*

Beneath it, after some thought, she wrote: *Rook covering for losses. Brigade or someone. Shade cut frees maintenance funds — to where?*

It was a neat theory. It explained the code. It put Rook in the cabal's column, or at least in its pocket. It did not explain the frost at all, and she knew that. She looked at the frost line for a long time and then drew a small square beside it, the one her father used for *later*.

---

At 0340 she rewatched the wedding.

She told herself it was for the record, because a notary should review the event she had certified. That was true. It was also true that she wanted to see Ziva's palm on the camera again. She wanted to see how long the bridge had gone dark for, and to count it. She was not sure which of the two truths was the larger, so she logged them both.

Ines had saved the full feed at native resolution. Piq scrubbed past the poem, past the collision of *I do*, past Lucky's four minutes, and stopped when Ziva said *I come home with the tanks full*. Then she did what she had not had time to do in the commons. She stopped looking at Ziva and looked behind her.

The panel she had filed without reading was a propellant status display on the *Steadfast Margin*'s forward bulkhead. It was slightly out of focus over Ziva's left shoulder, and at native resolution it was just legible. Piq froze the frame and enhanced it, which helped a little, and then read it the way she would read a gauge across a warehouse floor.

PROP MAIN — 97.2%
AUX A — 100%
AUX B — 100%
EXT 1-4 — 94–98%

She sat back.

The *Steadfast Margin* was a Ur propellant tanker. Its job was to carry water, as ice or as slush in pressure bladders, between Ganymede's processing plants and the depots that bought from them. Some of that water it burned, to push the rest. On a routine Ganymede-Callisto run, fully laden with cargo, a tanker like that would load perhaps sixty per cent of its own working propellant. It would arrive with a margin of fifteen, sell the cargo, refuel at the far end and come home. Nobody filled the auxiliaries for a routine run. Nobody filled the external tanks. External tanks meant you were planning to change your orbit by a great deal more than a routine run would ask of you, or you were planning to wait a long time and then move quickly.

SM-114 had been loitering in a Ganymede-Callisto transfer orbit for nine days. Saar's advisory called its posture provocative. Piq had read that as theatre, a Martian-aligned station rattling a sabre over Europa and the JDA rattling one back.

She did the arithmetic on the tally pad. She guessed the tanker's dry mass from the class, guessed the external tank volume from the hull silhouette in the tracking image, and ran the numbers through the rocket equation's curve. The answer came out at something over three kilometres per second of capability above a routine margin. That was not provocation. That was intent.

She thought about the 0874 sitting in Pell's in-tray. *Consistent with prior.* She thought about her fraud theory: a slow slug, thrown onto SM-114's ellipse, caught. It was weak, she knew. It had always been weak. With three kilometres per second, though, you could catch a great deal. You could catch something thrown badly. You could catch something thrown somewhere nobody was watching, because the watching would be cut off in twenty-two days.

*Throw before conjunction. No one will see it until it's done.*

She wrote it all down carefully, in her father's shorthand, and drew a long arrow from SM-114 to the Sling, and another from the Sling to Hesper's name. She did not draw a question mark anywhere on the page. When she finished it was 0412, and the arithmetic sat on the pad looking more certain than she felt, which she had been taught was a warning and chose to treat as fatigue.

---

She slept two hours. At 0630, on her way to the depot for the morning weigh, she turned the corner at Ring C's east junction and found Hesper Okafor-Lund waiting for her.

The chief engineer was not doing anything that could reasonably be called waiting. She was standing beside a pressure panel with her thermos, palm flat on the casing the way she rested it on every piece of hardware she trusted, apparently reading a gauge. But the gauge was a humidity readout that nobody needed to read at 0630. The corridor ran one way in both directions. There was nowhere else for Piq to be going.

"Auditor," Hesper said.

"Chief."

"Fine wedding."

"Thank you."

"Fine protocol. *Over.*" Hesper smiled into her thermos. Her eyes did not move from the gauge. "Learned it from your father, I'd guess. Weigh-masters all talk like that. I trained under one, a long time ago."

Piq said nothing, which was the correct move, and felt it fail. In her experience a silence only worked against people who needed you to fill it, and Hesper did not appear to need anything.

"Rafe says there was a face on the grating," Hesper said. "Up at forty-one metres. Night of Day 11." She took a sip. "Achterberg says there was an auditor airborne out of the Gallery Two plenum the same night. Fitch says you came in with five reference masses and a cylinder you wouldn't let him spin. Teo hasn't said anything at all. Teo always says something."

She turned at last. Her eyes were pale and very steady, the eyes of a woman who had watched twelve-tonne slugs leave a forty-kilometre rail at six kilometres per second. She had learned to tell, by the sound alone, which of them would fly true.

"Ninety-one spoons in Canteen Two," Hesper said. "Three hundred on the books. You found that your second day and didn't file it. I've seen your work, Piq Varga. You count everything. So I'm asking you once, nicely, while it's still just the two of us in a corridor."

She set the thermos down on the panel and let her palm rest on it.

"What are you really weighing?"
