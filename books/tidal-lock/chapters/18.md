# Chapter 18: Well Counted

The sick came at 2140, and Piq timed it. She had nothing else to hold.

It came in waves. She counted them the way she would count crates past a weigh-gate, entry and exit, gross and net. In free fall nothing went down, and everything had to be caught. Teodorescu, the tanker's medic, had clipped a roll of sealable bags to the couch frame within reach of her hand. He did it without a word, the way he did everything. Each time she tore a bag free, the roll spun a slow quarter turn on its clip and stopped. Between waves she lay strapped under a thin blanket and watched the bulkhead dosimeter. The sticker on its housing showed a cartoon sun in sunglasses, grinning. Someone on Ur had a sense of humour about radiation. Piq did not yet.

The readout said 4.9 mSv/h. An hour earlier it had said 6.4.

"She's rolling us," Teo said from the other couch. Her voice was flat with nausea, and she was holding it level by force. "Tankage to the belts. Four hundred tonnes of water between us and the old man."

"Three hundred and eighty," Piq said. "They burned some going down."

"Don't."

"You started it."

Teo made a sound that might have been a laugh if her stomach had allowed one. Piq watched her press two fingers to the bridge of her nose, then lower them and study them as if they belonged to a patient. It was the one gesture Piq had learned to read on her: Teo checking herself for a tremor.

By 0300 the readout was 0.6, and the waves had stretched to ninety minutes apart. Piq started a new page in her notebook, under the last Europa line, with a heading she didn't like:

*SELF. Onset < 3 h. Emesis 2140, 2212, 2251, 2340, 0108, 0241.*

She added a square, meaning *later*, and then crossed it out. There was no later version of this page.

---

Teodorescu drew bloods at 0810 D32. He was broad and wordless, with forearms like ballast bricks, and he moved through the little bay as if he had spent his life learning not to bump into things. When Piq asked whether the fourth tube was strictly necessary, he took a laminated card from his breast pocket and held it up. In Ur-standard block capitals it said: PLEASE BE STILL.

Piq was still.

The cabinet analyser hummed and ticked like a slow clock and printed a strip of thermal paper. Teodorescu handed it across Piq to Teo, the way a junior clerk passes a ledger to the senior signatory. Teo had pulled her reading glasses down from her hair, and the pencil stub was still behind her ear. It had come through Europa, the jettison, the rendezvous and the sick, and nobody had weighed it. Perhaps twelve grams. Piq judged it beneath the noise floor and let it stand.

"Lymphocytes," Teo said. "You, one point four. Me, one point six. They've fallen the right amount. That's what I'm telling you." Her voice had become the warden's, the one she used to give a resident her number. "If you'd taken two sieverts instead of point seven, you'd be under one already and I'd be frightened. I'm not frightened. Your marrow's going to have a bad month and then it's going to remember what it's for." She paused. "Mine too. Daily counts. Then weekly. Then for the rest of your life, someone like me is going to look at your blood and make a face."

"I'd like it to be you."

The words were out before Piq had checked them, which was new. Teo didn't look up. She lifted two fingers off the couch rail, held them in the air as if taking a pulse that wasn't there, and lowered them again.

"Ask me when you can keep breakfast down," she said.

---

Rhee was harder. He lay in the padded alcove beside the galley that the crew called the quiet room, with an oxygen cannula and an infusion line. Oduya was anchored beside him with one foot through a floor strap and one hand on his forearm, above the drip, where she could feel it run. His lymphocyte column read 0.1, flagged with an asterisk as below confidence. His platelets were forty. In the first week under the ice he had climbed the fissure twice into the open to bring his three dead down, and every one of his cells remembered it.

Teo went in against Teodorescu's card, which said NO. She told Rhee the number and did not make it nicer. They had stock aboard to support him to Ur and none to support him past it. When he asked whether he would live, she said she didn't know, and that the middle was where she had to work.

When she had gone back to her couch, Rhee found Piq with his eyes.

"Does your company still say it was Kat?"

*Claim LM-C-2213-1146. Loss attributed to pilot deviation to a low-energy trajectory. Pilot of record: K. Sefton.*

"Until the relay comes back, yes," Piq said. "Nobody there has heard anything else yet."

"She held it by hand all the way down. They wrote *deviation*."

"I have the flight recorder. I have the clause that made all of it." She touched her breast pocket, where the notebook sat against her ribs, seventy grams she had refused to give to Europa. "It isn't pilot error. It's arithmetic."

"Then say her name to them," Rhee said. "Not the arithmetic. Her name."

"I will."

Oduya looked at Piq over his chest and nodded once, as if signing a line.

---

Lucky came at noon with Ziva half a body-length behind him, one hand loose on a rail. Piq had come to understand this was Ziva's version of hovering.

"I want it on record," Lucky said, "that I have been married for twenty-six days and in the same room as my wife for nineteen hours, and in that time we have had zero point zero minutes of privacy. There's no room on a tanker. There's tankage, and more tankage, and a locker labelled DO NOT SLEEP HERE where her navigator sleeps. And the loadmaster keeps trying to weigh me."

"Priit is required to weigh you," Ziva said. "You are mass."

"I'm a *husband*."

"You are a husband with mass. He has a problem for you, Auditor."

The problem was the Ur manifest. It would not accept persons as cargo. It accepted *passengers, unticketed*, but then it wanted a port of embarkation. When Priit entered *Asgard Yard*, it pulled identity from the Asgard registry, returned *person not found*, and politely suggested *Rhee, J., deceased*.

"He's been at it three hours," Lucky said. "He's started talking to it."

Piq gave him the coordinates, sixty-one-point-two south, R-K4, to enter as the port. Then she dictated a correction of record to Ur's registrar, copied to the Authority, under her Ibis seal. Ziva confirmed that Ibis was valid everywhere they had scales. *The persons named were not deceased at the time of entry. Witnessed alive aboard by the undersigned notary.* Rhee, Wierzbicki, Oduya, Anand. Then a second list, so that column would be right as well: *Sefton, Bui, Lachance. Confirmed deceased. Remains in situ. Identification by tags, retained.*

Lucky went quiet, which didn't happen often enough for Piq to have a name for it, and left to tell Priit.

Ziva stayed. She took a slate from her flight-suit pocket and passed it over without being asked. Piq had been looking at her crew's dosimeter badges since docking, and apparently it showed. Five names and five numbers. Sabri 131 mSv, Priit 118, Halvorsen 144, Teodorescu 152, Kalnins 171.

"You're over the waiver," Piq said.

"I took the controls through the floor of the cuff. The controls are in front of the tankage." Ziva took the slate back. "It is logged. Haddad will review it. It is not your column."

Piq turned her notebook around. *PAID BY*, and five names. Ziva read them upside down, and her face did nothing at all.

"You may keep that page," she said. "You may not settle it. We said we would come. We came." At the door she stopped and touched her collar. A small brass fish hung there on a cord. It was Lucky's luck charm, nine grams, the one Piq had let through in the exceptions column of the jettison audit when everything else of his went out the lock. "He gave me this. He said you passed it."

"It was in the exceptions."

"Yes," Ziva said, and left.

Teo spoke without opening her eyes. "That was a woman telling you she likes you."

"How could you tell?"

"She stopped in the door."

---

At 2300 Amaya came in, crooked and thin, dragging one foot. The hydrophone slate was tethered to her wrist with suit cord. Her ice core was in a Margin cold locker. After a long conversation with the manifest system, Priit had labelled it SAMPLE, GEOLOGICAL (?), 0.79 KG, OWNER ANAND A., DO NOT OPEN.

Piq began unstrapping to give them the room. Amaya stopped her with a grip stronger than it looked.

"Stay. I want to see what she's like when someone's in the room."

So Piq stayed and made herself be furniture. Teo told her sister that she had closed her dose record the day after the Authority's ruling. She had written the final figure in pencil, though everything else in the office was in ink. She had told herself it was a mistake and had never gone back to fix it.

"Then write over it," Amaya said. She took the pencil stub from behind Teo's ear and put it in her hand. Piq understood it as an old move from a kitchen somewhere. "Reopened. One point two one sieverts, not including Europa. On top."

"I don't have the file here."

"You always have it in your head." Amaya looked across at Piq. "I used your grid on the band. The six by six. I thought if anyone heard it, it'd be you, or someone who'd go and get you. It was her."

"She works for the people who denied your claim," Teo said.

"Worked," Piq said, before she could stop herself. "I'm resigning when the relay comes back. There's no one to tell until then."

Amaya smiled with half her mouth, crooked, in a way that was entirely Teo's. Then she laughed, too fast and helpless, like air out of a punctured seal, and then she was crying. Teo had her by both shoulders before Piq saw her move. Piq looked at the cartoon sun and tried to be the instrument and not the observer. She failed, and she let it be failed.

When Amaya had gone, Teo was still holding the pencil.

"I'll write it in ink," she said. "On Ur."

---

D33 came in on a falling readout. At 0500 it said 0.18, and Piq kept down a cup of broth that tasted of salt and something that had once been a vegetable. She logged it with satisfaction out of all proportion to its mass. Rhee's saturation was ninety-three. Teodorescu held up a card that said BETTER, considered it, and added a small question mark in pen.

At 0710 Ur appeared in the forward feed. It started as a hard bright point on Ganymede's dayside limb. Then it resolved into a grid of half-buried domes in grooved terrain, the spindle of a docking mast rising out of a crater wall, and a mass driver much shorter and older than the Sling. Piq realised it was the first place she had ever arrived without a twenty-kilogram allowance. She had a notebook, an evidence pouch, a flight suit and a lymphocyte count, and she had paid for none of it.

At 0738 a message came through Haddad's office under an Ur header: FORWARDED WITHOUT COMMENT.

*JOVIAN DEVELOPMENT AUTHORITY. OFFICE OF SECURITY. ASGARD YARD.*

*Under Ruling JDA-SEC 2213-77, all crew of the vessel Ninefold Grace are entered deceased. The Authority does not recognise identity claims except through its own verification procedure at Asgard Yard. The Authority accordingly requests the immediate return of the vessel Mule, its contents, and the auditor of record, P. Varga, to answer inquiry into the misuse of an audit seal to obstruct lawful security operations.*

*The Authority expresses its most sincere and abiding regret for any inconvenience to Ur Station.*

*— For the Director. M. Saar, Security.*

*Contents.*

Rhee was on two litres in the quiet room. Oduya had a foot through a strap. Amaya was asleep with a slate tied to her wrist. A loadmaster had spent three hours persuading a machine to call them passengers, and the Authority had called them contents.

Piq opened the notebook under PAID BY and wrote the time. She drew one exact stroke through *contents*, light enough to leave the word legible, because evidence should stay legible. In the margin, in the hand she used for corrections of record, she wrote:

*Persons. 7. Fixed.*

The intercom chimed eleven minutes later. Ziva's voice was flat and unhurried.

"A second message from Asgard. The commissioner's office notes that the first was timestamped 2214 D32, 1802. It sat in the Authority's own outbound queue for thirteen hours. This one is from the Director's office, sent at 0719 today. I will read it, because the commissioner asks me to read it."

Three seconds of silence followed, out of habit.

"*Disregard prior Office of Security correspondence. At 0540 this morning, on the basis of the Ibis seal logs entered against the impounded vessel Mule, Marek Saar was relieved of duty and detained pending a Compact inquiry. The logs record his attempted entry into a sealed asset, the severing of its propellant feed, and the upload of navigation survey 2214.7c under his credential. The Authority withdraws its reservation of rights against Ur Station. The persons recovered are recognised as living. I. Rook, Director.*" Ziva paused. "There is a postscript. *Tell the auditor her seal held.*"

Piq looked at the pouch against her ribs, at the nav stack Saar had wanted back without knowing what it was.

"He asked for his own file," she said. "Rook just read it to him first."

Across the bay Teo had taken the pencil from behind her ear again. This time she was smiling, very slightly, at nothing.

---

Haddad met the vessel himself at mast two. He was a narrow, grey man in a formal tunic, and he bowed to each survivor as they came through the lock on stretchers or on their own feet. He bowed to Piq last.

"Auditor," he said. "Ur does not return persons. We have decided what Ur does with auditors."

"Weighs them?"

"Houses them," Haddad said, with the air of a man reading the sentence from a treaty, "pending."

Pending lasted nineteen days.

The Ur clinic was a warren under the crater wall that smelled of ozone and antiseptic. Ganymede's weak field and three metres of regolith brought the dose down to a number Teo called *rude but survivable*. Rhee got growth factors and a marrow specialist and a window, and on the sixth day he sat up. On the ninth he was allowed broth. On the twelfth Oduya slept for the first time anyone could confirm. Piq's lymphocytes bottomed at 0.9 on day eleven and began, grudgingly, to climb. She graphed them in the notebook. Teo saw the graph and told her she'd drawn the axis wrong. Piq redrew it, and Teo looked at it for a long time without correcting anything else.

The Callisto traffic didn't care about conjunction. Jupiter's moons could see one another; only the sun stood between them and home. Hesper called on the third day from the depot control room. She looked like a woman who had slept in a pump housing, and she said so.

"The pumps held," she said. "I held them. Manual valve cycling for sixty-one hours while Rook's people pulled a spare membrane out of a warehouse she swore didn't exist. The sunshade went back up yesterday at 1400. Depot face is cold. The frost's gone off the sunward side." She rubbed her face. "Ninety-six tonnes we'll never get back. But not one more."

"Two hundred and forty-four you took," Piq said.

"Two hundred and forty-four we spent," Hesper said. "There's a difference, and you're the one who'll have to write it."

Two days later a document arrived from the Asgard registry, flagged for the notary of record. Rook had signed it on her last day in office. Her resignation sat in the same packet, four lines long, with no adjectives.

*ADDITION TO STATION MANIFEST. Item: biological pest control. Mass: 4.1 kg. Origin: undeclared. Status: declared. Duty: waived in recognition of service in the ventilation system.*

Hesper had attached a still image. Ballast lay across the warm top of a duct junction in the depot control room, one paw over the edge, staring into the camera with the contempt of something that had finally been made legal and was not grateful.

Piq countersigned it. It was the most satisfying entry she had made in years.

Lucky and Ziva were given quarters on the ninth day, in a ring corridor on the third level: two rooms and a door. Lucky made Piq come and see the door. He opened it and closed it four times.

"It shuts," he said. "There's a lock. A whole lock. Nobody sleeps in a locker. Priit can't get in to weigh me."

"Priit has a key," Ziva said.

"*Why* does Priit have a key?"

"He is the loadmaster. It is a residential mass survey." Ziva did not smile. Her hand was on Lucky's back, flat, the whole time. "Dov has been offered tug work. Ur short-haul. Ganymede to the tank farms. It is boring."

"It's *perfect*," Lucky said. "I'll fly boring for ten years. I'll fly so boring they write songs about how nothing happened." He turned to Piq. "You'll come to dinner. When we have a table. We're getting a table. Seven point two kilos, I've already weighed it, Ziva says I'm not allowed to tell you the number."

"You just told me the number."

"I'm down to four percent angry," Lucky said. "You can show that as a trend."

---

The relay came back on D52, at 0311 Ur time. Piq was awake, because she had been awake at 0311 every night for a week.

The traffic came in a flood, out of order, the way it always would, and she let the Ur comms office sort it. At 0640 a technician called up to the clinic to say there was a packet addressed to her under a Lagrange header. It was long and unpadded. The Lagrange relay office had marked it PRIORITY, ALL ROUTES, in a way the tech said he had never seen before.

She read it in the corridor outside the clinic, alone, standing on Ganymede's seventh of a g with her shoulder against the wall.

*VARGA. From A. PELL, Special Losses, L4.*

*Your reports reached me in full on D41 by relay drain before the blackout closed. I have read all of them, including the one about the cat. I am answering everything at once, because I have learned this month what my habit of answering one thing at a time costs.*

*First. The Society has reversed its denial on claim LM-C-2213-1146. The loss is reattributed to the effect of Clause 14(c) on the vessel's delta-v margin. The record now states that the pilot, Katarzyna Sefton, flew the descent by hand after a radiation latch-up of the flight computer and was not at fault. The claim is paid in full to the crew and their families, with interest from the date of denial.*

*Second. Clause 14(c) is withdrawn from all Europa policies as of today. The Board has asked for a replacement that weighs shielding against transit time instead of mass alone. They have asked me who should draft it. I have given them a name you will recognise and do not yet know you agreed to.*

*Third. The recovery is classed as salvage under Clause 31 and is funded by the Society. Every tonne of ice diverted from Asgard depot for the outfitting of the vessel Mule is a salvage expense. On that basis the Society has secured from the Authority a release of all persons involved. The Bucket Brigade has legal amnesty. Rook signed it before she left. I believe she wanted to.*

*Fourth. Ur's war-risk cover over the Europa zone has been reviewed. The Authority has withdrawn its reservation of rights, and the entry is classed as a rescue under salvage. The war-risk claim will not be triggered. No one is at war. I would like that sentence in the file where people will see it.*

*Fifth. You.*

Piq stopped and read the word again. Pell did not write paragraphs about adjusters. Pell wrote *noted* and *proceed* and, once, memorably, *no*.

*I gave you orders that answered a report you had stopped believing. I did not ask why your messages had gone short. I assumed you were working from my ledger. I was not working from yours. You were alone for nineteen days because I sent you to a place where I could not hear you and then behaved as if I could. If you had obeyed me, seven people would be dead. You did not obey me. I am glad. I have never written that to an adjuster, and I find I don't know how to end it except by saying that I am sorry, and that I mean it without qualification, and that I expect you to resign. If you do, I will accept it, and I will not like it.*

*— A.P.*

She stood in the corridor for some time. A cleaning drone came by and went patiently around her feet.

Then she went back in and wrote her resignation on the clinic's guest terminal. It took four lines. She looked at the four lines, then deleted them and wrote five, because Rhee had asked her for something and she had promised it.

*To A. Pell, Special Losses. I resign my appointment, effective on receipt. I accept your apology without qualification. Please enter into the record of claim 1146 that Katarzyna Sefton held the Ninefold Grace by hand for every second of its descent, and that she was the best pilot on the file. — P. Varga.*

The round trip was a hundred and one minutes. She spent them sitting by Rhee's bed and telling him what she had sent. When she said Sefton's name aloud, he turned his face to the window.

Pell's acknowledgement came at 0834. It was one line, and for the first time in four years it was not clipped.

*Received, entered as you wrote it, and I hope wherever you go next they understand what they've got.*

---

Teo's license review opened the same day. The Asgard dose board sent the notice through the Callisto link, formal and polite. It informed Warden T. Anand that her certification was suspended pending review of a reported exceedance of the emergency dose limit, and that she would continue to be paid. It was very sorry. Teo read it twice and set it aside without comment.

That evening she came into Piq's room with a paper folder and a pen.

It wasn't a pencil. It was a cheap black-ink stylus from the Ur clinic stores. She had signed for it; Piq had seen the requisition. The paper was the Asgard dose ledger, the two pages that mattered. Hesper had scanned them in the dose office and Ur had printed them on grey recycled stock. Teo sat on the edge of the bed and opened the folder across both their knees.

The first page was Amaya's. Under the last line, ground into the paper fourteen months ago in pencil, was the closing entry. It was faint but legible, because Teo had never been able to make herself erase anything.

Teo uncapped the pen and wrote across it in ink.

*REOPENED. 2214 D52. Cumulative 1.21 Sv, surface interval excluded, to be updated. Subject living.*

She signed it. Her hand didn't shake. Piq watched for it, and she knew Teo knew she was watching.

The second page was Teo's own. Ten years of numbers in her neat warden's hand, every one of them under limit. Under the last one Teo wrote:

*ANAND, T. Europa surface and transit, 2214 D29–D32. 612 mSv. Exceeds emergency limit by 362 mSv. Exceedance knowing and voluntary. Self-reported.*

She signed that too, then capped the pen and set it on the folder.

"You could have rounded down," Piq said. "Nobody on Callisto has your dosimeter. The board would have taken your figure."

"That's why I didn't." Teo looked at the page. "It's the only rule that never lied to me. I'm not going to be the first."

"They'll take your license."

"They might. They'll look at my counts every week for the rest of my life, and they might." She closed the folder. "You have no job."

"I have no job," Piq agreed. "No home moon. A lymphocyte count of one point one, rising. A notebook. A seal Ibis may also revoke when they notice what I used it for."

"And a bad month of marrow."

"And a bad month of marrow."

Teo looked at her for a long moment, the way she'd looked at the thermal strip, reading it twice.

"Breakfast," she said. "Did you keep it down?"

"Toast and broth. 0710."

"Then ask me."

Piq had a whole page ready. She had been writing it for two weeks in her head and it had columns: dose, counts, prospects, employment, gravity, distance, risk. She had been trying to make it balance and it would not.

"Stay with me," she said instead. "Wherever that turns out to be. I don't know the numbers yet."

"Neither do I," Teo said. "I'll tell you when I know them. I won't make them nicer."

She took Piq's hand. It was the first time she had touched Piq without a medical reason, and she didn't pretend she had one. Her fingers lay over the inside of Piq's wrist where the pulse was, and stayed there. After a while they stopped counting.

Later, when Teo was asleep against her shoulder, Piq opened the notebook to a clean page. She wrote *T. ANAND* at the top. Under it she started a column for what she owed, and a column for what had been paid, and a column for the difference. She looked at the three empty columns for a long time.

Then she closed the notebook without filling any of them. She did not reconcile it. She let it stand.

---

On D54 Amaya brought the hydrophone slate.

They listened in Rhee's room, because Rhee wanted to and Rhee was the captain. Oduya was there, and Wierzbicki in his cap. Teo had a borrowed set of headphones and Piq had the other. Amaya sat cross-legged on the end of the bed with the slate in her lap and the cord still around her wrist, out of habit now.

"It's six months of recordings," she said. "The fissure was a good instrument. The ice transmits well when it's cold. Most of it's tidal: cracking, settling, the moon flexing every time it goes around the old man. That's eighty-five hours and you can set a clock by it." She touched the screen. "This isn't that."

She played it.

At first Piq heard nothing but a low hiss, like a vent at the edge of a room. Then she heard a sound under the hiss. It was a slow, round pulse, three beats and a pause. Then four beats and a pause. Then three again. It wasn't loud. It was deep enough that she felt it in the bones behind her ears more than she heard it.

"The spacing isn't tidal," Amaya said. "It drifts against the orbit. It isn't cracking either. Cracking's broadband, and this has a pitch. It repeats in blocks of about forty minutes. The blocks change. Not randomly. Every few days a block comes back, slightly altered." She stopped the playback. "I timed it for six months. I don't know what it is. I don't know what's under there. I want you both to know I'm not saying it's anything. I'm saying I can't make it into anything else."

Teo had taken the headphones off and was holding them in her lap.

"Play it again," Piq said.

Amaya did. Piq took out the notebook and timed the first block against her own watch: three, pause, four, pause, three, pause. She wrote it down in the same column format she used for everything. It looked like any other ledger. It looked like nothing she had ever written.

"The core," Wierzbicki said quietly. "Point seven nine kilos. She cut it out of the fissure wall where the sound was loudest."

"Do not open," Amaya said. "Priit's label. He's right."

Nobody laughed. Rhee looked at the slate for a long time and then at the window, where Jupiter hung low and banded over the grooved horizon, and Europa was a pale chip of ice sliding out from behind it.

"Whatever it is," he said, "it was there before we were. We just lay on top of it and listened."

---

The Compact's notice came on D56, through the restored relay, and it was addressed to Haddad and copied to Piq by name. Haddad brought it to her himself and stood by the window while she read it, his hands clasped behind his tunic.

*THE COMPACT OF EARTH AND LUNA. OFFICE OF INQUIRY.*

*In respect of the entry of Ur-registered vessels into the Europa Exclusion Zone, the conduct of the Jovian Development Authority Office of Security, and the circumstances of the loss and recovery of the vessel Ninefold Grace, the Compact has dispatched the enforcement vessel Integrity to the Jupiter system. Estimated arrival twenty-six months from date. The following persons are named as witnesses and are requested to remain available to the inquiry: Haddad-Ferreira, Y.; Kalnins, Z.; Anand, T.; Varga, P.*

"Twenty-six months," Piq said.

"The Compact is very patient," Haddad said. "It is the most frightening thing about it." He looked at her. "Ur did not send the fragment that brought you here, Auditor. I told you so on the open channel. I tell you again in this room. The codec was ours. The hand was not."

"I know."

"Then someone else wanted you on Callisto," Haddad said, "and knew you would count."

He bowed and went out.

---

That night Teo's dosimeter woke them.

It was an old warden's unit, Asgard pattern, which she had refused to give up to the Ur clinic. It sat on the shelf by the bed with the cartoon-sun sticker Teodorescu had given her stuck crooked on its back. At 0217 it chirped twice, the way it hadn't chirped since Piq had first heard it in the dose office on Callisto. It was a receive tone on the old dosimetry band, the one Amaya had keyed from under the ice. Nobody else used that band. Amaya was asleep three doors down. Her slate was dark.

Teo sat up and held the unit to the light. The screen was running the six by six, the dosimetry grid she had taught her sister when Amaya was eleven, keyed slowly and cleanly by a hand neither of them recognised. No header. No callsign. No origin. The signal strength gave Piq nothing she could reconcile: too strong for Europa, too faint for Ur, with no Doppler drift to place it.

Teo decoded it aloud, one cell at a time, and her voice did not round.

*WELL COUNTED.*

The receive light went dark. Piq was already reaching for the notebook, and the pen, and the time.
