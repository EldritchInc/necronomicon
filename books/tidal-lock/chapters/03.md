# Chapter 3: Pattern

By the fourth night the log sheet had become six log sheets, clipped to the fold-down desk with a binder clip that Piq had declared at intake as eleven grams of stationery. The pulses lived on them in her father's shorthand. Each line carried a time stamp to the second, a duration, a pattern code and a signal-to-noise estimate she had stopped trusting after the first hour. A column at the right edge held her doubts.

The signal was not doing what signals were supposed to do. It did not keep a schedule. It came in clusters of eight or twelve or twenty marks, then nothing for forty minutes, then a single short and a long as if someone had sat down, thought better of it, and stood up again. The marks were hand-keyed. She was sure of that by the second night. A machine would have been either perfect or broken in a consistent way. This was neither. The long marks stretched when the sender seemed tired, and there was a recurring hitch, short-short-pause-short, that she had begun to think of as a stammer.

She tried Morse first because everyone tried Morse first, and got VTE EEI TT, which was not a word in any language she could pull from the station library. She tried the old Compact maritime set, three commercial telemetry framings and a five-bit teleprinter code from a museum file. Then she wrote the raw marks out in a long ribbon across sheet four and looked at them the way her father had looked at a trim table that refused to close. She was not asking what it said. She was asking what kind of thing it was.

It was a person, keying slowly on a band that had been dead for four years, at a power so low that her receiver could barely pick it out of the hiss. It was either far away or deliberately whispering. On this station she could not decide which was more alarming.

She wrote *Who* again at the top of sheet five, because the first *Who* had been on sheet one and she wanted it in front of her.

---

At 1940 on her fourth evening, the pulses stopped.

She noticed the way you notice a ventilation fan cutting out: as an absence before it became a fact. She had been reconciling the depot's intake flow meters against the cradle-loader cycles, real Ibis work for which someone was actually paying, and the receiver's soft audio chirp had become part of the room's texture, like the tick of the heat exchanger and the grit grinding in the door seal whenever the pressure shifted. Then the room was quieter, and she looked up.

She waited an hour. She ran the gain to the point where the hiss became a roar and the roar became a headache. Nothing came through.

Equipment failure, she wrote, with a question mark. Then, because the sender had seemed tired, she added a second line: *sleep?* and a third: *caught?*

The third one kept her up. If someone was keying on a retired dosimetry band from inside Asgard, the obvious way for that to end was for someone else to walk in on them. She thought of Saar's smile, the one that took inventory, and of his voice saying *any talk you find worrying*, and she found she did not want the pulses to have ended that way. She noted the wanting and did not log it.

At 0150 on her sixth day, thirty hours and ten minutes later, the receiver chirped.

Short, long, short, short. Pause. Short.

It was the same opening as the first night, she was almost certain. Then came a run of new marks, faster than before, as though the sender had a backlog. She sat in the dark in her sleep shirt with a stylus in her teeth, writing as fast as her father's notation allowed, and felt something she had not felt since her first year with Special Losses, when she had found the Vesta shipping fraud by noticing that a refrigerated container was the wrong temperature on the outside. It was an embarrassing, uncomplicated delight. She had a pattern. She did not yet know the shape of the thing, but there was a thing.

Thirty hours of silence was not a fault. Faults did not end on a round number and then resume with a stammer. Thirty hours was a decision.

---

The administrator's office occupied the top of Ring A, under three meters of regolith instead of the usual five, which was the station's way of saying *rank*. Its light-pipes were wider and brought down more of the sky. When Piq arrived at 1000 on day five, answering a message that had cheerfully asked for *a few minutes of your notarial time*, the sky was a flat black with the sun hanging in it a little lower than yesterday. It cast long, hard shadows from the depot's black sunshade across the frost of the basin floor.

She had not known she had notarial time.

"Oh, you're on the register," said the aide, a small brisk woman named Odette Prew. She wore her hair in a knot skewered with two styluses and had the energy of someone who had not been interrupted by a real emergency in years and looked forward to one. "Ibis Metrology, notary public, general and commercial, JDA-recognized. It came through with your credential packet. You didn't know?"

Piq thought back to the credential course on Luna. She had sat a notarial module in the second week, two afternoons on attestation, witnessing and chain of custody, because it was bundled with the metrology certificate and cheaper than opting out. She had passed it without paying attention, the way you pass the safety brief on a shuttle you intend never to crash.

"I knew I'd sat the module," she said.

"Well, it took." Prew turned a slate toward her. On it was the Ibis register entry, with Piq's face looking out of it at a sensible angle. "And it makes you the only notary within six hundred thousand kilometers. The last one was a Compact clerk on Ganymede, and he retired back to Earth in the spring. He's about four months out by now, I suppose, eating something with a real crust."

"There's a notary at Ur Station, surely."

"Martian civil," Prew said, with the air of a woman defusing an objection she had defused many times before. "Ur's notaries can certify under Martian cooperative code, which the JDA doesn't recognize for property, inheritance or" (she paused for effect) "marriage. And ours can't certify under theirs. You, however, are Ibis. Ibis is commercial and neutral. You're recognized by both."

Piq felt the floor of the conversation shift under her the way the shuttle's deck had shifted during the last burn.

"Marriage," she said.

"Cross-moon!" Prew produced a second slate as though she had been holding it behind her back for days, which, Piq realized, she might have been. "Dov Mbeki, tug pilot, ours. Ziva Kalnins, tanker pilot, theirs, out of Ur on a propellant hauler. They've been trying to marry for nineteen months. The jurisdictional problem has been, well." She made a small gesture that took in the entire political arrangement of the outer solar system. "And now there's you."

"I'm here to audit a water depot."

"And you can audit it on all the other days." Prew smiled. "It's a live link. Three-second lag at the present geometry, depending on where her tanker is. You'd just need to witness, certify, seal and say the words. There's a form for the words."

The office's inner door opened before Piq could find a way to refuse that would not sound like a woman with something to hide. Refusing would make her the auditor who had come to the most isolated civil jurisdiction in human space and declined to marry two pilots who had waited nineteen months. People would talk about it in the canteens. Saar would hear them.

"Ms. Varga." Director Ilse Rook came out with her hand already extended, at the correct height. She was tall and spare, perhaps fifty-five. Her grey-blond hair was cut very short, the way long-tenure residents wore it so it stayed out of suit seals, and her face had been arranged into warmth with what Piq judged to be real effort. "I've been wanting to thank you. The spot-check. Twenty households in an afternoon, and nobody's lodged a complaint. Do you know how unusual that is?"

"The accordion was a close call."

Rook laughed, a beat too late, then on time. "The accordion. Yes. I heard it played. It was lovely." Her eyes went past Piq's shoulder to the wall display behind Prew's desk. It showed the depot schedule: intake windows, cradle loading slots and a column of figures for standing inventory that Piq could have recited with her eyes shut, because she had spent four nights failing to reconcile it. Rook looked at it the way people look at a door they have heard a noise behind. Then she looked back. "Odette's asked you about the wedding?"

"She has."

"Good. It would mean a great deal to the station. It's been a hard year." She said *hard year* the way people on Asgard said everything about money, low and to the side, as if Lagrange might be listening through the vents. In a sense it was. "The repricing has everyone very careful. Something hopeful would be welcome. And a wedding with Ur, of all places, shows our neighbors we're still neighbors. You understand."

Piq understood that she was being asked to perform a small act of diplomacy for an administrator who signed station messages with the same office code that had restated the depot inventory flat six quarters running. She understood that Rook had looked at that inventory just now as if it were capable of looking back. She bit the inside of her right cheek, felt herself doing it, and stopped.

"I'd be glad to," she said.

"Wonderful. Wonderful." Rook's warmth briefly stiffened into something like relief, then settled back. "Odette will send you the packet. And, Ms. Varga, if you need anything for the depot audit, any access at all, come to me directly. Not through Facilities." She hesitated, and the hesitation weighed more than the sentence had. "Facilities is very stretched."

She went back through the inner door. Prew pushed the packet across, beaming.

"Appendix F is the seal's statutory powers," Prew said, in the voice of someone reading a sign aloud because the sign required it. "Attestation of disputed mass. Certification of livestock weight for transit, which we don't have, and of hydroponic yield for tithe, which we do. Witnessing of bequests under one tonne. Custody of unclaimed effects. Impound of contested assets. Annual sign-off on the canteen spoon inventory."

"The spoon inventory."

"Eleven years ago someone declared three hundred spoons on intake and there were two hundred and ninety-one in the drawers. It went to arbitration." Prew's expression did not change. "Now a notary counts them every year. It's in the statute. You'll be doing it in the spring, if you're still here. I have to make sure you've read Appendix F."

"I've read it," Piq lied, and signed that she had.

---

The wedding packet ran to forty-one pages, and two of them were not paper.

The first was a voice memo from the groom. It played as soon as she opened it in her quarters and did not stop for six minutes and twelve seconds.

"Hi! Hello. Ms. Varga, auditor-notary, very official, this is Dov. Lucky, everybody says Lucky, you'll say Lucky, I'm telling you now to save time, ha. Odette said you said yes. I don't know if you said yes yet, actually, she might have been planning ahead, she does that, but if you said yes, thank you, and if you didn't say yes, please say yes. Nineteen months. Nineteen. Do you know how many rescheduled windows that is? Eleven. I counted. I'm superstitious about counting but I counted anyway, and I knocked on the console three times after, so it's fine. So: she's on the Margin, the *Steadfast Margin*, Ur tanker, she's the best pilot in the system, don't tell her I said that, she'll say it's inaccurate, she says everything's inaccurate, it's the romance of it. The lag is three seconds, maybe two-nine, maybe three-one, depending, and we've practiced, sort of, we practiced saying hello and it went wrong, but in a nice way—"

Piq pressed pause, wrote *Mbeki: talks* on the pad, wrote *Mbeki: knocks x3* under it, and pressed play again to get the rest of the facts out. The facts amounted to a date eleven days away, a request for a reading of a Latvian poem the bride's grandmother had liked, a request for the officiant to say *by the power vested in me* even if no power was vested in that exact form of words "because it's the best part," and the information that the groom had been born in a Ceres cylinder, had flown tugs for Hesper Okafor-Lund's training school since the second class, and was "honestly the luckiest person in the Jupiter system, apart from the obvious, haha, the obvious being—" Piq had to listen to the obvious being described for some time.

The second was a text attachment from the bride. It said, in full:

*Officiant. I accept the terms. Please do not let him write the vows. —Z. Kalnins, Master, SM-114*

Piq read it three times. She found that she liked Ziva Kalnins enormously and also could not say a single thing about her, which she supposed was the point.

She logged it all under *Cover: duties, unrequested*, and under that, because her father had raised her to see that every entry had a second side, she wrote: *Hesper's school, second class*. It was a thread, if not much of one. The depot's chief engineer trained the pilots, and one of those pilots had a reason to spend time talking to Ur.

---

The sun set on the evening of her sixth day. It did not do it quickly. On Callisto, nothing involving the sun was quick.

She watched part of it through the light-pipe port in her quarters. The disk was small and white, a fifth the width she had grown up with in the cycler's sun-side windows. It sat on the basin's western rim for what the station clock insisted was most of an hour. The depot's sunshade threw a shadow across the frost plain that went on forever, a long black wedge pointing east. The frost on the sunward face of the depot caught the last light and glittered where no frost should be. She noted it again in the column where it already was, the one headed *unexplained*, and did not move it.

Then the sun was gone, and Jupiter was the only light. It hung where it always hung, low over the western rim, a vast pale banded disk. It did not rise or set. It did not move. It simply was, a lamp left on in a room you had not been in for a long time. In its light the frost went the color of weak tea. The Sling's long trench vanished north into a darkness that, the station calendar informed her, would last eight days.

She thought about pulses being sent at night, by people who did not sleep on a schedule because there was no sky to schedule it by.

---

On the morning of day seven she went to the blue door for her weekly read.

It was not mandatory to go in person. The badge pinged the mesh every ninety seconds and the dose warden's office pulled the integrated totals automatically. But Piq had kept her own parallel log since intake, because she kept parallel logs of everything, and the station handbook said discrepancies between self-logs and badge totals should be reconciled at the dose office "in a spirit of cooperation." She wanted to see what Teo's spirit of cooperation looked like.

It looked like Teo Anand sitting at a terminal with her sleeves rolled to the elbow. Her dark hair was tied back with what appeared to be a length of cable insulation, and the old analog dosimeter hung on its cord at her throat, scuffed brass with a cracked face. Piq had noticed it on intake day and put it in the column. Teo did not look up when the door slid open. The borated walls swallowed the corridor noise like a hand over a mouth.

"Varga," Teo said to the screen.

"Warden."

"You submitted a self-log."

"The handbook encourages it."

"The handbook encourages a lot of things." Teo finally turned. Her eyes were very dark and very steady, with the unhurried fatigue of someone who had stayed awake reading other people's totals. "Mostly people use it to argue with me. Are you here to argue with me?"

"I'm here to reconcile."

Something moved at the corner of Teo's mouth and was put back. "Sit."

Piq sat. The chair was the same one as on intake day, and so was the faint ozone smell, from the oxygen plant two levels down, or from the electronics, or from Teo herself, who seemed to Piq the kind of person who would smell faintly of electrolysis if she stood still long enough. Teo pulled up two columns side by side: Piq's badge on the left and Piq's self-log on the right.

"Your badge says you're at ninety-five point three microsieverts since intake," Teo said. "Over six days and, let's call it, nineteen hours."

"My log says seventy."

"Your log says seventy," Teo agreed. "Your log also says that your background in quarters is ten microsieverts a day, flat, every day, which is the handbook figure. Your surface time on the Sling tour was forty minutes, which you've logged at eight. And at the end of each day you round your running total down to the nearest hundredth of a millisievert and carry the rounded figure forward."

"It's a self-log. It's indicative."

"It's wrong." Teo touched the screen and Piq's own figures redistributed themselves. "The handbook figure assumes five meters of regolith overhead. Your quarters are on Ring C, two levels down, outboard side. You have three point eight. Your background isn't ten a day. It's twelve point four. Your forty minutes on the surface weren't forty. The north lock cycled you out at 1258 and back in at 1347, so it was forty-nine. And you spent at least four of those minutes on the east side of the depot, where the reflected flux off the frost is about twice what the open plain gives you. That's eleven, not eight." She looked up. "I assume you were looking at the frost."

Piq kept her face still. "It's wrong."

"The frost? People mention it. Facilities says it's vent plume." Teo's voice did not change, but her attention sharpened by a degree. "Your rounding is also wrong. You round down. Everybody rounds down. Two or three microsieverts a day doesn't feel like anything. Over four months it's close to three hundred, which is fifteen percent of your two-millisievert stay allocation. That's fifteen percent you'd think you had and wouldn't."

"So I'll stop rounding."

"So you'll stop rounding," Teo said. "Which is a very strange thing for me to have to tell an auditor."

It landed. Piq felt it land, in a place she would not have predicted. It was not her pride, exactly. It was nearer the place where her father's voice lived. *Read the number, then look at the thing.* She had read her own number and not looked at the thing, and here was a woman who did nothing but look at things, telling her so.

"In my defense," Piq said, "I didn't expect anyone to read it."

"I read everything." Teo turned back to the screen and made two small corrections in Piq's self-log, in a different color, without asking. "That's the job. Everyone thinks it's the badge. It's not the badge. The badge counts. I read."

"That's my job too."

"Then you should be better at it." The corner of her mouth moved again. This time Teo let it stay a fraction of a second longer before she put it away. "Ninety-five point three. Carry that. Not the rounded one."

Piq stood. At the door she said, because some instinct she did not trust wanted to leave the room with the last entry, "The dosimeter on your cord. That's not station issue."

Teo's hand did not go to it. Piq had seen enough people protect a thing to recognize when someone was deliberately not protecting it.

"No," Teo said. "It's not."

The door slid shut between them. In the corridor Piq walked eleven of her long low-g strides before she realized she had brought her own pad up and written *95.3 µSv* in the dose column, with no rounding, and under it, in the right-hand margin, a question mark beside nothing at all.

---

The second silence began at 0742 on day nine.

She had expected it, and that was the strange part. She had done the arithmetic at the end of the first gap: if thirty hours of silence was a decision, then it could be a recurring decision, and the place to look for its recurrence was a station rhythm. Asgard had several. Its calendar ran seven-day weeks for payroll, 16.7-day local days for surface operations, and twelve-hour shifts for nearly everything. None of those made a thirty-hour gap. A thirty-hour gap was two and a half shifts, which was nobody's off-duty.

Then she found the depot.

The depot's ledger, to which Ibis had given her read-only access, contained staffing tables because staffing was a cost and every cost touched mass somewhere. The Sling and depot crews did not work the station's seven-day pattern. They worked what the tables called a nine-shift cycle: nine twelve-hour shifts of rotation through the cradle, the coils, the intake and the pumps, which came to 108 hours. Four and a half days.

She had sat back in the fold-down chair, which creaked in that unhurried low-g way, as if surprised to be leaned on, and looked at the figure for a long time.

At 0742 on day nine, four and a half days and a few minutes after the first silence had begun, the receiver's chirp stopped mid-pattern. It cut off after short-long-short. The stammer never arrived.

She was ready. She had both tables open, the depot's nine-shift roster and her own pulse log, and she began laying one over the other with the care of someone fitting a stencil to a wall.

The roster had forty-one names. She filtered by role first, then by duty pattern, looking for anyone whose off-rotation matched the gaps. The first gap had run from 1940 on day four to 0150 on day six. The second was running now. Nobody matched either gap perfectly, because nobody's rest window was thirty hours. The nine-shift cycle allowed two consecutive off-shifts, twenty-four hours at most, with a sleep shift tacked on either side if you traded. But four names had a rest block that covered most of the first gap, and the system logged them as trading the edges.

One of the four was H. Okafor-Lund, Chief Engineer.

Hesper had been off-roster for twenty-six of the first thirty silent hours. For the other four she was logged as "on call, residence," which was a polite way of saying she had been reachable and doing nothing official.

The second gap was messier. Hesper's roster showed her on duty for its first eight hours, logged for a coil inspection at the north end of the trench, thirty-eight kilometers from the cradle. Then she was off.

Piq made herself write the inconvenient half first: *H. on duty, start of gap 2*. Then she wrote the half she wanted: *Coil inspection, north end, 38 km. Unreachable. Eight hours alone on the trench.* And then, under it: *Being somewhere no one can check is the same as being off.*

Of the other three names on the first gap, one had flown out on the *Paper Lantern* on day four and could not be keying anything from Asgard. One was a pump technician with a medical exemption. One was a twenty-three-year-old cradle loader whose rest block covered the first gap entirely and the second gap not at all.

Hesper had trained four classes of pilots. Lucky Mbeki was one of them, and Lucky was about to marry an Ur tanker pilot. Hesper had paused when she said *the best I've had*. Hesper owned the Sling, and the Sling was the thing you would use to throw anything before conjunction where no one would see it until it was done. When Piq had asked about the frost on the depot's sunward face, Hesper had answered in a single breath: intake-heater plume drifting off the vents and refreezing on the shade struts, happened every local day, nothing to log. It was an answer quick enough to have been either practiced or true. Piq had left the frost in the unexplained column anyway, because plume did not usually choose the sunlit side to freeze on. But either way the answer meant that Hesper knew the depot better than anyone, and that if 340 tonnes of ice were going somewhere, she was the person who would know where.

Piq looked at her tally pad. She felt the rare, physical pleasure of a ledger approaching balance, the sense of figures leaning toward each other across the page, wanting to meet.

*The equals sign is where you stop looking.*

She heard it in her father's voice and did not stop.

---

The trouble with following anyone on Asgard was the badge.

She had turned the problem over for a week, the way you turn a coin that won't land. The badge pinged every ninety seconds. Saar could read the pings, or could have them read, which amounted to the same thing. Leaving it on its hook gave her an alibi of being in bed and a flat line of quarters background with no corridor time in it, which Teo would catch within a day and question within two. She had just been shown, with some precision, exactly how closely Teo read. Shielding it made a badge that went dark in the middle of a corridor. That was worse than a badge that logged where she went.

So she let it log where she went. She simply made sure it had a reason to.

On day ten she filed a work order with Facilities under her Ibis contract: *Flow meter verification, depot feed lines, service levels 3 through 5, ring-wide.* It was real work. The meters were on her schedule, and three of them were overdue for calibration under Ibis rules. She set the window at 1700 to 2300, which covered the evening shift change, and the scope as ring-wide, which covered nearly the whole underside of the station. Anywhere the badge put her in those six hours, she would have a meter to point to.

Facilities approved it in four minutes, with an automated stamp and a note from a human that read *ty, they've been overdue since spring*. Piq felt a small, unhelpful pang of guilt at being thanked for her cover.

At 1748 she was at the meter on feed line D-7, one level below the Sling crew's changing rooms, with a calibration rig plugged in and genuinely running. She watched the corridor junction ahead through a curve of polished pipe lagging that made a serviceable mirror. The depot crew came down from the changing rooms at shift change in loose twos and threes. They walked with the lope of people who had lived a long time in an eighth of a gravity: knees soft, each stride a low flat arc, never letting the feet leave the floor more than they had to.

Hesper came at 1804, alone, with a thermos clipped to her belt and her broad shoulders slightly rounded, like a woman carrying a weight that was not on any scale. She did not turn toward the residence rings. She turned down.

Piq let the rig finish its cycle, because the meter would know if she didn't, logged the reading, and followed.

Following in 0.126 g was not like following on a cycler's spin deck, where she had learned the trade, and not like following under the full Earth weight of the training rooms at L4. The rules here were made by momentum. You could not stop quickly. Every stride committed you to a distance, and if the person ahead turned a corner you could not check yourself without catching a handrail and making a noise. So you did not follow by sight, which needed you to be close. You followed by count.

She had walked the service tunnels twice already on genuine meter runs and had their geometry in her head the way she had once held the cycler's trim tables. She knew the stride length of a tall woman loping in low g: about one point nine meters for Hesper, she judged, from watching her walk the Sling. She knew the rhythm of the soft footfalls carried through the deck plating: the low thud of a heel and the scuff of the follow-through, almost a second between each. Piq dropped back to the edge of hearing and counted. Twelve strides. A pause, a junction. A change in the acoustics, a narrower tube. Twenty more strides. A hatch cycling; she heard it through the plating, the particular groan of an old seal with grit in it. She reached it nine strides later, read the number stenciled beside it, and followed the map in her head down.

Level five. Then level six, which was not on her work order, because level six was not supposed to have any feed lines on it.

At the hatch to six she stopped and looked at her badge. It was pinging normally, and the mesh nodes were still lit along the ceiling. She had a choice to make, and she made it with the speed of someone who had already made it before leaving her quarters. She would go on. If the badge logged her on level six, she would say she had traced an unmapped branch of the D-line, which was exactly the sort of thing an auditor with a work order might do. It was even, faintly, possibly true.

Level six was old. The walls were raw sintered regolith rather than panelled. The light came from construction-era strips set every five meters, half of them dead. The air was colder, and it had a smell Piq had not met on Asgard before: not the ozone of electrolysis or the dry mineral breath of the regolith, but something wet. She thought of ice, of a freezer opened.

The mesh nodes ran out thirty meters past the hatch. She watched the last one go by overhead, dark, its housing cracked. Her badge kept pinging, but now it pinged into nothing. Somewhere in a security log her trace was ending at the edge of a mapped zone. She wrote it on the pad without slowing: *Badge goes dark past L6 junction. Explain later.* Then she underlined *later* twice, because she had no idea how she would.

Hesper's footfalls had stopped.

Piq stopped too, which took four meters and a hand on the rough wall that left grit under her nails. She listened. There was a sound ahead, not footsteps. It was rhythmic and high, a grinding hiss that rose and fell. Under it ran a lower thump, like something heavy being set down slowly. And under that she heard voices, a few, quiet, the way people talk when they have been working a long time on something they are not supposed to be doing.

She went forward at a crawl, one hand on the wall, letting each step settle before taking the next. She had learned that it was almost impossible to walk silently in low g. It was very easy to walk slowly.

The tunnel opened onto a gallery that overlooked a hangar.

It had once been a construction hangar from the station's first decade, a vault cut deep into the basin floor for assembling the Sling's tugs out of the weather and the light. She could tell from the scale: thirty meters across, perhaps twenty high, a great curved roof of sintered regolith ribbed with old steel. She could tell from the rust-brown JDA decommission notice still bolted to the gallery rail, SEALED PENDING SALVAGE — NO ENTRY — AUTH. FACILITIES. Below it someone had chalked *ha*.

The hangar was not sealed.

There were eleven people on the floor. Hesper was among them, her thermos set down on a crate, her palm resting flat against something in the way Piq had seen her rest it on the Sling's coil housings, listening. Overhead lights had been run from a portable power cell and hung on cables from the roof ribs, so the space was lit in pools and shadows like a theater in rehearsal.

In the middle of it sat a tug.

It was old: a blunt, workmanlike hull with a stubby nose, reaction-control clusters bolted on like afterthoughts, and a drive bell scarred dark at the rim. Its registration plate had been removed and the bolt holes painted over in a slightly different grey. The hull had once carried a name in block letters, and those had been sanded off too. Someone had put a new one on the nose cone in fat white marker, in the round confident hand of a person who believed in signs:

MULE.

And they were wrapping it in ice.

The grinding hiss came from the far corner, where two people worked a wire saw through a block the size of a footlocker. The block was white and cloudy and steaming faintly in the lights. The thump came from the hull itself, where four more people with a manual hoist and a lot of patience were easing a sawn block into a frame of aluminum strapping bolted around the crew section. Only the crew section: the drive and the tanks were bare. They had done perhaps two-thirds of it. The blocks were stacked edge to edge in a rough armored shell. Where the blocks met, someone had filled the seams with slush that froze as Piq watched into a dull, pebbled grey.

Piq did what she had been raised to do. She counted.

Blocks on the hull: twenty-two that she could see, with more hidden on the far side. Call it thirty. They were roughly a meter by a meter by half a meter. At the density of pressed depot ice that came to about four hundred and sixty kilograms a block. Thirty blocks made fourteen tonnes on the hull. Finished, the shell might run to twenty. That was a radiation jacket for a small crew cabin, heavy and serious, and very nearly the number she would have written herself if someone had asked her what a tug wanted for shielding on a dirty run.

Against the near wall stood the rest, and the rest did not fit. There were ninety-odd more blocks, uncut, wrapped in thermal blankets that she was fairly sure had come from the same station stores as the two in Ballast's nest. Beside them sat a row of squat bladder tanks with depot fittings, and a melt rig she could not see the far end of. Forty tonnes, perhaps sixty, if the tanks were full. She had no way to tell from the gallery whether it was more armor waiting its turn, feedstock to be melted down for the drive, or something she did not yet have a column for.

She stood very still at the gallery rail with her badge pinging into a dead mesh and her heart doing something she did not log, and she understood two things at once, the way a scale settles.

The first was that she had found where the ice was going.

The second was that she had found where *some* of it was going. Shell and stack and tanks together came nowhere near three hundred and forty tonnes. And no tug needed three hundred and forty tonnes of anything. No tug could fly with it. The ledger that had been leaning so pleasingly toward balance in her quarters, Hesper, the roster, the gaps, the Sling, now tipped hard to one side. Below her, the woman she had built it around lifted her palm from the hull, said something too quiet to hear, and the saw crew laughed. It was not a conspirator's laugh. It sounded like people who had been working hard and believed they were working for something.

Piq did not know what a hand-shielded, unregistered tug in a sealed hangar was for, or where it meant to go. She did not know how its armor could be an answer to *throw before conjunction*, or how either one could be an answer to a whisper on a dead band that went silent for thirty hours every four and a half days.

She did know that she now had twenty-eight days.

Below her, on the hull of the *Mule*, a man she could not quite see reached up and knocked three times on the nose cone, *tap, tap, tap*, and said something to the ice, too low to hear.
