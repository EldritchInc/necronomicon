# Chapter 16: Exceptions Last

The cradle let go of them at 1612, and for twenty-four seconds Piq weighed more than she ever had.

The Sling did not shove. It pulled at fourteen g, held steady down forty kilometres of rail, and the load came through the couch as one long press, like a palm flattening a letter. Her vision narrowed to a grey tube. She counted through it out of habit, because counting was something to hold. At twenty-four the rail ran out and the weight came off all at once. The Mule's cabin went quiet in a way she felt in her inner ear before she heard it.

Something drifted past her face: the stub of Teo's pencil, turning end over end, very slowly, its eraser describing a circle the size of a coin. Nothing pulled it anywhere. It would keep going until it met a wall.

"Throw nominal," Lucky said to the board. He did not say it to them. "Leg one, three point three two outbound. We're on the line."

Hesper's voice came up on the depot channel with a little hiss of distance, still close enough to be warm. "Clean release, Dov. I'm going back to my pumps. Don't bring her back scratched."

"Copy."

That was all. Piq had expected more from him and was ashamed of having expected it. She wrote *1612 D28, release, 3.32 nominal* in the notebook clipped to her thigh and drew no box around it.

Teo had already unstrapped. She caught the pencil without looking and put it behind her ear. Then she pushed off toward the bulkhead where she had taped her tables. There were three laminated sheets in her own handwriting, gridded in the six-by-six blocks of the old maintenance protocol she had taught her sister at nineteen. Teo hooked one foot under a rail and began to read the cabin's dosimeters aloud. Nobody had asked her to. It was the way a person runs a hand over a wall in the dark.

"Cabin centre, point zero four microsieverts an hour. Flight couch, point zero six. Aft locker, point zero five." She wrote. "We're still in Callisto's lee. This is the last of the cheap air."

---

The Mule had been built to haul and had never been meant to be lived in. It was a stubby pressure cylinder six metres long, with a flight deck at one end, a reactor bulkhead at the other, and a stripped cabin between. Outside, thirty-one hand-cut blocks of Callisto ice were strapped and foamed to the hull, Juno's drip block among them. Together they made 21.8 tonnes of crust that turned the tug into something like a hermit crab. Aft of the cabin sat Tank C, a cylinder of water the size of a wardrobe. It was propellant and shield at once, and it would grow thinner as one became the other.

In the lee of Tank C, Teo had built what she called the water room. It was not a room. It was the region where the tank, the crust and the reactor bulkhead's shadow overlapped. On her map it showed as a lopsided lozenge shaded in grey pencil, its shape changing with their attitude, their distance from Jupiter and the volume left in the tank. Piq had watched her draw it on the hangar floor. It had looked like a lung.

"You'll sleep here," Teo told her that first evening, and tapped the darkest part of the lozenge. "Lucky sleeps here," she said, pointing to the lighter grey beside it. "When he's on the couch he's on the couch. The couch is where the ship's eyes are, and the eyes need a window."

"Where do you sleep?"

Teo looked at the map as if the question had been asked about someone else. "Wherever's left."

Piq let it stand. Over the last fortnight she had learned that some of Teo's answers were doors and some were walls, and that knocking on a wall only told it you'd mistaken it for a door. She took her ration, a dense brick the Brigade's galley had labelled *ENERGY (SAVOURY)*, and ate it slowly in the dark grey, watching Teo work.

Teo worked the way Piq's father had weighed, except that she had no scale. She had three dosimeters on the bulkhead, one at the couch, and her own cracked brass badge, worn face-out on her collar. Every fifteen minutes she read them, wrote, projected and redistributed. When Lucky came off the couch for a ration and two hours' sleep, she moved Piq a hand's width aft to make room. Then she moved herself a hand's width forward. Piq noticed both adjustments and the direction of each.

---

*Steadfast Margin* burned at the node at 0628 D29. They saw it as a datum, not as light. It came three seconds late on the Ur public tracking feed that Haddad had opened to them under the joint plan. On Lucky's side screen the tanker's solid line peeled off its old loiter ellipse and lay down along the dashed predicted track like a thread pulled into a seam.

Ziva's voice packet followed, flat as a ruler. "*Steadfast Margin to Mule. Transfer insertion within two metres a second. Holding high above the cuff per Dose Warden Anand's tables. Tanks at seventy-four percent.*" A pause. "*Which is full, for what we need.*"

Lucky put his face in his hands for about four seconds. Then he keyed the reply, which would reach her in three. "Mule copies. We're on our line. You're on yours. Love you. Over."

He turned his head, perhaps for the first time since Callisto, and looked at Piq. "What's the fourth leg now?"

"Three point one one. With her doing half the match. Every metre a second she carries for us is one we don't. It's her propellant and her dose."

"She knows that."

"I know she does."

Piq wrote *0628 D29 SM node burn; 74% = full* and, under it, *L asked a question*. She didn't know what column that belonged in. She left it without one.

---

The dose climbed on schedule. That was the only comfort in it. Callisto lay outside the belts, in the quiet suburbs. Europa was the old town, where the plasma ran thick and the moon's own ice sputtered off and glowed. The Mule was descending a slope, and the slope kept getting steeper.

"Five microsieverts an hour," Teo said on the morning of D30, the way someone else might announce rain. "Couch is seven. That's through twenty-one tonnes of ice. We're still fine. I want everyone to understand that we're still fine, until about 0300 tomorrow, when we cross the cuff. After that I'll stop using the word."

At 1100 a packet came up on 410.250 MHz. Piq heard it first, because the covert receiver in the hollow comms unit was strapped beside her head. A rhythm sharpened out of the static on the band nobody else used.

They decoded it as they had on Callisto. Teo read the pulse groups off the waterfall and Piq set them into the grid. Piq caught herself mouthing the letters before Teo said them.

R-H-E-E C-O-U-G-H B-L-O-O-D. O-D-U-Y-A H-O-L-D-S. D-R-U-M 3-5.

Then came the doubled final pulse. Amaya.

Teo read it three times, which she never did. Her face did nothing at all, and Piq had learned that this was not the same as feeling nothing.

"Thirty-five days on the drum," Piq said, because someone had to say the number that wasn't the worst one. "Thirty-seven on D28, less two. She's counting right."

"She was always counting right." Teo looked at the pencil in her hand. "If Rhee's bringing up blood, it's dose in his gut lining, or infection on a marrow that can't fight one, or both. I can't do anything for him from here."

"You can tell them we're two days out."

Teo keyed with two fingers: *2 DAYS. SITE FIXED. KEEP RHEE STILL. FLUIDS. HOLD DOSE.* Then she sent her own doubled pulse, the mark she'd taught a fourteen-year-old so that they would always know which of them was speaking.

No reply came. Every climb to the transmitter cost Amaya dose. Piq wrote down the time anyway.

---

That night Teo did the arithmetic for Rhee, and Piq did the arithmetic for Teo.

Neither of them said so. Teo sat in the light grey with her knees folded under a rail and the survivors' 2.4-tonne note smoothed out on her thigh. She was working out how long the crawler's shielding could hold four extra bodies before their mass and breathing cost more than it could carry. Piq floated in the dark grey with Teo's laminated sheets, doing what her father had taught her to do with a manifest someone had handed her in good faith. She checked it.

It was clean. The assumptions were conservative in every column but one.

"Teo. Your column."

Teo didn't look up. "What about it."

"Lucky's projection through the descent is thirty-eight millisieverts. Mine is forty-one. Yours is sixty-four. You've put yourself at the forward rail for every couch rotation and by the hatch for the crawler transfer. And you moved yourself a hand forward every time you moved me a hand aft. Four times yesterday."

The ventilator's tick became audible. "The medic goes where the work is."

"The medic is also the only person aboard who can read a blood count."

"And you're the only person aboard who can close a ledger." Teo looked up then. In the red night-lighting the brass badge on her collar caught a single point of it. "We each have one thing. I'm not going to spend yours to protect mine."

"That isn't how a ledger works."

"It's how a dose budget works. That's what I'm keeping."

Piq set the sheet against the bulkhead and let it float there, held by static and the ventilator's faint pressure.

"Your rules," she said. "You bent one on D28, at the hangar door. You logged it. Your hand was shaking." She chose the next words one at a time, as if crossing a rail with nothing beneath it. "I'm asking whether you've decided that because you bent a rule for them, you have to pay for it out of yourself. Because that isn't in any code I've read."

Teo looked at her for a long time.

"When Amaya was fourteen," she said, "I was nineteen and working dose at a relay on Himalia. A rock with a dish on it, eleven people. She was on a training rotation at Ur. Our mother had died the year before. We didn't have anyone who would ask about us in a way that meant anything. So I taught her the maintenance protocol. Badge codes on a six-by-six. It was meant for engineers to report dosimeter faults without tying up voice. Nobody used it. I thought it was ours."

"It was."

"Every night at 2100 Himalia time she keyed me her dose. Her whole day's dose. Nobody needed to know. She did it because I'd told her it was important. Because if you have a number and somebody's watching it, you aren't lost." Teo pressed her mouth flat. "I believed it. The badge reads what it reads. The limit is the limit. If you keep inside it, nothing on the dose side of your life can hurt you."

Piq said nothing.

"Then the *Grace* went down, and Lagrange said it was pilot error. I read fourteen-c in the denial. Minimum shielding water on any Europa-bound vessel. A dose rule. I'd have written it myself." Her voice did not rise, but something under it came very near the surface. "For fourteen months I hated them for the denial, and I never once thought the rule was what killed her. Because rules don't. That's what I'd told her, and what I've told everyone on Asgard for twelve years."

"It didn't kill her," Piq said. "She isn't dead."

"No. She's under ice with a man coughing blood, doing a dose warden's job on three people with her bare hands, because I taught her to keep the number. And she's doing it right. And it isn't enough, and she knows that, and she keeps doing it." Teo looked at her own column. "So, no. I didn't decide to pay for D28 out of myself. I decided that if the rules can kill her, I'd better be standing where they're aimed."

The ventilator ticked. Outside the crust, Jupiter's field poured past in a wind no one aboard would ever feel.

"My father was weigh-master on a cycler that never stopped anywhere," Piq said. "You've read my intake. He weighed me every month until I was twelve and could do it myself. Twenty kilos. That's what I've been since I left. Twenty kilos, plus whatever I can declare." She took a breath. "He used to say: gross, tare, contents, exceptions last. Weigh the whole thing. Take off the container. What's left is what you're carrying. And at the very end, separately, you write down the things you can't reconcile. You don't fold them into the totals to make the totals balance. You write them in their own place, so whoever reads the manifest knows they're there." She looked at the floating sheet. "I've been good at the first three and bad at the last. I've folded a lot of exceptions into a lot of totals. I did it to you."

"I know."

"I'm not asking you to say anything about that. I've spent a long time with nobody to weigh me but me, and I've only lately noticed how much I wanted someone to. And I won't watch you spend yourself on a rule that has no line for it without writing it down. That's all."

Teo reached across the gap between the light grey and the dark, slowly, as if through water. She took the sheet and erased the sixty-four, carefully, as she did everything. The graphite came away in a little smudge that drifted and hung between them in the red light.

She wrote fifty-one. She moved her crawler-transfer position from the hatch to second-in-line, behind Lucky, and adjusted the other two numbers to match.

"That's still the highest," Piq said quietly.

"It's still the medic." Teo put the sheet back. Her hand stayed flat against the laminate a moment longer than it needed to, a few centimetres from Piq's shoulder. Neither of them moved. "Exceptions last," Teo said. "Put me in that column if you want. I'll know I'm there."

Then she pushed back into the light grey, and the smudge drifted until the ventilator found it.

Piq did not write anything down. She lay in the dark grey with her eyes open, listening to Teo's breathing slow. When it was slow enough she realized she had been counting it.

---

They crossed the cuff at 0247 D31. There was nothing to see, only the couch reading sixty-one, then a hundred and forty by 0400. Teo pulled Lucky out every forty-five minutes and took the couch herself for the ten minutes that let him eat. Piq noted it and let it stand.

Europa grew in the forward window from a bruise of light into a world. It was the colour of old ivory, laced with red-brown lines like veins in cut stone. On the night side the ice gave off a faint, sickly glow, sputtered gas recombining. Nothing in the pictures had told her it was the colour of a wound.

At 0600 Lucky loaded the terrain survey into the optical navigation stack. As the surface resolved under the cameras, the ship would match what it saw against the survey and know within metres where it was. *EUR CONAMARA SECTOR SURVEY, rev. 2214.7c, issued JDA-SEC 0350 D28, checksum verified.* It was the only Conamara chart there was.

Piq had copied that revision line three days earlier and had underlined the issuer without knowing why. Surveys came from the Authority's survey office. This one had come from security, at ten to four on the morning Saar locked the launches. *Checksum verified* proved only that whoever had signed it had signed it.

She dropped her own fix onto the map. It was three numbers and an error ellipse under three hundred metres across, built from four emissions and the Doppler track. The ellipse fell squarely onto a broken raft of ice four kilometres long, seamed with a dark fissure. The survey tagged it *R-K7* in amber: *FRACTURED, UNSTABLE, NO-LAND*. Two kilometres northeast lay a smooth green plate, *R-K4*.

"I set us down on K4 and we crawl across," Lucky said. "Teo?"

"An hour each way at rated speed. Two hours on site. Inside four. Tight. Not impossible."

At 0640 the optical nav began matching. At 0652 it disagreed with the inertial solution by a hundred and eighty metres.

"Gyro wander," Lucky said. "Three days without a terrain update. Optical's always right." He let the optical solution win. The ship's idea of itself slid southwest, and the autopilot, still aiming at K4, bent the descent track a fraction further northeast to make up the difference.

Piq wrote down the number. At 0711 the gap was three hundred and forty metres. At 0729 it was six hundred and ten.

"We took fourteen g's on the throw," Lucky said. His voice had gone flat. "Could have knocked something."

"It didn't knock anything for three days." Piq turned the notebook toward him. "I've logged star tracker against inertial every four hours since Callisto. They agree inside twenty metres. They agreed this morning. The gyros didn't change. The ground did. Can you show me residuals by tile?"

He looked at her, then pulled up a diagnostic panel she hadn't known existed. It was a lattice of small squares, each coloured by how well its terrain matched the cameras. Most were green.

A block of nine tiles, centred under her fix, was red. Every residual in them pointed the same way, two kilometres southwest, and the disagreement stopped at their edges as cleanly as a parcel's sides.

"Honest errors spread," Piq said. She heard her own voice come out level and far away, the way her father's had sounded when he found a short weight on a pallet stacked carefully to hide it. "A bad datum, a worn gyro. They smear across everything, because nobody was aiming them. This is nine tiles, square, centred on the one place on Europa we are trying to reach."

"Show me what it does," Teo said.

"If optical flies us in, it thinks we're two kilometres short the whole way down. It carries us two kilometres past K4. We put down four kilometres from the fix and believe it's two."

Teo didn't need the sheet. "Four out, four back. Four hours in the crawler. Nothing left on site."

"We'd find out halfway across, when the ground stopped agreeing with the map. Out there, under five sieverts a day."

Lucky looked at the issuer line. "JDA-SEC."

"Security doesn't survey anything," Piq said. "It countersigns."

He said the name once, the way you'd read a figure off a scale. "Saar."

"Three witnesses," Piq said. "Gyros, stars, my emission fix. They agree with each other, and the green tiles agree with all of them. One document disagrees, in nine squares, around the people it would be convenient to lose. You don't throw out three receipts because one invoice says otherwise."

Lucky masked the nine tiles out of the solution by hand. The ship's estimate jerked six hundred metres back and the descent track straightened. He killed the downrange velocity and held them in a hover over the chaos while the inertial re-solved against star fixes and the green tiles at the block's margins. Piq read radar altitude aloud every five seconds. The board counted propellant out of the landing reserve in steady digits.

"Could I put her on the fix?" Lucky said, half to himself. Below them the raft was a puzzle of grey blocks and black seams. "No. I've got no hazard map I trust in those tiles. Maybe the NO-LAND is a lie. Maybe the cracks aren't. Amaya's said for weeks that the ice is moving. I'm not setting forty tonnes on a slab I can only see through a window, over people living under it." He slid them northeast onto ground the cameras and the green tiles agreed on.

The Mule touched down on R-K4 at 0758 D31. Europa's gravity came back faintly, a little more than Callisto's, and every loose thing in the cabin settled.

"Hover cost thirty-eight metres a second of reserve," Lucky said.

Piq wrote it in its own line.

Teo was already at the crawler hatch with her sheet. "Two kilometres. Crawler interior runs about nine millisieverts an hour at this flux. Outside it's two hundred and nine. An hour each way is eighteen millisieverts each, before we're on site. Two hours there is another eighteen. That's thirty-six on top of the descent, and that's the cap. The clock starts when the hatch seals."

It sealed at 0815.

---

The crawler was a low box on four tracked pods with a water jacket thirty centimetres thick, and it moved as though it disliked every metre. Lucky drove. Teo called time and dose at every quarter hour. Piq watched the inertial and the star tracker and nothing else.

Conamara was not a plain. It was a field of shattered plates, some the size of buildings, tilted and refrozen in a matrix of jumbled ice, ridged and pitted. The crawler climbed onto a block, nosed off it and climbed again. Jupiter sat on the horizon, cut in half by it, enormous and banded, throwing ochre light across frost that glittered for a moment and went dark. The exterior counter never dropped below two hundred.

"Zero eight forty-five. Four point six," Teo said. Later: "Zero nine hundred. Six point nine."

At 0912 the fissure opened ahead of them, a black seam three metres wide running into the raft. Wedged twenty metres down it, tilted forty degrees and furred white, lay the forward section of the *Ninefold Grace*. Someone had strung a line of chemical lights down the wall, and two still glowed. A soft-collar adaptor the Brigade had built for exactly this hull stood folded at the crawler's nose. Lucky eased the crawler to the lip and paid it down. Piq listened to the collar hiss as it mated with the *Grace*'s dorsal lock.

Teo went second, behind Lucky, as she had written it.

Inside, the air was cold enough to hurt, and it smelled of scorched insulation and of people kept too long in one place. The cabin had been walled into a burrow with bagged ice and stowage panels. In the middle of it a man lay strapped to a cot, grey-faced, with a stained cloth folded under his chin: Rhee. A tall woman with her hand flat on his chest was Oduya. A wiry man was already standing and reaching for a bag, as though he had been packed for months: Wierzbicki.

And a small figure crouched at the far wall beside a radio rig, hair cropped close, a dosimeter badge pinned face-out on her collar.

Amaya stood up slowly. She didn't speak. She lifted two fingers and tapped them twice against the visor of Teo's helmet, the doubled pulse.

Teo said her name once. Then she did what she had trained her whole life to do. She took her sister's badge between gloved fingers, tilted it to the light and read it. Her lips moved on the number. She let it go, and her hands closed on Amaya's shoulders, and she held on without a sound while Amaya pressed her forehead to the faceplate. Neither of them moved for eleven seconds. Piq counted them and wished she hadn't.

"Rhee first," Teo said, and her voice was entirely steady. "Fluids, then the litter."

---

The hull groaned.

It was not a loud sound. It came through the deck plates into Piq's boots, a long settling, like a heavy man shifting in his sleep. A strut by the radio rig was scored with chalk marks and dates in Amaya's small, square hand.

"The walls," Amaya said, following her eyes. Her voice was hoarse from disuse. "Forty centimetres since we came down. Nine in the last week. It's speeding up." She looked at the bulkhead as if she could see through it. "Another month and it closes on us."

Above the litter the overhead had bowed visibly inward. A seam of ice had pushed through a panel join and frozen there, glassy.

"Then we leave now," Teo said.

"One thing first." Amaya caught Piq's sleeve. She had been told who Piq was, as far as a dozen pulse groups could tell her. "You're the one who counts."

She pulled a scuffed data slate from the rig. A cable ran from it down through a bore in the floor, melted through a metre of ice into whatever lay below. "Hydrophone. The fissure goes deeper than the hull. There's brine down there, and past the brine, I think, water." She keyed playback.

First came cracking, the snap and rumble of a moon flexing in Jupiter's grip, broadband and irregular. Piq had heard recordings of it. Under the cracking ran something else: a low tone that pulsed, stopped, pulsed. It was grouped. She found herself counting. Four, a gap, four, a gap, seven. Then the whole sequence again, a little shifted, as if the second pass were an answer to the first.

"Tidal cracking follows the orbit," Amaya said. "Three and a half days. It's random inside that. This isn't. It repeats at night. Every night. Shorter intervals nested inside longer ones." She stopped the playback. "I've been the only one listening for fourteen months. I needed someone else to have heard it."

"I heard it," Piq said. It was the truest line she had written in a while. She didn't know what column it went in either.

"Later," Teo said gently, and Amaya nodded and closed the slate. She slid it into her suit with a wrapped cylinder about the length of a forearm, which she did not explain.

"Zero nine forty," Teo said. "Clock's running."

---

They moved Rhee through the collar strapped to the litter. Wierzbicki and Oduya followed, then Amaya, and the crawler, which had been tight for three, became a press of knees and helmets and breath. Lucky backed off the lip. Piq braced herself against the jacket wall with the notebook on her knee and did the thing she had come to do.

She did it in her father's order. Gross: the Mule as it sat on K4, less the thirty-eight metres a second the hover had spent. Tare: hull, crawler, crust, Tank C at its present level. Contents: seven people now, not three. There were four bodies, four suits, Rhee's litter and fluids, Wierzbicki's bag, and whatever Amaya was carrying in her suit. She weighed them by estimate and then by the survivors' own 2.4-tonne note, struck through where the note had counted things the crawler could not take.

She ran the fourth leg against it: ascent, then the climb to Ziva's line, with *Steadfast Margin* doing half the match from high above the cuff.

Three point one one needed. Three point zero four available.

She ran it again from the top, the way you reweigh a pallet you don't believe. It came out the same.

"Piq," Teo said, without turning. "What's the number."

"Seventy metres a second short." Piq heard her own voice come back off the jacket wall. "At our mass ratio that's eleven hundred and forty kilograms we can't lift to her line. With all four aboard, the Mule is too heavy to meet the tanker."

The crawler ground over the first ridge toward K4. In the jacket around them, and in the crust strapped to the waiting tug, the only thing aboard heavy enough to make up the difference was the ice that was keeping them alive.

"Ten hundred," Teo said. "Eighteen point four."
