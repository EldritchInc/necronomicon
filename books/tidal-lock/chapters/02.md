# Chapter 2: The Ledger

The depot ledger was honest, and that was the problem with it.

Piq had spent the small hours reconciling it by hand. She had gone to sleep with the number 340 sitting in her chest like a swallowed stone. She woke at 0600 station convention with the stone still there and the sun exactly where she had left it, a third of the way up a black sky. Callisto did not do mornings. It did shifts.

She read the ledger again over reconstituted tea that tasted of the electrolysis plant, faintly metallic and faintly of struck matches. Every entry carried a timestamp, a scale ID, an operator code and a countersignature. The scales had been calibrated on schedule. Ibis had certified them, and therefore so had she, in a sense, as a member of the profession. Each quarter the inflows from the regolith processors matched the outflows to the Sling cradle and the propellant farm within tolerance. Then each quarter the standing inventory, the great buried mass of the depot itself, came out forty to seventy tonnes lighter than the arithmetic said it should. Someone had then quietly restated it to match last quarter's figure.

Six times. Flat as a tabletop. Nobody lies with a flat line by accident.

She wrote in the margin of her tally pad, in her father's shorthand: *Books clean, pile short. Not a scale fault. Someone restates. Who signs the restate?* She checked. The restatements carried the Director's office code and no name. She added a question mark to the column that already held the frost, the dose warden and Pell's tone. Then she put the pad in her thigh pocket and went to do the job she was actually being paid for. That was the job that would give her reasons to be everywhere.

---

The pads were a set of pressurized bays cut into the basin rim. Each opened onto the surface through a lock wide enough to swallow a tug's cargo frame. Piq's Ibis schedule had her verifying outbound manifests on Lagrange-rated cargo: weigh the frame, check the declared mass against the rated mass, sign or don't. The work was boring and precise and almost entirely real. She liked it more than she would have admitted to anyone at L4.

Bay Four held a survey-support tug called the *Paper Lantern*, licensed by the JDA for a run to Europa orbit with relay buoys and a crew of three. Its cargo frame stood on the scale deck under the work lights. Strapped around the crew can were six blue bladders, each the size of a sleeping person, sweating faintly in the cold.

"Twenty-two point four tonnes of water," said the captain, a woman named Galloway with a shaved head and a voice that had been sanded down by years of shouting over pumps. "Twenty-two point four tonnes I can't drink, can't burn, and can't sell. Go on. Weigh it. Weigh it twice. It doesn't get lighter."

Piq weighed it. The deck scale read the frame at 61.218 tonnes. She checked the bladders individually against their tags with the handheld load cell, walking the cell from strap to strap while the mate held the bladders steady. At 0.126 g a person could shoulder a tonne. Steadying it was another matter, because a tonne swinging at walking pace still wanted to keep going.

"It's the clause," said the mate, Szabo, to nobody in particular. He was young and thick-wristed and had the low-g build that ran to height. "It's always the clause."

"Lagrange's water," Galloway said. "That's what we call it. Lagrange's water. You know what it costs us? I put that on the frame and I lose, what, Pritt?"

The engineer didn't look up from her tablet. "Two-eighty, two-ninety meters a second on the outbound leg. More on the return if we burn heavy to make the window."

"Two hundred and ninety meters a second," Galloway told Piq, as if Piq had personally carried each bladder aboard. "So we run soft. Lower energy, longer transit. And the longer we're in there—"

"The more water the schedule wants," Pritt said. "Yes."

Piq finished the last strap, noted the figure, and lowered the load cell. She could have let it go. Auditors who argue get remembered. But she had been raised in the weigh-master's office of the *Long Patience*, with the trim tables on the bulkhead. Some things she had learned before she learned to lie, and they came out of her the way her father's sayings did, whole and without asking.

"Clause Fourteen, sub c," she said. "Any hull rated under the Society and bound for the Europa environment shall carry shielding water of not less than the scheduled minimum for its crew complement. The minimum scales with projected transit time inside the torus. The water is to be disposed about the crew volume and is not to be counted as propellant reserve."

Szabo looked at her the way people look at a dog that has started reciting poetry.

"The schedule wasn't drawn up in an afternoon," Piq went on. "It's forty years of crew dose data off every hull Lagrange has ever rated in the Jovian system. You get about five sieverts a day on Europa's trailing hemisphere. Inside the torus, in transit, it's less, but you're there for days and it adds. Two hundred and ninety meters a second is a cost. A crew with marrow failure is a total loss." She paused. "And it's not to be counted as propellant, because the first thing a captain does when she's short of delta-v is burn her shielding. The clause assumes you'll be tempted. It's right to."

There was a short silence. A drop of condensation slid off the nearest bladder and fell toward the deck. It took long enough that Piq could have counted it in.

"She's got the whole thing by heart," Szabo said.

"She's an auditor," said Galloway. "They don't have hearts, they have schedules." She signed the manifest anyway, hard, so the stylus squeaked. "Water keeps you alive. Fine. Tell that to the meters per second."

"I'll put it in my report," Piq said, and made a note. The note read only *Paper Lantern, Bay 4, 61.218 t, in tolerance.* She believed the clause, the way she believed in the mass of a kilogram. The grumbling was what crews did with numbers they couldn't change. Her father had heard it every day for thirty-one years. *They'll argue with the scale, Piq. Let them. The scale doesn't care.*

---

The comms office sat one level below the pads, in a bay that smelled of warm electronics and somebody's lentils. Piq had two reasons to be there. Ibis audit uploads went out through station bandwidth, so she had to register her traffic class. And she needed to see the shape of the pipe she would be talking down for the next five weeks.

The duty tech was a round, delighted man named Benno Lisk, who introduced himself while still eating.

"Ibis! Bulk class, then. Routine." He spooned lentils with one hand and tapped her into the system with the other. "You'll want to get the heavy stuff out early. Has anybody told you about the relay?"

"Tell me about the relay," Piq said, because chatty people were a gift and you didn't refuse gifts.

Lisk put down the spoon. "Right now we've got a clean line to Earth-Luna, forty-five, forty-six minutes one way, getting longer. Jupiter's sliding round toward the far side of the Sun from home, yes? Line of sight gets closer and closer to the solar disc, and the Sun is very loud. Over the next couple of weeks it gets noisy enough that we stop talking straight to Earth and start bouncing everything through Mars."

"The store-and-forward relay."

"Deimos Relay, God bless it. They take everything from everybody out here, us, Ur, the Trojans, every tug with a transmitter. They stack it up, and when their window to Earth opens they send it. But they don't send it in the order it came in." He held up a lentil-flecked finger. "They sort. Priority first: JDA flagged, Compact flagged, medical, safety of life. Then small packets ahead of big ones, because they can fit small ones into the gaps. Big fat routine bulk goes when there's room. And the closer we get to conjunction, the less room there is."

"And then it goes dark."

"Nineteen days." He said it with relish, the way people say the number of stitches they once had. "No line. Sun's right in the way. Deimos holds what it's got and we hold what we've got, and everybody plays cards. Your last week before blackout, a big upload might sit at Deimos four, five days before it goes. Might sit right through blackout. Nobody minds. Who's in a hurry for a calibration table?"

"Nobody," Piq said, and meant it in two ways.

Every covert report she sent would go out the way Lagrange had taught her. She would encrypt it by hand against a one-time crypto pad, then wrap it inside forty or fifty megabytes of perfectly genuine Ibis bulk: scale drift logs, temperature compensation curves, the full certification history of every load cell in the depot. Nobody on Callisto would bother to open that, and nobody at Deimos would bother to prioritize it. The padding made the reports invisible. That was the point. She filed Lisk's explanation in the part of her mind where cover details lived. *Bulk goes late. Good. Late is quiet.*

"Bulk class," she said. "Routine. Thank you."

"Ibis bulk class," said a voice behind her. "The most reassuring words in the language."

She turned at an unhurried pace. She knew he had chosen the moment.

He was in his fifties, pale in the way of people who were born in a spin habitat and never browned since. He was slim, with a grey JDA security jacket cut well enough that someone had paid to have it cut. His hair was close-cropped iron. His face was pleasant and his eyes were not quite. He held out a hand at a height that made her lean slightly to take it.

"Marek Saar. I run security for the Authority here. I like to meet everyone who comes down on the *Interval*. It's a small station. I'd hate to be a stranger."

"Piq Varga. Ibis Metrology."

"I know." He smiled. Piq had sat across from loss adjusters, fraud investigators and two customs services. She had met very few smiles that took inventory. This one did. It went to the Ibis seal on her lapel and the dosimeter badge clipped to her collar. It went to the tally pad in her thigh pocket, the scuff of regolith on her left boot and the slight hollow in her right cheek where she bit it when she was thinking. Then it came back to her eyes, having logged everything and countersigned it.

"I saw your intake," he said. "Five reference masses. A full set. We rarely get that level of care."

"The depot scales are overdue for an independent check," Piq said. "Ibis likes its auditors to bring their own truth."

"Do they." He seemed pleased. "I've always liked auditors. You write everything down. It makes my job so much easier." He let a beat pass. "You're on the depot, the pads, and the Sling, I understand. Anything farther afield?"

"Farther afield than the Sling?"

"Some of our visitors develop an interest in the inner moons." He said it lightly, as though they were talking about a cold. "So, for the avoidance of doubt: Europa is a JDA exclusion zone under the Compact claim of 2209. Nothing lands, nothing loiters, nothing transmits without an Authority license. If you hear anyone talking about Europa in a way that worries you, I'd be very grateful to know about it."

"I'm a mass auditor, Mr. Saar. The only thing I want to know about Europa is how much it weighs."

"Four point eight times ten to the twenty-two kilograms," Saar said. "Give or take. And it's Marek." He tapped two fingers on Lisk's console. The tech, who had been chewing very quietly, stopped altogether. "Your reference masses. I'm sure they're exactly what the attestation says. I'd love to see them sometime. Professional curiosity."

"Any time," said Piq. "They're presented for inspection on demand."

"So they are." The smile again, a little warmer, which was worse. "Welcome to Asgard."

He left. Lisk breathed out and resumed his lentils with the air of a man who had just watched a large animal pass by without stopping to eat him.

"He does that," Lisk said. "Meets everybody. It's very friendly."

"Very," said Piq.

She went back to her quarters by the long way, and on the way she took apart, in her head, the fifth of her five reference masses: the one-kilogram steel cylinder that was not a reference mass at all. Its end cap came off with a quarter-turn and a reverse quarter-turn, on threads cut fine enough to pass for a machining seam. Inside it was hollow, and the hollow held Lagrange's kit: the burst transceiver, the folded antenna lead, the crypto pad, the wideband receiver. Hollow steel weighs less than solid steel, so the cylinder had been made a finger's width taller than a true one-kilogram billet. A collar of tungsten shot packed around the cavity made up the difference. On a calibrated balance it would weigh exactly one kilogram, which was all the intake officer had checked. A densitometer did not care what a thing weighed. It mapped what a thing was made of, slice by slice, and a real reference mass read as one uniform block of steel. This one would show a dense ring of tungsten, an empty core and a tidy little constellation of electronics. Hold it up to a densitometer and it would light up like a festival.

*Presented for inspection on demand.* She had signed that three times.

She added a fifth line to the question-mark column: *Saar. Knows the attestation by heart.*

---

Hesper Okafor-Lund met her at the north lock at 1300 with two crawler suits and a thermos.

The chief engineer of the Sling was somewhere past sixty. She was broad through the shoulders and carried herself as though she had spent decades lifting things in gravities that made lifting dishonest. Her white hair was cropped to stubble and she had a quiet voice. Piq soon learned that she never raised it, because she never had to. People leaned toward Hesper to hear her.

"You've seen it from the shuttle," Hesper said as they suited up. "That's nothing. From the shuttle it's a line."

The crawler was an open-frame rover with a pressurized cab the size of a coffin for two. Hesper drove it with one hand along a graded track. On both sides the regolith of the Valhalla basin lay dark and churned, old impact ejecta beaten flat by four billion years and a decade of construction. Jupiter hung where it always hung, low over the western rim, banded in rust and cream and a pale ochre. It was enormous and perfectly still, a door that had been painted onto the sky. Its light fell on the frost at the edges of the track and turned it the color of weak tea.

Then the ground fell away on their left, and there was the Sling.

It ran north, a straight-edged trench cut into the basin floor. It was so straight that the eye kept trying to find where it bent, and it never bent. Ring after ring of coil housings marched along it, each a squat grey torus bolted to a sintered regolith footing. The gap between them shrank with distance until they blurred into a single bright seam that met the horizon. Forty kilometers, Hesper said. Piq knew the number already. Knowing it was different from seeing a straight line run over the edge of the world.

"Twelve-tonne slugs," Hesper said. She stopped the crawler at a pullout above the cradle end, where a gantry stood over the first coils. "Ice, pressed and frozen, in a sintered shell. The shell takes the acceleration and spalls off on the way out, like a seed husk. We throw them at six point one kilometers a second. It's ugly to stand next to and very beautiful to watch from here."

"What's the acceleration?"

"Forty-six g, peak." Hesper poured tea from the thermos into a bulb and passed it over. "Ice doesn't mind. Ice is the most patient thing in the solar system. A slug we throw this month gets shepherded by a tug at Jupiter departure. Then it falls inward for two years, three, four, depending on the customer. Somebody at Ceres or Vesta or a Martian cooperative catches it and drinks it, or breathes it, or burns it, or wraps it around a ship. We'll never meet them." She drank. "I like that. It's like writing letters."

Piq watched her face. She was used to people talking about their work as a job, or a cost, or a grievance. Hesper talked about the Sling the way her father had talked about the *Long Patience*'s trim, as a living thing you kept in balance by paying attention.

"The crews are young," Hesper said, and nodded toward the gantry. Figures in suits moved there, slow and deliberate, walking with the long careful glide of people who had learned low g properly. "Most of them came up through my school. Loading, coil alignment, cradle work, then pilots. I've trained four classes of tug pilots off this pad." She paused, briefly, the way a person pauses on a stair they know is uneven. "Good ones. The best I've had. You could hand them a bad trajectory and a worse tug and they'd bring it back."

"You keep track of them."

"I keep track of everybody I've ever sent out of the cradle." Hesper looked at her, and for a moment the quiet voice had something under it like rock under regolith. "That's what the job is, auditor. Everything you throw, you're responsible for where it lands."

Piq said nothing, and made a note of the nothing.

They drove the first two kilometers of the trench at walking pace. Hesper named coil sections the way other people name children's friends. She stopped twice to put a gloved hand flat against a housing and wait, listening through her palm for something Piq couldn't hear. On the drive back she pointed out the depot: the low terraced mound of the ice pile, its vast black sunshade canted overhead like a wing. Piq looked for the frost on the east face and found it, a thin pale crust where the sun got in under the shade's lower edge.

"That frost," she said. "On the sunward side."

Hesper glanced at it the way she might have glanced at a stranger's coil housing, interested but not responsible. "Out-gassing condenses wherever it finds a cold surface."

"It's the sunward side."

"Hm." Hesper squinted, and her free hand made a small flattening gesture against the dash, the same one she had made against the coils. "Then the shade's not seating at the lower edge and you're getting sun on the face. Thermal cycling, probably. That wing belongs to Facilities, not me." She drove on. "I'll put in a ticket. They'll lose it. They lose all my tickets on the shade."

"All of them?"

"It's a big wing, auditor. It doesn't throw anything, so nobody loves it." She said it with an engineer's mild contempt for any machine that wasn't hers, and moved on to the cradle's coolant loop.

Piq didn't push. She noted that the chief engineer of the Sling had not known about the frost, had diagnosed it in under ten seconds, and had then dismissed it as somebody else's problem. She also noted *They lose all my tickets on the shade*. Two clean facts from the same answer, and neither of them explained a damned thing yet.

---

The afternoon was where the station punished her for being competent.

There was a message waiting when she came in from the lock. It came from the Director's office in an upbeat tone that suggested a staffer had been told to be nice to the Ibis auditor. Asgard's annual residential mass declaration spot-check was due. The JDA's own assessor had rotated out to Ganymede. Since a certified Ibis auditor was conveniently on station, would she be so kind? Twenty households, random selection, standard schedule. The Director would be *so grateful*. The message was signed with the office code, the same code that had restated the depot six times in a row, and with a name: *Ilse Rook, Director.*

It was not part of her contract. Refusing would have been perfectly legal and a little odd. Piq took the handheld densitometer out of her Ibis case and went door to door through Ring B.

Asgard hid mass the way old ships hid rust: everywhere, a little at a time, and with great ingenuity.

The first household declared 211 kg of personal effects. Piq's sweep found 214. The difference was a hollow ballast brick on the windowsill, holding four hundred grams of saffron in a vacuum sleeve. The owner was a soft-spoken processing tech. He explained that the brick was *decorative*. The saffron, he said, had been a gift from his mother, who had died. Piq logged it at the spice rate. He paid, and then he gave her a pinch, which she did not log.

The second household had an accordion.

It was not on any manifest. It had not been on the shuttle manifest that brought its owner up from Ganymede six years ago. By its owner's own admission it had come down from orbit inside a hollowed-out spare oxygen candle. It weighed 9.2 kg, which at the personal tariff was a little under four thousand marks.

"It's a family instrument," said its owner. She was a large, red-faced woman who ran the hydroponics night shift. She said it with her arms folded across the accordion as though Piq might try to repossess it on the spot.

"It's still nine point two kilograms," Piq said.

"It's my grandmother's nine point two kilograms."

"I don't doubt it. I have to log it."

"Log it as *ballast*," the woman said, and then, with dawning cunning, "It's *trim*. For the ring. You need mass distributed. I'm distributing it."

Piq considered this. It was very nearly a good argument. "The ring has a trim schedule. You're not on it."

"Put me on it."

Piq put her on it. She logged the accordion as *distributed rotational trim, musical*, at the instrument rate of nine marks per kilogram, under Schedule Nine, paragraph four. That was very nearly the same thing she had done for herself at intake, and she was aware of it. The woman insisted on playing her something, as either a thank-you or a threat. It was a Latvian folk tune, mournful and slow. In a 6 m³ room at 0.126 g it was very loud.

By the seventh household Piq had found three more decorative ballast bricks. One held saffron, one held tea, and one held, inexplicably, more ballast. She found a set of cast-iron cookware listed on a manifest as *heat sink*. A Martian-made bicycle had been declared at its dismantled frame weight, minus the wheels, which the owner claimed were *circular art*. She found nothing that came anywhere near three hundred and forty tonnes. Nobody in Ring B was eating a depot one ballast brick at a time.

It was the ducts that caught her.

The residential spot-check schedule included a volumetric sweep of common spaces: corridors, ducts, crawlways. It was meant to catch exactly what she was actually hunting, ice quietly bled off the depot and cached somewhere it could be moved later. Piq ran the densitometer along the duct runs of Ring B's eastern quadrant with real interest. She found condensation, a lot of it, beading on the inside of the ducts and slicking the access plates. She noted that too, because she noted everything.

At Junction 14, where a main air return dropped into the ring's lower plenum, the densitometer chirped and showed her a number.

*4.1 kg. Hydrated. Mean density ~1.0. Warm.*

She looked at it for a long time.

Hydrated, near the density of water. Ice would read cold, unless it had been bagged and insulated, unless someone was keeping a small cache inside the warm air return so it wouldn't frost the duct and give itself away. It was four kilograms, a sample, a test run. She thought of the flat line in the ledger and the restatements under the Director's office code, and her pulse picked up.

She signed out an access tool from the ring maintenance locker. She logged it, because she always logged it.

With the tool in her hand she felt the small double tick of her badge against her collarbone, the mesh handshake it made every ninety seconds, and she stopped. The badge was a dosimeter first and a locator second, and the locator was not shy. Somewhere in the station mesh a log was already building a tidy column of entries: *VARGA, P., Ring B east, Junction 14*, every ninety seconds for as long as she stayed there. Anyone with mesh access could read it, and Saar had mesh access the way he had a jacket. This time it didn't matter. A volumetric duct sweep was on the Director's own schedule, and the log would read like an auditor doing exactly what she'd been asked. But Lagrange had a phrase for a cover that only held because the paperwork happened to agree with you: *borrowed luck*. She filed the badge in the column of things that would matter later, then unbolted the plate at Junction 14 and wormed into the air return headfirst with a lamp in her teeth.

The duct was half a meter square and lined with dust. It smelled of warm metal, recycled breath and something faintly musky she couldn't place. She went in up to her hips and then her knees. In low g a body in a duct isn't heavy so much as unwieldy, all elbows and inertia. Every push carried her a little farther than she meant to go, and she had to brake with her palms against the walls.

Three meters in, where the return opened into a junction box warmed by a heat exchanger, the lamp found the cache.

It was curled in a nest of shredded insulation and what looked like at least two stolen thermal blankets. It was striped brown and black and orange like a tabby rug. It had one ear with a notch out of the tip, a white bib, and yellow eyes that regarded her from a distance of forty centimeters with the total calm of a creature that has never once been made to declare itself.

It was a cat.

Piq lay in the duct with the lamp in her teeth and did the arithmetic anyway, because she couldn't help it. Roughly four kilograms. Roughly sixty percent water. Mean density near one. Warm. The densitometer had been exactly right, and she had been exactly wrong. Her father would have laughed until he had to sit down. *You read the number. Then you look at the thing. In that order, Piq. Never only the first one.*

"You," she said around the lamp, "are not on any manifest."

The cat yawned at her. It showed a great many teeth.

There was no way this animal had come down legally. Every kilogram landing at Asgard was weighed. Pets were technically possible and catastrophically expensive: four kilograms at personal tariff was sixteen hundred marks just to land it, and then there were air, water and protein. It would have had to be smuggled in a case or a crate, or born here. The notch in its ear was old and healed. It had been in these ducts for years, living on warm air and whatever it could find, and nobody had ever entered it into anything.

She thought about logging it. She thought about what logging it would mean: a report to the Director's office, a question about why the Ibis auditor had been three meters inside Ring B's air return, and an answer in her own handwriting with Saar's smile reading it. Beside that answer would sit the mesh log, which would put her at Junction 14 for exactly as long as she was taking.

The cat had moved while she was thinking. It rose, unhurried, and stretched with its front legs out and its rear in the air. It took two steps toward her and sniffed delicately at her chest pocket. The protein bar she'd forgotten was in there was apricot-flavored and had cost her forty grams of her personal allowance.

"No," said Piq.

The cat hooked a claw into the pocket's flap, flipped it open with insulting skill, and took the bar in its teeth.

"*No,*" said Piq, and grabbed. In a duct, at 0.126 g, with the lamp in her teeth and her arms pinned at her sides by sheet metal, she achieved nothing. She slid half a meter backward, struck her head on the duct ceiling and lost the lamp.

The cat turned with the bar and gathered itself. It leapt.

It was the slowest, most graceful thing she had ever seen an animal do. The cat went up and over her, its body a long striped bow, through the lamp's tumbling beam. It rose and rose against the duct's dim upper corner. It seemed to hang there for a long second, ears flat, tail streaming, the apricot bar clamped in its jaw like a trophy. Then it came down in a soft unhurried curve past her shoulder and onto the far wall. It pushed off again without a sound and vanished down the plenum into the dark.

The lamp landed on her face.

Piq lay in the duct for a moment longer, breathing dust. Then she took out the tally pad and wrote, by feel: *Ring B, Junction 14 return, 4.1 kg, hydrated, warm. Biological. Feline. Undeclared. Owner: ?* She looked at it. Then she drew a careful bracket around it and wrote beside the bracket, in the shorthand she used for things she didn't yet know how to file: *query.*

The densitometer was still chirping softly on her wrist. It had begun to register her.

---

At 2200 by station convention she sat cross-legged on her fold-down bunk in her 6 m³ quarters, in thermal leggings and an undershirt. Her jumpsuit hung on the hook by the door with the badge still clipped to its collar. Jupiter was a hard banded disc behind the frosted light-pipe. The steel cylinder sat on the bunk beside her, its end cap off.

The first report to Pell went out in pieces. She encrypted it on the burst transceiver from the crypto pad, page fourteen, line two. She wrapped it in forty-six megabytes of genuine Ibis bulk: drift logs from the depot load cells, the *Paper Lantern*'s manifest, and the full residential spot-check, accordion and all. She queued it under her registered traffic class, *Ibis bulk, routine*, so that it would go out with the rest of the station's commercial traffic and look exactly like the rest of the station's commercial traffic. Lisk would never open it. Deimos would sort it with the other fat packets and send it when it had room.

*Late is quiet,* she thought, and was satisfied.

Inside the wrapper the report was short, because Pell liked short.

*SL-2214-0391. Arrived. Cover holding. Depot inventory short 340 t over six quarters, restated flat each quarter under Director's office code, no name. Not scale fault. Not yet traced. Sling, pads, depot crew under observation. JDA security (Saar) attentive; aware of my instrument attestation, which is a vulnerability. Europa reiterated as exclusion zone, unprompted. Frost on sunward depot face; sunshade suspected, cause unknown. Proceeding.*

She paused, thought about it, and added a line beneath. It was half a joke and half a genuine cover problem. Pell would answer it either way, because Pell answered everything.

*Cover query: residential spot-check found undeclared feline, 4.1 kg, in ventilation. Logging would draw attention to my duct access. Not logging is a false attestation if discovered. Advise.*

She sent it and watched the queue mark it *sent—routine*.

Then, because Lagrange had trained her to do it every night and because she was awake anyway, she plugged the wideband receiver into the cylinder's power cell, threaded its antenna lead up through the light-pipe housing, and ran a sweep.

The station mesh was a wall of noise on the bands she expected: badge pings every ninety seconds, including her own from the hook by the door; crawler telemetry; the Sling's coil controllers chattering to one another; the pads; the long thin hiss of the downlink carrier to Ganymede. She let the receiver walk upward through the spectrum. She did it the way her father had walked the trim tables, line by line, not hurrying, because the point of a sweep is that you don't skip.

Above the mesh, there were bands nobody used anymore. The station's frequency allocation table had them listed as retired. One was an old telemetry channel from the first construction phase. One was a disused beacon band from the original survey. One, labeled in the table with a dry little footnote, was the *dosimetry maintenance band*. Before the mesh upgrade, every personal dosimeter on Asgard had used it to send its calibration checks to the dose warden's office. It had been retired four years ago, when the badges went onto the mesh. It should have been silent.

It wasn't.

The receiver's display showed her a line of noise, flat as the depot ledger. In the middle of the line, faint, almost below the floor, there was a pulse.

She waited and watched it come again. And again: short, long, short, short, a pause, then short again. It was not regular in the way an automated beacon is regular. It had a slight hesitation between some groups, and the spacing varied in a way her ear wanted to call deliberate. It was very faint, at the edge of what the receiver could find, as though whatever sent it was either far away or very weak, or both.

Someone was keying on a band no one should be using.

Piq sat very still. She looked at the badge on her jumpsuit collar across the room, telling the station mesh every ninety seconds that Piq Varga was in her quarters, as far as anyone could prove, asleep. Tonight that was true enough. But tomorrow she meant to follow somebody, and the badge would follow her, a ping at every junction, and Saar would only have to read the column. She could leave it on the hook. Then the mesh would put her safely in bed while she was elsewhere, which was a good alibi right up until someone knocked. It would also log zero dose for every hour it hung there, and the dose warden, who had already refused her one waiver, did not seem like a woman who missed a zero. She could wrap it in something wet and dense to choke the signal, but a badge that went quiet in a corridor was louder than a badge that pinged. None of the options balanced. She wrote *BADGE* at the bottom of the question-mark column and ruled a line under it.

She was aware of the 340 tonnes, and of Saar's smile, and of Hesper's hand flat against a coil housing in the cold, listening for something only Hesper could hear.

She took out the tally pad and began a new sheet. She wrote the date, the station hour and the band. Under that she ruled a column for time and a column for duration. She made the first mark, then the second, then the third.

At the top of the page, above everything else, she wrote one word in her father's shorthand and underlined it twice.

*Who.*

Beside it, on the right margin where she kept the running count, she wrote the other number she would be counting from now on.

*Day 2 of 38.*
