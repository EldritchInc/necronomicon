# Chapter 11: Billable

At 0412 Piq was awake and doing what she always did when she couldn't sleep. She was counting.

She had nineteen blocks frozen on the hangar floor, twelve on the frame, and one in a lift dripping into a cup. She had seven outbound reports marked ACK PENDING. Amaya's drum count stood at fifty-three, falling by one a night. Her own recalculation put shutdown at day fifty-one, plus or minus three.

Then there were three hundred and seventy metres per second that the *Ninefold Grace* had not had fourteen months ago. Lucky's grumble in the hangar had been three hundred on every Europa job the Mule flew. The Grace had smaller tanks and a heavier frame, and the flight recorder put her figure at three-seventy. Both numbers came from the same box. It was the Clause 14(c) shielding tank, eighteen tonnes of water bolted around the crew compartment nineteen days before departure. The engines had to carry that mass the whole way, and they could never burn it.

And there was the tick on page four, in her own hand. *Day 2, Bay Three. Cl. 14(c) recited in full to tug crew. Objections noted. No exemption granted.* After it came a small, neat check mark. She had been pleased with that check mark.

That tick was where her mind kept snagging.

She lay in the dark of her quarters with the kit sealed beside her and the ledger open on her chest. Its last entry read *0600. Tell her.* with the equals sign after it. She had put the sign there deliberately. It meant she would stop looking for a different answer. She had been looking anyway for four hours.

The door chimed at 0438.

In 0.126 g she reached it in one push and caught the frame with two fingers. Prew stood outside, Director Rook's aide, in a pressed collar with a slate held to his chest. He wore the air of a man who reported to two offices and was afraid of disappointing both.

"Notary Varga. I'm so sorry. We need a seal."

"At four-thirty?"

"Four thirty-eight." He looked at the slate as though it might correct him. "It's interagency. The Correspondence Act. Anything formal that crosses jurisdiction gets a notarial timestamp, and you're..."

"The only notary within six hundred thousand kilometres," Piq said. "I know. Whose jurisdiction?"

"Ur's," said Prew, and he looked haunted.

---

The JDA security office sat at the junction of Rings A and B, behind a pressure door with a reinforced lip. The room was small and very clean. Regolith grit got into everything on Asgard, but it did not seem to get in here. Piq suspected Saar had a seal fitted that the rest of the station could not afford.

Marek Saar sat at the console in shirtsleeves, cuffs turned back twice, a stylus in his hand, with the patient face of a man doing something beneath him. Koskinen stood behind him holding a printout, his clipboard tucked under one arm. Saar's *no* on thermal expansion was still pinned to it.

"Auditor." Saar didn't look up. "Thank you for coming. We are at war."

Piq waited.

"With adjectives," Saar said. "Koskinen. The sequence, for the notary."

Koskinen cleared his throat. He was a careful young man, and he read carefully, as though every word might be weighed later.

"Twenty-three ten, day twenty-five. A communiqué from the Office of the Commissioner, Ur Station, Ganymede, addressed to"—he checked—"'the Honourable Dose Warden of Asgard Yard, with the highest consideration.'"

Piq kept her face still. "To the dose warden?"

"By name of office." Saar's stylus turned once between his fingers. "Not to the Director. Not to the Authority. To the woman who hands out badges."

"Text," said Piq.

Koskinen passed it to her. It was a single page of formal Ur protocol, the kind that read as though it had been composed by a committee of very old courtiers who were afraid of verbs:

*The Commissioner presents his compliments and, mindful of the shared radiological hazards of the Jovian environment, respectfully requests the Asgard dose authority's current operational protocols for surface activity on the trailing hemisphere of Europa, including chaos terrain, together with any guidance on the management of persons of unknown prior exposure. The Commissioner further proposes, under the Interagency Radiological Cooperation Framework of 2201, an arrangement of dose sharing between the respective medical offices, at the convenience of the Warden. The Commissioner renews the assurance of his highest consideration.*

She read it twice. The phrase *surface activity on the trailing hemisphere* sat on the page like a mass with no manifest. Ur's tanker had been carrying three kilometres per second it had no use for, and Ur had bought war-risk cover over the zone. Now it wanted Europa surface tables, and it wanted them from the one person on Callisto who would have the best ones.

*Persons of unknown prior exposure.* She nearly squared that line. Soldiers, she thought. A landing party with no dose history, rotated in from the cheap seats. She squared it.

"Then," said Koskinen, "at zero-one-ten, our response. JDA Notice 2214-SEC-112." He read with real feeling. "'The Authority notes with profound regret that correspondence addressed to Authority personnel must be routed through the Authority liaison, and that the Dose Warden is not authorised to enter into radiological arrangements with foreign offices. The Authority reminds the Commissioner that the Europa zone is subject to exclusive Authority claim, and that any landing by a non-Authority vessel within the zone constitutes an incursion under Article Eleven of the Compact Jovian Accord, which would oblige a Compact response.'"

"Seal on that one?" Piq asked.

"Director Rook's office sealed it under her own stamp," Prew said. "But then."

"Zero-two-oh-four," said Koskinen. "Ur's reply. 'The Commissioner receives with profound regret the Authority's profound regret, and expresses his own deepest regret that the Authority should have found occasion for regret.'"

Piq looked at Saar. Saar was looking at the wall.

"Protocol," Prew said in a small voice, "requires that we match or exceed the register of the preceding communication. It's in the 2198 annex. Otherwise it's construed as a diplomatic slight. Which they could log. And cite."

"So at zero-three-thirty," said Koskinen, "we sent 'the Authority's most sincere and abiding regret.'"

"Abiding," said Saar. "I signed abiding."

"And at zero-three-thirty-four," Koskinen went on, because there was no way to stop him, "they sent 'a regret beyond the Commissioner's power to adequately express.'"

"It's four seconds," Prew said to Piq, as though apologising for the solar system. "To Ganymede. They answer before we've finished the cosignature."

"Which brings us to now," said Saar, "and to you."

The draft sat on the console. Piq leaned over it. In 0.126 g, leaning was something you did slowly or you overshot. Prew had written: *The Authority's regret, which it holds to be without limit, unbounded by any measure, and*

The sentence ended there. The cursor blinked after *and*.

"We are not," said Saar, "going to unbounded."

"Protocol," said Prew.

"I know what protocol says. I am asking the notary." Saar finally turned the chair and looked at her, and his smile was the one from the second day, the one that audited her back. "Notary Varga. Is there a ceiling on register under the Correspondence Act?"

"Not in the Act," Piq said. "Ibis's style guide caps superlatives at three per instrument. Anything above that is held to be decorative and is not notarised."

Koskinen's pen stopped moving.

"Three," said Saar.

"You've used 'most', 'sincere' and 'abiding'," Piq said. "You're at the cap. If you go to 'unbounded' I can't seal it. It becomes an unsealed instrument, and an unsealed instrument has no standing for the Ur office to cite."

Saar looked at her for a long time. It was the first time she had seen him actually weigh something she'd said, as opposed to her.

"So we exceed," he said slowly, "by subtraction."

"You match the register," said Piq. "You state that the Authority's regret is fully expressed by its previous notice. Then you're compliant and you're done. They can't escalate past a completed instrument without opening a new one, and opening a new one needs a new subject."

"Write that," Saar told Prew.

Prew wrote that. Saar cosigned. Piq set the seal and noted the timestamp, 0452:17. She entered it in the notarial register and copied it to the margin of her ledger, because she always did.

The console chimed at 0452:26.

"Ur acknowledges receipt," Koskinen read, "and has 'nothing further to add to the record at this time.'"

Prew sagged against the wall as gently as a coat on a hook.

"Well," said Saar. "Remarkable. A whole night of courtesy undone by a style guide." He pushed his chair back. "Koskinen, file it. Prew, wake the Director at a civil hour and tell her we've held the line at abiding." He stood, which in his case took a long time, as though standing were also protocol. "Auditor. You're up early."

"You woke me."

"Prew woke you." He took his jacket from the back of the chair. "I've been up all night. So has your transmitter, some nights."

The room didn't change, but the air in it did, the way pressure changes before a seal lets go.

"My uploads go to Ibis on a schedule," Piq said. "Calibration data's large. The relay prefers it at night."

"The relay," said Saar, as though committing the word to memory. "Of course." He shrugged into his jacket. "The Warden will not answer the Commissioner. You'll make sure she knows that. You see her more than most."

"I see her when my badge ticks."

"Then your badge must tick a great deal." He gave her the smile again and went out. The pressure door cycled behind him with a soft, expensive hiss.

Koskinen looked at the closed door, then at Piq, then down at his clipboard. "He's not wrong about the uploads," he said quietly. "Somebody logs them. Bearing and duration. I don't read the bearings." He swallowed. "I just watch the floor."

"Thank you, Koskinen."

"I didn't say anything."

"I know," said Piq. "That's what I'm thanking you for."

---

It was 0521 when she reached Ring A. That left thirty-nine minutes.

She went to her quarters first and did not open the ledger. Then she opened it.

She had written the confession out the night before, in order, the way her father had taught her to read a manifest aloud at a weigh station. You gave the gross weight first, then the tare, then the contents, and the exceptions last, because the exceptions were what people remembered:

1. Employer: Lagrange Mutual. Division: Special Losses.
2. Handler: A. Pell, L4.
3. Assignment: the 'throw' fragment. A cabal, a weapon, or fraud.
4. Credential: Ibis. Genuine. Cover.
5. The kit. What it does.
6. Reports. Seven. What they say.
7. R7. 'Any means.'
8. Page 4.

She read it once and hated it. It was correct and orderly, and it was a list. She knew Teo would hear it as a list, because that was what it was. Then she tore the page out, and that was worse, because the ledger was sewn and the stub showed. She put the torn page in her breast pocket anyway. She could not bring herself to leave a confession behind as unlogged mass.

Before she closed the ledger she turned back to page four and looked at the tick for a while. It was small and confident, the mark of someone who believed the rule was the reason people came home.

At 0547 she was in the long ramp up to the dome, taking it in the slow loping stride the Asgard-born used, which she had nearly learned. The ramp's lights ran amber at night cycle. Grit had collected along the seam of the floor plates in a fine dark line. Someone had drawn a small cat in it with a fingertip and then, apparently out of conscience, drawn a little box around the cat with *4.1* written inside.

At the top the pressure door opened on her own badge. The dome was for anyone. It was the only free thing on the station.

---

The dome was a shallow blister of sintered regolith and borosilicate, half buried in the berm, its glass layered so thick that the outside reached her softened, as though through water. The benches were old shipping cradles bolted to the floor. The air was a degree colder than the ring, and it smelled faintly of ozone from the electrolysis plant two levels down, the clean edge that meant somewhere ice was being turned into breath.

Jupiter hung where Jupiter always hung on Callisto's leading hemisphere, low over the rim of the Valhalla basin, and it did not move. Up here a day lasted sixteen Earth days and the planet never rose or set. It simply sat on the horizon, enormous and patient, its bands drawn across a sky with no air in it. The light it threw was banded too. It lay across the frost on the basin floor in faint stripes of cream and rust, as though the ground had been ruled for a ledger and nobody had written in it yet.

A small bright point stood just off the planet's western limb.

Europa. It was a day and a bit from going behind, by Piq's clock. Gap six would open at about 0742 tomorrow, and the old man would swallow it for thirty hours. For now it was there, as near and small as a pin in a map, and somewhere on it four people were breathing air made from heated ice and watching a counter fall.

Teo was already there.

She sat on the farthest cradle with her back half to the door and her knees drawn up, her grey cable-insulation hair tie pulling her hair back hard, a flask of tea held in both hands. The cracked brass dosimeter hung face-in against her collarbone. She did not turn when the door opened. She lifted the flask an inch in acknowledgement, and Piq noted the gesture and could not get the notation to stop. *Arrived first. Brought tea. Brought two cups.*

There were two cups on the cradle beside her.

"You're on time," said Teo.

"I said I would be."

"You say a lot of things you mean." Teo poured the second cup without looking at it. The tea left the flask in a slow amber rope and took its time finding the bottom. "It's the other kind I've started wondering about."

Piq sat. The cradle was cold through her trousers. She took the cup because refusing it would have been its own statement, and she was trying to make only one statement this morning.

"Before you start," Teo said. "Ur wrote to me."

Piq's hand did not move on the cup. "I know."

"Of course you do." Teo slid a slate along the cradle between them. The Commissioner's page was on it, the same text, but with a different routing header: *MEDICAL / RADIOLOGICAL — FRAMEWORK 2201 — AUTO-COPY*. "The Framework copies radiological correspondence straight to the named medical office. It's a twelve-year-old routing rule. Saar's people forgot it exists, or they never read it. I had this at twenty-three twelve. I've been reading it all night."

"So have they," Piq said. "Saar's office. They spent the night trading regrets with Ganymede. I sealed the last one."

"I heard you were up." Teo touched the slate and the text enlarged under her finger, one line held under the light. *Persons of unknown prior exposure.* "Do you know what that phrase is?"

"Soldiers," Piq said. "A landing party with no logged history. Rotated crew."

"It's intake language." Teo's voice was level and professional, the voice she used across a desk. "That's what I write when someone comes in off a breached hull and I don't know what they've already taken. You don't call your own crew 'unknown prior exposure.' You know your own crew to the microsievert. That's the whole point of having a crew." She took her finger off the slate. "And nobody plans a surface walk in chaos terrain. Chaos is where you go when you have no choice. Either Ganymede means to put bodies on the worst ice on Europa and wants to know how long they'll last, or..."

She stopped. The *or* hung there, like Prew's sentence on the console.

"Or what?"

"I don't know." Teo picked up the flask again. "That's what bothers me. I like knowing. I'd like to answer him and find out."

Piq looked at the pin of light off Jupiter's limb. Then she said what she had been sent to say, because she had promised herself she would leave nothing out this morning, including the parts that made her look small.

"Saar says the Warden will not answer the Commissioner." She heard how flat it came out. "He told me to make sure you knew."

Teo turned her head and looked at her properly for the first time since the door had opened.

"He told you."

"He sent me. In a manner of speaking."

"So you came up here at five fifty-nine with Marek Saar's message in your mouth." It wasn't loud. "I brought two cups."

"That isn't why I asked you here."

"Then why did you?"

Piq set the cup down on the cradle. In this gravity it settled with a delay, a small soft knock a moment after she let go, and the tea shivered and went still. She reached into her breast pocket and took out the torn page. It had been folded twice, and the sewn edge was ragged where it had come away from the stub.

"I wrote it down," she said. "So I'd say it in order."

Teo looked at the page and said nothing, so Piq began.

"One. Employer: Lagrange Mutual. Division: Special Losses."

Teo went still. It was not the stillness of someone listening. It was the stillness Piq had seen in people at weigh stations when a number came up on the scale that they had known would come up and had hoped wouldn't. Her hands stayed on the flask. Her eyes stayed on Piq's face. Even the dosimeter at her collarbone seemed to stop catching the light.

"Two," Piq said. "Handler—"

"Stop."

Piq stopped.

"Don't read me a manifest." Teo's voice had gone very precise, each consonant set down separately like a sample in a tray. "I've watched you for twenty-six days. I know how you do it. Gross, tare, contents, exceptions. If you read me that page I'll hear every line as a weight, and I'll know you picked the order so the worst of it comes last, when I'm too tired to hit it properly. Put it away."

Piq folded the page and held it, because she did not know where else to put her hands. Then she put it back in her pocket.

"All right," she said. "Without the list."

She told it plainly, and it came out badly, the way true things usually did.

She worked for Lagrange. Not for Ibis, not really, though the Ibis credential was genuine and she had earned it in a dull week at a dull desk on Ceres. She was what the society called a loss adjuster and what anyone else would call a spy. Whoever insured the ships knew where the ships were, and Special Losses existed to know the rest. Her handler was a man named Pell at L4, fifty light-minutes away, and she had never heard him use an adjective. She had been sent because of a fragment of intercepted traffic, *throw before conjunction, no one will see it until it's done*, and because Lagrange feared a weapon on the Sling or a fraud on the depot. The 340 tonnes were real. The kit in her quarters was real. It was 1.3 kilograms of receiver, encryption and burst transmitter that she had declared at the weigh-in as audit reference masses, while Teo stood two metres away refusing her a dosimeter waiver.

"That receiver is how I heard Amaya," she said. "It was built to listen for the cabal. There wasn't a cabal. There was your sister."

Teo said nothing.

"I've sent seven reports since the night we decoded her. All seven say four alive. All seven say the drum. All seven are marked acknowledgement pending, and the replies I get back read as if I never sent them. The last order I had from Pell..." Here was the place on the list she had meant to reach last. She reached it in the middle instead, because Teo had taken the order away from her. "It said Ur had bought war-risk cover over the zone, and that I was to stop any unlicensed launch by any means."

"Any means," Teo said.

"Those were the words."

"Does that include the Mule?"

"It would. By the words."

"Does it include Hesper? Lucky? Me?"

"I haven't done anything with it," Piq said. "I haven't answered it. I've been writing back telling him he's wrong."

"That isn't what I asked."

Piq made herself hold Teo's eyes. "I don't know what I'd have done with it on day five. I know what I'm doing with it now."

Teo didn't answer that. Piq hadn't expected her to.

"There's one more thing," Piq said. "It was the last line on the page. Page four."

"Your ledger."

"My ledger." The banded light lay across both their hands. "On my second day here I stood in Bay Three in front of a tug crew who were complaining about water blankets, and I recited Clause 14(c) to them in full. Every subsection. Minimum shielding water on any Europa-bound vessel. One of them said the blankets cost her three hundred metres per second a run and she'd rather take the dose. I told her the clause was the reason crews came home. I told her it as gospel. I meant it as gospel." She breathed in, ozone and cold. "Then I went back to my quarters and wrote it down, *Clause recited, objections noted, no exemption granted*, and I put a tick next to it. I was pleased with that tick."

Teo's knuckles had gone pale on the flask.

"Eighteen tonnes," Piq said. "That's what it was on the Grace. A tank fitted nineteen days before departure, because a Lagrange inspector told them to. It cost them three hundred and seventy metres per second they didn't have. That forced them onto the slow path through the torus. The dose latched up the flight computer, and Lagrange wrote it up as pilot error. Some other adjuster in some other bay ticked that tank, the same way I ticked mine. I don't know who. It might as well have been me. It's the same hand."

The dome was quiet except for the circulation fans, a breath going in and out somewhere above the glass.

When Teo spoke again, she sounded almost gentle, and that was worse.

"You'll want this numbered," she said.

"Teo..."

"No. You started a list. I'll finish it for you." She set the flask down. It took its slow low-g second to land and made no sound at all. "One. Day one, intake. You asked me for a dosimeter waiver. I thought you were vain about a badge. You wanted it off because a badge logs where the wearer goes. Was that billable?"

Piq said nothing.

"Two. Day nine, my office, the consultation. Your reading had ticked up and you came in charming about it. You asked me what the Mule's reactor was putting out at the hangar door. I told you to the decimal, because you asked like a woman frightened for her own marrow. I gave you the shielding geometry. I drew you a diagram on the back of a requisition." Her mouth moved, not quite a smile. "You kept the diagram. I watched you fold it. Was that billable?"

Piq had promised herself she would leave nothing out. That promise did not stop at the parts she said first.

"Yes," she said. "It went into report two. The reactor class. The hangar."

Teo nodded once, as though a reading had come in exactly where she'd predicted. That was worse than if she had flinched.

"Three. Day ten. Here." She did not gesture at the dome. She didn't need to. "I told you Europa ducks behind the old man every four and a half days and the dosimeters sing differently when it does. I told you I had a sister. I've told four people on this station about Amaya in fourteen months. You were the fourth, and you were the only one who didn't say sorry. You asked what she did on the Grace. I liked that. I thought it meant you could stand to hear the answer." Her voice did not rise. Each word was set down separately. "Billable?"

"The occultation note went in," Piq said. "Amaya didn't."

"Why not?"

"Because it wasn't relevant to the file."

"So it was assessed." Teo turned the phrase over like a sample she meant to keep. "And found not relevant. Four. Day fourteen. My office, after hours. I showed you her last dose log. I had never shown it to anyone outside the board of inquiry, and the board looked at it for nine minutes. You looked at the spike for forty. Your hand was on the desk and mine was on the desk, and neither of us moved." She held Piq's eyes. "You knew the Grace's claim had been denied before you ever landed. It's in your society's files. You sat across from me and let me tell you how Lagrange had called my sister's pilot a fool. Billable?"

Piq could have said that she hadn't read the claim file until day twenty. It was true. It was also an exception, and Teo had told her what exceptions were for.

"I knew the claim was denied," she said. "I didn't know why. I let you tell me."

"Five. Day nineteen, two in the morning. We sat on your floor with the receiver between us and I read my own keying back to myself in my sister's hand. FOUR ALIVE. You put your jacket round my shoulders. I've been trying all night to work out whether you did that because I was shaking or because I was the only person on Callisto who could read the code." Her voice caught for the first time, a small hitch, like a valve seating, and then it was level again. "Which was it?"

"I don't know," Piq said, and that was the most honest thing she had ever said aloud. "I'd like to tell you the first. I don't trust myself enough to swear to it."

"Six. Day twenty-two. The tank farm. We wrestled a valve for twenty minutes in a vacuum suit with a bladder trying to kill us both, and you laughed. I'd never heard you laugh. You logged that run as audit fieldwork. I saw the form."

"It was audit fieldwork."

"I know. That's the point." Teo picked up her cup at last, looked into it and put it down again untouched. "Seven. Day twenty-four. The airlock."

The fans breathed overhead. Out past the glass the frost lay ruled in cream and rust and nothing moved on it.

"Ballast came out of the duct," Teo said, "and we both laughed and stepped back, and I went home and lay awake and thought, she was going to, and so was I, and the cat saved us both from something stupid. That's what I thought. That it would have been stupid." She looked out at Europa. "Was that billable, Piq?"

"No."

"How would I know?"

Piq had no answer to that, because there wasn't one. That was the whole shape of it. She had built every conversation on this station to bear a load nobody else could see, and now she had nothing to point to that would prove any one of them had borne nothing.

"Eight," Teo said quietly. "Exceptions last. You see? I can do it too." She stood. In this gravity it was a slow, deliberate unfolding, and she did it without touching the cradle. "Eight. This morning. You came up here with a list in your pocket and Saar's message in your mouth, and you told me the truth because you'd decided to, and you put an equals sign after it in that book so you couldn't change your mind. I know you did, because that's exactly what I'd have done." She clipped the flask to her belt. "That one I can't price. I've tried. I'll keep trying."

"Teo."

"I'm answering the Commissioner." She picked up the slate. "Dose tables for chaos terrain, the management of persons of unknown prior exposure, and an open line under the 2201 Framework. Saar can file a regret about it. He's got one left before he reaches the cap." She turned toward the door, then stopped with her back to Piq and Jupiter's light banded across her shoulders. "Your uploads go out at night. He logs the bearings. Koskinen told me that too, two days ago. He tells everyone eventually. If Saar asks me what you are, I'll tell him you're an auditor, and that's true, and it's all he'll get."

"Why?" Piq asked. It came out barely a sound.

Teo didn't turn around. The pressure door opened for her badge, because the dome was the only free thing on the station.

"I won't burn you," she said. "I'm not you."
