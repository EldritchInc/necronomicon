# Chapter 5: The Dome

Saar walked her home.

He did it courteously, a pace behind and a little to the left, the way a man might escort a guest who had wandered into the wrong wing of a house. They climbed from L6 to Ring C in a silence measured by the slap of their soles on the gritted treads. At 0.126 g every stair took a half-second of float, so the climb had the rhythm of a slow, insincere waltz. At her door he waited while she palmed the lock. The seal rasped on its grit, as it always did.

"Your badge flagged," he said. "That isn't my department. I'll ask the warden for your number anyway, and she'll tell me no. She always tells me no." He seemed pleased about it. "Sleep, Auditor Varga. The station's quite safe. I've looked."

The door closed on his smile. Piq stood in her six cubic meters with her back to it and counted to a hundred and twenty before she let herself sit. Then she wrote on her tally pad in her father's shorthand:

*0304. S. waited at first live node. Read addendum. Knew time.*

Under it, smaller:

*Badge = beacon. 90 s. Forgot.*

That was the real failure, and it was worse than being seen. She had planned the night around the dead node at L6 and the thirty meters of corridor beyond it where the mesh went blind. The badge on her collar had pinged faithfully every ninety seconds right up to the edge of that blindness, and then it had stopped. Anybody watching would have seen the stop. The badge had picked up again when she walked back into coverage, and its next ping had uploaded fourteen ticks of dose all at once, like a drunk confessing.

Saar had not needed to follow her. He had only needed to read where she vanished and wait where she would reappear.

She lay down on the bunk in her coveralls. Above her the light-pipe held Jupiter in a coin of pale ochre, unmoving, as it had been since she arrived and would be until she left. A sliver of something green was stuck in the seam of her sleeve. She picked it out, weighed it on her fingertip by habit (under a gram, call it 0.3), and could not think what to do with it, so she ate it.

---

The summons came at 0611, as a line of text across her badge's little display:

**REPORT DOSE OFFICE 0800. BRING YOUR LOG. — T. ANAND**

No *please*, and no reason given. Piq approved of it professionally and resented it personally, and she spent the hour and forty-nine minutes until eight building her log.

It was a good log. She had kept it honestly through Day 11 and only lightly fictionalized Day 12, and the fictions were the kind an auditor could defend. She had been surveying the D-line branch, which was true. She had traced it to L6, also true, and the meter at D-7 really had drifted. She had followed the conduit beyond the mesh to check routing against the as-builts. That was nearly true, if a hangar counted as routing.

She would have to account for forty-six minutes and roughly eighty microsieverts. She assembled the minutes first, because minutes were easier. She gave herself eleven for conduit inspection, fourteen for photographing junction labels, nine for a pump-room door that was genuinely stiff, and twelve for retracing her route after losing her bearings in an undocumented space. Ninety seconds per ping meant thirty-one pings missed. She checked the arithmetic twice.

At 0758 the blue door slid back on its borated-poly smell and its faint ozone, and Teodora Anand looked up from three screens and did not stand.

"Sit," she said. "Hand me the log."

Piq sat. The chair was hard, and set at the exact height where the desk edge caught her forearms. She handed over the tablet. Teo's hair was tied back with the same length of cable insulation, grey this time and fraying at the cut end. Her sleeves were rolled to the elbow. The old brass dosimeter hung from its cord against her shirt with its cracked face turned inward, like a locket somebody had stopped opening.

Teo read the log. She took her time, and her mouth did nothing at all.

"Eleven minutes of conduit inspection," she said.

"The branch is unmapped. I had to verify the run."

"At L6 the conduit is buried in regolith fill, under four point two meters of overhead in that stretch. The fill reads point four microsieverts an hour. I know because I surveyed it after the floor slumped in 2209 and I had to sign the reoccupancy." Teo did not look up. "Eleven minutes at point four is seventy-three nanosieverts. Go on."

"Fourteen minutes photographing junction labels."

"Same overhead, same fill. Ninety-three nano." She scrolled. "A stiff door for nine minutes. I'll grant you the door. Doors here are very stiff. They don't emit."

"I lost my bearings," Piq said. "The as-builts are thirty years old and wrong about—"

"Twelve minutes of being lost." Teo put the tablet down flat on the desk and finally looked at her. Her eyes were as dark and steady as Piq remembered. Being looked at by them felt like being placed on a scale and left there. "Forty-six minutes in L6 fill comes to three hundred and seven nanosieverts. Point three one micro, if you like round numbers, and I don't. Your badge is reporting eighty-one point six. You're out by a factor of two hundred and sixty-six. Auditor Varga, let me tell you what I think you're doing, and you can correct my rounding."

"I'd be glad to."

"You've given me a sum of minutes that equals the minutes. Forty-six exactly. That's the part that's right, and you're presenting it as if the dose will follow from it." She turned one screen around. A histogram filled it, a sawtooth of narrow peaks across a band of energies. "Your badge doesn't just count. It bins. It keeps a spectrum, a very poor one, but I can read it. This is Ring C." She tapped a smooth low hummock. "Callisto regolith, a little cosmic, a little Jupiter leaking in through the overhead. This is the Sling tour with Hesper, which had the frost face." She tapped a second trace, the same hummock with a shoulder on it. "And this is last night."

The third trace was not a hummock. It was a broad continuum that climbed the energy axis much higher than the others had any reason to go, and near its upper end, alone, a single needle stood up out of the noise.

"Two point two two three MeV," Teo said. "Do you know what that is?"

Piq did. Her father had kept a chart of capture lines taped inside the weigh-master's locker on the *Perihelion Ladder*, because the cycler's reactor shield had been water and he had liked to know what the water was doing. She said, "No."

"No," Teo repeated, as if entering it in a column for later reconciliation. "Then I'll tell you, and you can be surprised. A free neutron wanders into hydrogen, slows down, and gets caught. The hydrogen becomes deuterium and throws off the difference as a gamma, always the same gamma. Two point two two three. It's the cleanest line in nature. You can't fake it with rock, and you can't fake it with cosmic background, not at that height. Neutrons have to come from somewhere, and the hydrogen has to be standing close enough to catch them." She let the needle sit between them. "Water, say, packed around something that makes neutrons."

The ozone in the room seemed to sharpen. Piq kept her hands still on the desk edge, where the chair had put them.

"And the continuum," Teo went on, in the same tone, "is what you get when you're near the thing that makes them. Prompt gammas, activation products, the whole ugly shelf. On this station there's one licensed fission source with that profile. It's in the depot pumphouse behind two meters of borated wall. Your badge did not go to the pumphouse." She leaned back. "So my rounding says you spent some meaningful fraction of forty-six minutes within a few tens of meters of an operating or recently operated reactor that is wrapped in ice and isn't on my register. Correct me."

Piq could have said a great many things. She could have said *junction labels* again, with feeling. She could have said *calibration fault*, and watched Teo take that apart in under a minute. She sorted through them as she would sort a cargo manifest for the item that would bear inspection, and found none.

"I'd want to see the badge's calibration history," she said, "before I accepted a single needle as dispositive."

Something happened at the corner of Teo's mouth. It was not a smile. It was the place a smile would have been if she had been a different person, or this a different conversation.

"That's the most honest thing you've said since you sat down."

The desk chimed. A text banner slid across the top of the leftmost screen, and because the screen was still turned half toward Piq she could read it:

**JDA SECURITY / SAAR, M. — REQUEST: CURRENT CUMULATIVE + 24H INCREMENT, RESIDENT VARGA, P. (TEMP). PURPOSE: SITE SAFETY.**

Teo looked at it for about as long as it took to read twice. Then she pulled the keyboard toward her and typed with two fingers, unhurried:

**DOSE RECORDS ARE MEDICAL UNDER JDA HEALTH STATUTE 4.11. NO SAFETY CONDITION EXISTS. REQUEST DECLINED. — T. ANAND, WARDEN.**

She sent it. The banner folded itself away.

"He said you'd tell him no," Piq said, before she could weigh whether to.

"He asks every time someone goes somewhere he can't see. I always tell him no. It's the only regular conversation we have." Teo's eyes went back to the histogram. "It wasn't for you."

Piq waited for the rest of the sentence, the part that said who it was for. It didn't come. Teo cleared Saar's banner from her history with a flick and did not open a report form. There was a report form. Piq had seen its tab on the second screen: *NONCONFORMING SOURCE — NOTIFICATION*, with a red field for the reporter's name. Any warden who found an unregistered neutron source on her station was obliged by about four statutes to fill that form in the same hour. The hour was passing. Teo's cursor stayed in the spectrum view.

Piq tried to tally it and couldn't. On one side was a woman who believed rules were the only thing that had never lied to her, a woman who had refused Piq a dosimeter waiver on Day 1 with a contempt so pure it was almost restful. On the other side was a reactor in the ice, a form unopened, and a security chief told no. The columns didn't foot. There was a missing entry somewhere, and Piq could not find even its line number.

"You're not going to file it," she said.

"I'm going to file your dose." Teo touched a key. "Eighty-one point six microsieverts, source unattributed, investigation open. Investigation will remain open until I'm satisfied." She looked up. "I'm rarely satisfied."

"That isn't the same thing."

"No. It isn't." Teo held her gaze a moment longer than the sentence required, and whatever she was weighing, she did not share the scale. "You also smell of the hydroponics gallery. There's a lettuce rack in Gallery Four that came down off its rail yesterday, and the gardener filed a complaint about an *airborne auditor*. Is that also in your log?"

"It's in my log as an unplanned contact with produce."

"Mass of produce?"

"Roughly two hundred grams displaced," Piq said. "Point three consumed."

Teo shut her eyes briefly, the way people did when they decided not to laugh, and that was worse. It was much worse, because it meant there had been a laugh to decide against.

"Your badge," Teo said, opening her eyes, "is a cheap Ibis unit with a crystal that drifts warm. If I'm going to hold an open investigation on it, I want it calibrated against a reference I trust. Take it off."

Piq unclipped it from her collar and held it out. Teo didn't take it. She stood, picked a hard case off the shelf behind her, and put on her jacket.

"Not here," she said. "My check sources are in here. The background in this office is contaminated by my own good intentions. Bring it."

There were three techs on the dose staff, and any one of them could have walked an Ibis badge to a jig. Piq noted this. Teo, sealing her case, gave no sign of having noticed anything. Neither of them said it aloud, and the omission went into the ledger unbalanced alongside the rest.

---

The viewing dome sat at the top of the oldest shaft in Asgard, a blister of laminated glass and polymer under a hood of regolith that could be cranked back on a winch. It was the only place on the station where a person could stand under sky with nothing between her and Jupiter but a few centimeters of window and a polite fraction of a millisievert a day.

Teo cranked the hood. It ground back along its track, shedding grit that fell upward-and-then-down in slow, lazy arcs and pattered on the glass like the beginning of a rain that would never commit. Light came in.

Piq had seen Jupiter every day since arrival through the light-pipe in her room, reduced to a coin. Here it was the size of a held-out hand, low over the crater rim and fixed there, because Callisto had stopped turning its face away a billion years ago and would never start again. The bands were bigger than any photograph had prepared her for: cream and rust, tan and a grey that was nearly blue, ruled across the disc with a draftsman's patience. The edge of the dome's outer pane was furred with frost. The banded light lay across the frost in stripes, ochre on one crystal and white on the next, as though the old man were pressing his own ledger into the ice.

"It doesn't move," Piq said.

"It moves. Very slowly. You'll see it nod about seven degrees over a libration cycle if you stand here long enough." Teo set her case on the bench. "Nobody stands here long enough."

The calibration jig was a squat aluminum fixture bolted to the bench, with a lead-lined well and a clamp. Teo opened her case. Inside, nested in foam, lay three small sealed discs, each labeled in her square hand. She lifted the cesium source with tongs, dropped it into the well, and held out her other hand for the badge.

Piq gave it to her. Their fingers didn't touch. Piq noted the distance anyway: about a centimeter, though it was a centimeter that had cost something to keep.

"Six-six-two keV," Teo said, clamping the badge over the well. "If your crystal is honest, the line lands in bin forty-one. If it's warm, it'll walk." She watched the display. "It's walking. Bin forty-three. You're reading two percent hot."

"So the needle last night—"

"Was still two point two. Two percent of two point two two three is forty-four keV. Your needle is in the right place for a hot crystal." She looked up without lifting her head. "You were hoping I'd say otherwise."

"I was hoping you'd say it with less satisfaction."

"I'm a scientist. It's all the satisfaction I'm allowed." She keyed a correction into the badge through her own tablet, and the display flickered and settled. "Now you're honest. Clip it on."

Piq lifted it toward her collar and fumbled, because the clip was a stiff cheap thing and she was watching the frost. The badge slid on her knuckles and dropped. At 0.126 g it took its time, falling in a slow tilt that gave everyone present ample opportunity to catch it.

Teo caught it. Then, instead of handing it back, she stepped in and clipped it to Piq's collar herself. Her knuckles brushed the line of Piq's jaw as she seated it. Her breath smelled faintly of the station's bitter tea. The procedure took four seconds, and Piq would later be able to reconstruct each of them and assign none of them a value.

"Collar, left side, face out," Teo said, stepping back. "The way the form says. You've been wearing it face in."

"I haven't."

"Your Ring C spectrum is attenuated by about a centimeter of soft tissue. It's been face in for at least four days." She closed the case. "Your log is a work of fiction and your badge is upside down. You'd make a terrible auditor."

"I'm told I'm an excellent one."

"Then Asgard is in a great deal of trouble."

The cesium source went back into its foam. Teo didn't crank the hood closed. She sat down instead on the far end of the bench, under the banded light, and after a moment Piq sat at the near end. Between them lay the case and about a meter and a half of scuffed polymer, which was a measurable distance and, Piq judged, a deliberate one.

The badge clicked softly on Piq's collar. It had an audio mode for field work, a quiet tick per detected event, and Teo hadn't switched it off. Up here, under thin cover, it ticked more than it did in Ring C: a sparse, unhurried rain, two or three clicks to a breath.

"Listen to that," Teo said. "Jupiter's background. Most of what you're hearing is the old man's belts leaking out this far. Callisto sits outside the worst of it, so it's barely a drizzle." She tilted her head. "The old wardens said you could tell the time by it."

"By the click rate?"

"By the song." Teo's voice changed slightly, as if she were quoting someone she'd been fond of. "*Europa ducks behind the old man every four and a half days, and the dosimeters sing differently when it does.* Haralsen used to say that. He was warden before me. He'd sit up here with an open counter and a cup of something illegal and swear he could hear it."

"Could he?"

"He was drunk most nights after 2200, so it's hard to audit." She shrugged one shoulder. "It's folklore. The physics says Europa's torus is nowhere near us and shouldn't make any difference to a counter on Callisto. But I've logged six years of night readings, and there's a little dip in the high-energy bins that repeats. It's not much. It's in the noise, mostly. It's there if you want it."

"Every four and a half days."

"Europa's synodic period from here. Four point five. Everything on Asgard keeps that clock. Sling windows, depot shifts, half the station's sleep. You'll start keeping it too, if you stay."

Piq turned the number over. Four point five days. The silences she'd been logging on her covert receiver came about thirty hours in every four and a half days, steady as a shift change. She had read them as a roster on Day 9, and she read them as a roster now, with a click of satisfaction that felt like a column footing: of course the cabal kept Europa's clock. The depot shifts ran on it. Everyone here ran on it. Whoever was keying those pulses worked the same rota as the Sling, went off-watch when the windows closed, and came back when they opened. It was precisely what she would expect of people who weren't careful.

She filed it, tidy, and didn't ask herself why it felt like the old man's light on the frost: one band striped across two different crystals.

"My sister used to sit up here," Teo said.

Piq kept her face as it was. It took some effort, and she was good at it.

"Amaya. She's five years younger than me. She learned to count clicks on this bench when she was twelve, on Haralsen's counter, with his illegal cup confiscated. She was better at it than me. She could hear the difference between a cosmic and a belt electron by ear, or she said she could. I could never prove she was lying." Teo was looking at Jupiter, not at Piq. "She went on to be an engineer. Tugs. She was on the *Ninefold Grace*."

The name went through Piq like a dropped tool, slow and certain.

"You'll know it," Teo said. "You're from that side of the business. A Europa tug, lost with all hands fourteen months ago. Lagrange Mutual underwrote the hull and the crew's lives. They denied the claim." Her voice didn't rise; it got flatter, as if it were being rolled. "Pilot error. The pilot *deviated to a low-energy trajectory*. That's the phrase. I've read the denial forty times. I could recite it to you. They didn't come out, they didn't send anyone, and they decided from forty-five light-minutes away that four people died of a bad choice, which meant nobody owed anyone anything. Then they raised our rates."

Piq's hands lay on her knees. She checked that they were still, and they were. She checked her breathing: even. She checked her face, which she could not see, against the memory of what it was supposed to look like, and judged it acceptable. Under all of it, in a place she did not normally keep accounts, something had gone cold and tight around the word *Lagrange*, and she let it stay there unexamined, because she had no column for it.

"I'm sorry," she said, which was true and which was the smallest true thing available.

"Don't be. You're an auditor." Teo glanced at her, quick and hard. "Not that you're so different. You lot write the rules, and they write the checks, and the checks bounce off the rules. Same species of vulture. I told you that on Day One."

"You did."

"I meant it."

"I know."

They sat in the ticking for a while. The banded light had moved, or seemed to have; a stripe of rust now lay across the frost where cream had been. Piq couldn't tell whether that was Jupiter's slow nod or simply that she was looking at the frost from a slightly different angle, because at some point she had turned on the bench to face Teo more than the window.

"Why did you bring me up here?" she asked. "One of your techs could have run the jig."

Teo considered the question the way she'd considered the log: thoroughly, without visible reaction.

"The background's stable," she said. "Good reference."

"That's a reason."

"It's the reason I'm giving." Teo stood and picked up her case. "Wear your badge face out. Stay out of L6. If your number goes up again by a factor of two hundred and sixty-six, I'll ground you, and I won't tell Saar why, and I'll enjoy both."

She went to the winch and cranked the hood. Regolith ground along the track, and the banded light narrowed over the frost to a strip, then to a line, and then went out. The ticking on Piq's collar slowed almost at once to the Ring C drizzle, as if something had stopped singing.

At the hatch Teo paused without turning.

"Amaya would have liked you," she said. "She liked people who were wrong in interesting ways."

Then she was gone down the shaft in a long, unhurried low-g drop, and Piq was alone under the closed hood, holding a tally that would not close. It had too many entries on one side and nothing she could name on the other. Her father would have said that when the ledger wouldn't foot, you hadn't finished counting. Her father had never sat under that light.

---

Back in her six cubic meters, with the door sealed and a towel stuffed in the vent grille out of a superstition she couldn't justify, Piq took the comms unit out of its housing.

The unit was the hollow one she had declared at the weigh-in as *audit reference masses*, 1.3 kilograms of Lagrange engineering pretending to be lumps of calibrated steel. Opened, it unfolded into a receiver no bigger than her palm, a burst transmitter, and a crypto core that would turn to slag if anyone pried at it wrong. She ran the receiver's lead up the light-pipe, where it could see a little of the sky through the station's surface optics, and tuned to the retired dosimetry maintenance band.

The pulses were there. They were faint, patient and regular: short groups of keyed carrier, a pause, another group. She had stopped trying to read them on Day 9; whatever code they used wasn't one Lagrange had given her. She logged timing and carrier, and she let the receiver's little waterfall display paint them down the screen in green dashes.

Tonight, because she'd just had her own badge proven two percent hot, she checked the carrier frequency with the same suspicion.

It wasn't where she'd left it.

She pulled the log. On Day 9 she'd written the center frequency to six figures. On Day 10 it was a hair higher, and Day 11 higher again. Tonight it was lower than Day 9. She plotted it, center against time, and the points didn't scatter as noise would. They traced a curve: a slow, smooth rise and fall, as if the transmitter's whole voice were breathing over a period of days.

Piq looked at it for a while.

Then she did what any auditor did with an anomaly too tidy to be fraud: she looked for the cheapest explanation. Crystal oscillators drifted with temperature. Her own badge had drifted two percent warm, and that was a factory-calibrated Ibis unit, not some backroom transmitter built out of salvage by people who stole ice. A cheap oscillator in an unheated space would wander with the station's thermal cycle, and on Callisto the thermal cycle followed the local day, sixteen point seven Earth days of slow light and slow dark. Or it followed the depot's duty cycle, pumps running and resting, on that same four-and-a-half-day clock that everything here obeyed. A careless cabal would bolt a cheap rig to a warm pipe and never think to compensate. The drift was, if anything, a signature. These were people who could steal three hundred and forty tonnes of ice and couldn't be bothered to buy a temperature-compensated crystal.

She wrote it down:

*Carrier drift, slow periodic. Thermal? Cheap osc., uncompensated. Pumps or local day. Careless.*

She underlined *careless*, because it pleased her, and didn't notice that the period of the curve fit neither the local day nor the pump cycle as neatly as she'd written.

The crypto core chirped.

It was a quiet sound, designed to be mistaken for a badge alarm, and it meant a packet had come down the Lagrange channel and passed authentication. She set the receiver aside and opened it. The header read **ORIG L4 / PELL A. / SENT 0941 UTC / RECV 1030 LOCAL**. Forty-nine minutes. It had been written before she ever sat in Teo's hard chair, by a man answering a version of her that was already gone.

The body was three lines. Pell never used four where three would hold.

**ACKNOWLEDGE THROW FRAGMENT CORROBORATED. PRIORITY: IDENTIFY THROW CABAL, PERSONNEL AND LAUNCH ASSET. TREAT DEPOT CREW AS HOSTILE UNTIL CLEARED. PELL.**

Piq read it twice, the way Teo had read Saar's request: once for what it said, and once for what it would cost.

*Depot crew* meant Hesper, who had shown her the Sling with the pride of a woman showing off a child. It meant the gardener in Gallery Four, and Lucky, whose wedding she was apparently going to officiate. It meant every one of the faces she'd seen in the hangar last night, wrapping ice blocks around a tug with the care of people dressing a wound. Forty-nine minutes ago, in Pell's ledger, they had all become a single entry, and the entry was red.

She thought, without wanting to, of a centimeter of air between two fingers over a calibration jig. She thought of a report form left unopened on a screen.

The receiver on her bunk kept painting green dashes down its little screen, patient and regular, the carrier breathing up and down on its slow, careless curve. Somewhere, someone she had just been ordered to treat as an enemy was keying them.

Piq set her stylus against a fresh line of the tally pad, and for the first time in her working life she didn't know which column to write in.
